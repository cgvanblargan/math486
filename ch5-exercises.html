    Exercises 5.7 Exercises

    Many of these exercises use the Fundamental Theorem of Nash Equilibria (Theorem 5.2). As a reminder, a mixed strategy profile \(\sigma\) is a Nash equilibrium exactly when, for every player \(i\text{:}\)
    🔗
    1. (the equality condition) If \(s_i\) and \(s_i'\) are both active, then \(\pi_i(s_i, \sigma_{-i}) = \pi_i(s_i', \sigma_{-i})\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. (the inequality condition) If \(s_i\) is active and \(s_i'\) is not, then \(\pi_i(s_i, \sigma_{-i}) \geq \pi_i(s_i', \sigma_{-i})\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    A few parts have a Solution dropdown with the final answer. Use it to check your answer before moving on, since the later parts build on it. But make sure to try the part on your own first!
    🔗
    Throughout, Player 1 is the row player and Player 2 is the column player, and payoffs are listed in the order (Player 1, Player 2).
    🔗

    1. Computing a Mixed Equilibrium.

    Consider the following game.
    🔗

    (a)

    Use the Fundamental Theorem of Nash Equilibria to find a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium in which both players use both of their strategies. Write Player 1’s mixed strategy as \(\sigma_1 = pa + (1-p)b\) and Player 2’s as \(\sigma_2 = qx + (1-q)y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\left(\frac{1}{5}a + \frac{4}{5}b,\ \frac{3}{4}x + \frac{1}{4}y\right)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    What is each player’s expected payoff at the mixed strategy Nash equilibrium you found in part (a)?
    🔗
    Hint.
    By Theorem 5.3, a player’s equilibrium payoff equals what any one of their active strategies earns against the other player’s mixed strategy. So you can compute, for example, \(\pi_1(a, \sigma_2)\) instead of adding up all four cells.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark. This game also has two pure strategy Nash equilibria: \((a, x)\text{,}\) with payoffs \((2, 4)\text{,}\) and \((b, y)\text{,}\) with payoffs \((3, 7)\text{.}\) (You can check these with best response.) Comparing these with your answer to part (b), we see that both players prefer \((b, y)\) to the mixed strategy equilibrium. So a game can have pure and mixed strategy Nash equilibria at the same time, and the mixed one isn’t necessarily better for anyone.
    🔗
    🔗

    2. A Symmetric \(3 \times 3\) Game.

    Consider the following two-player game.
    🔗
    This game is symmetric: if the players swap strategies, they also swap payoffs. So you only need to do the computation for one player, and you get a symmetric Nash equilibrium \((\sigma, \sigma)\text{,}\) where both players use the same mixed strategy \(\sigma = xa + yb + zc\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Use the Fundamental Theorem of Nash Equilibria to set up a system of linear equations whose solution gives a symmetric Nash equilibrium \((\sigma, \sigma)\) in which all three strategies are active. Explain where each equation comes from.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} 2x + 6y + 5z = 3x + 3y + 9z \amp = 4x + 4y + 7z\\ x + y + z \amp = 1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Solve your system and state the Nash equilibrium \((\sigma, \sigma)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    A symmetric game can also have Nash equilibria that are not symmetric. You have already seen this: Hawk-Dove (Exercise 3.5.5) is symmetric, and its Nash equilibria \((h, d)\) and \((d, h)\) are not symmetric.
    🔗

    3. Active and Inactive Strategies.

    Consider the game below, where Player 1 has three strategies and Player 2 has two.
    🔗
    Throughout, write Player 2’s mixed strategy as \(\sigma_2 = qx + (1-q)y\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Use the equality condition of the Fundamental Theorem to show that there is no mixed strategy Nash equilibrium in which all three of Player 1’s strategies are active.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium \((\sigma_1, \sigma_2)\) in which \(a\) and \(b\) are active in \(\sigma_1\text{,}\) \(c\) is not active in \(\sigma_1\text{,}\) and \(x\) and \(y\) are both active in \(\sigma_2\text{.}\) Write \(\sigma_1 = pa + (1-p)b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    You must check whether both conditions of Theorem 5.2 can be satisfied:
    🔗
    1. The equality condition on \(a\) and \(b\) determines the value of \(q\text{.}\) The equality condition on \(x\) and \(y\) determines the value of \(p\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Check the inequality condition for every inactive strategy. Notice that \(c\) (for Player 1) is the only inactive strategy.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Show that there is no mixed strategy Nash equilibrium in which \(a\) and \(c\) are active in \(\sigma_1\) and \(b\) is inactive.
    🔗
    Hint.
    This is similar to part (b), with \(a\) and \(c\) active instead of \(a\) and \(b\text{.}\) Use the equality condition for Player 1 to find the only possible \(\sigma_2\text{.}\) This time, the inequality condition should fail for \(b\text{.}\) (You don’t need to find \(\sigma_1\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark. In fact, the Nash equilibrium you found in part (b) is the only Nash equilibrium of this game. The remaining cases (\(b\) and \(c\) active, pure strategy profiles, or one player mixing while the other uses a pure strategy) can all be ruled out in the same way.
    🔗
    🔗

    4. A Two-Stage Prisoner’s Dilemma.

    Consider the following Prisoner’s Dilemma, where each player can cooperate (\(c\)) or defect (\(d\)).
    🔗
    Now the players play this Prisoner’s Dilemma twice, and each player’s total payoff is the sum of their payoffs from the two stages. Both players see what happened in the first stage before the second stage starts.
    🔗
    Because the game has two stages, \(c\) and \(d\) are now moves that a player makes in each stage. A strategy for the two-stage game has to say what move to make in each stage, and the move in the second stage can depend on what happened in the first. We will only consider three strategies (there are more). To keep them separate from the moves, we write strategies with capital letters:
    🔗
    • \(C\text{,}\) always cooperate: play \(c\) in both stages.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(D\text{,}\) always defect: play \(d\) in both stages.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(T\text{,}\) tit for tat: play \(c\) in stage 1. In stage 2, copy the move the other player made in stage 1.
      🔗
      🔗
    Restricting the players to these three strategies gives the following game in normal form, with two entries left blank.
    🔗

    (a)

    Find the payoffs for the strategy profiles \((D, T)\) and \((T, D)\text{,}\) and fill them in to complete the payoff matrix. Briefly explain what happens in each stage.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((D, T)\) gives \((5, -1)\) and \((T, D)\) gives \((-1, 5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use best response to find all pure strategy Nash equilibria.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Now look for a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium \((\sigma_1, \sigma_2)\) in which, for each player, \(C\) and \(T\) are active and \(D\) is inactive:
    \begin{equation*} \sigma_1 = pC + (1-p)T, \qquad \sigma_2 = qC + (1-q)T, \qquad 0 \lt p \lt 1,\ 0 \lt q \lt 1. \end{equation*}
    Show that the equality condition of the Fundamental Theorem holds for every \(p\) and \(q\) in \((0, 1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Use the inequality condition to find exactly which values of \(p\) and \(q\) make \((\sigma_1, \sigma_2)\) a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium. Then explain in words what your answer says: how often can a player use always cooperate (\(C\)) before the other player would rather switch to always defect (\(D\))?
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark. Like Exercise 5.7.2, this is a symmetric game. But here there are infinitely many mixed strategy Nash equilibria, and most of them are not symmetric: if \(p\) and \(q\) both satisfy your answer to part (d) but \(p \neq q\text{,}\) the players are using different mixed strategies.
    🔗
    🔗

    5. A Simple Poker Game, Revisited.

    In Exercise 4.4.3 we found that A Simple Poker Game has no Bayesian Nash equilibrium in pure strategies, and we promised that it would have one once we allowed mixed strategies. Let’s find it, this time using the partial derivative method of Section 5.3.1 (Section 5.3, Other Ways to Find Mixed Strategy Nash Equilibria).
    🔗
    Recall that Player 1’s strategies say what he does with the high card and then with the low card. Since \(FB\) and \(FF\) are strictly dominated, we drop them, which leaves the following game:
    🔗
    Write Player 1’s mixed strategy as \(\sigma_1 = p\,BB + (1-p)\,BF\) and Player 2’s as \(\sigma_2 = qB + (1-q)F\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Write each player’s expected payoff as a function of \(p\) and \(q\text{:}\) find \(\pi_1(p, q)\) and \(\pi_2(p, q)\text{,}\) as the book does for Tennis in Section 5.3.1.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use the partial derivative method of Section 5.3.1 to find a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium with \(0 \lt p \lt 1\) and \(0 \lt q \lt 1\text{.}\) Which partial derivatives do you set equal to \(0\text{,}\) and why those ones?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find Player 1’s expected payoff at the Nash equilibrium. Then, without doing a second computation, find Player 2’s expected payoff, and explain why you only needed to compute one of them.
    🔗
    Hint.
    In every cell of the table, the two payoffs add to \(0\text{.}\) So for every strategy profile, including mixed ones, Player 2’s payoff is the negative of Player 1’s: \(\pi_2 = -\pi_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Interpret the equilibrium:
    🔗
    • What does Player 1 do when he has the high card?
      🔗
      🔗
    • When he has the low card, how often does he bid anyway (that is, bluff)?
      🔗
      🔗
    • How often does Player 2 bid back?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Remark. Player 2 can never come out ahead in this game: none of Player 2’s payoffs in the table are positive. The best Player 2 can do is keep the losses small, and the equilibrium mix does exactly that.
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Maximins and Theorem 5.6.

    Consider the following zero-sum game. We’ll come back to it in Exercise 5.7.7.
    🔗

    (a)

    First, suppose each player may only use pure strategies.
    🔗
    1. Find each player’s worst case for each of their pure strategies: \(m_1(a)\) and \(m_1(b)\) for Player 1, and \(m_2(x)\) and \(m_2(y)\) for Player 2. For example,
      \begin{equation*} m_1(a) = \min_{\sigma_2} \pi_1(a, \sigma_2). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Among pure strategies only, which strategy gives each player the largest worst case? Call these \(s_1\) for Player 1 and \(s_2\) for Player 2. What payoffs \(m_1(s_1)\) and \(m_2(s_2)\) do they guarantee? (This is a “max of mins” like a maximin, but it is not the maximin payoff \(m_1^\dagger\) from Section 5.5, because we’re only allowing pure strategies.)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Is \((s_1, s_2)\) a Nash equilibrium? Do the two guaranteed payoffs \(m_1(s_1)\) and \(m_2(s_2)\) add up to \(0\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    (Keep your answers in mind. In Exercise 5.7.7, you’ll see what changes when the players can mix.)
    🔗
    Hint.
    For i: the minimum is over all of Player 2’s strategies, including mixed ones. But against a mixed strategy, Player 1’s payoff is a weighted average of her payoffs against \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) so the worst case is always at a pure strategy.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Now let’s consider a general zero-sum game. Suppose that \((\sigma_1, \sigma_2)\) and \((\tau_1, \tau_2)\) are both Nash equilibria of a two-player zero-sum game, and that \(\pi_1(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) = 5\text{.}\) Answer each question, and say which part of Theorem 5.6 (or the interchangeability property, Checkpoint 5.5.1) tells you.
    🔗
    1. What are \(m_1^\dagger\) and \(m_2^\dagger\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What is \(\pi_1(\tau_1, \tau_2)\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Is \((\sigma_1, \tau_2)\) a Nash equilibrium? What is \(\pi_2(\sigma_1, \tau_2)\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Must \(\sigma_1 = \tau_1\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    For iv: does the interchangeability property say the halves of two Nash equilibria are equal, or only that you can swap them?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark. In part (a), \((s_1, s_2)\) is not a Nash equilibrium, even though the game is zero-sum. This doesn’t contradict Theorem 5.6. In Section 5.5, \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) range over all strategies, including mixed ones, so the maximin strategies and maximin payoffs \(m_i^\dagger\) in Theorem 5.6 are taken over all mixed strategies. As part (a)(ii) points out, \(s_1\) and \(s_2\) are only the best pure strategies, so \((s_1, s_2)\) is not a maximin equilibrium. In Exercise 5.7.7, you’ll see that mixing guarantees Player 1 more than \(m_1(s_1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In fact, Theorem 5.6 couldn’t hold for pure strategies alone. A pair of best pure strategies like \((s_1, s_2)\) always exists, but this game has no pure strategy Nash equilibrium. Mixed strategies are what make the theorem work.
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Computing a Maximin Equilibrium.

    Consider the game from Exercise 5.7.6 (Table 5.7.7). This time the players can use mixed strategies. Write Player 1’s mixed strategy as \(\sigma_1 = pa + (1-p)b\) and Player 2’s as \(\sigma_2 = qx + (1-q)y\text{.}\) Like the book, we write a strategy profile as \((p, q)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    We want Player 1’s worst case \(m_1(p) = \min_{0 \leq q \leq 1} \pi_1(p, q)\) for each \(p\text{.}\) Follow the Battle of the Sexes example in the notes (starting at Table 5.4.5).
    🔗
    1. Show that Player 1’s expected payoff is
      \begin{equation*} \pi_1(p, q) = 7pq - 2p - 3q + 1. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Hold \(p\) fixed and differentiate with respect to \(q\text{.}\) Fill in the blanks:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial q} = \underline{\qquad\qquad}, \quad\text{which is } \begin{cases} \text{negative} \amp \text{if } 0 \leq p \lt \underline{\qquad}, \\ 0 \amp \text{if } p = \underline{\qquad}, \\ \text{positive} \amp \text{if } \underline{\qquad} \lt p \leq 1. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Use the sign from (ii) to fill in which value of \(q\) gives Player 1 her worst case:
      \begin{equation*} m_1(p) = \begin{cases} \pi_1(p, \underline{\qquad}) \amp \text{if } 0 \leq p \lt \underline{\qquad}, \\ \text{any } \pi_1(p, q) \amp \text{if } p = \underline{\qquad}, \\ \pi_1(p, \underline{\qquad}) \amp \text{if } \underline{\qquad} \lt p \leq 1. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
      Then evaluate \(\pi_1(p, 1)\) and \(\pi_1(p, 0)\) using the formula from (i) to write \(m_1(p)\) as a piecewise function of \(p\) alone.
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    If \(\dfrac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial q}\) is negative, \(\pi_1(p, q)\) goes down as \(q\) goes up, so it’s lowest at \(q = 1\text{.}\) If it’s positive, it’s lowest at \(q = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\dfrac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial q} = 7p - 3\text{,}\) which changes sign at \(p = \frac{3}{7}\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} m_1(p) = \begin{cases} 5p - 2 \amp \text{if } 0 \leq p \lt \frac{3}{7}, \\ \frac{1}{7} \amp \text{if } p = \frac{3}{7}, \\ 1 - 2p \amp \text{if } \frac{3}{7} \lt p \leq 1. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Sketch the graph of \(m_1(p)\text{.}\) Use it to find Player 1’s maximin strategy \(p^\dagger\) (write it as a mixed strategy \(\sigma_1^\dagger\) too) and her maximin payoff \(m_1^\dagger\text{.}\) Compare \(m_1^\dagger\) with the payoff Player 1 could guarantee with pure strategies only, from Task 5.7.6.a(ii).
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(p^\dagger = \frac{3}{7}\) and \(m_1^\dagger = \frac{1}{7}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Now find Player 2’s maximin strategy and maximin payoff.
    🔗
    1. Explain, without doing any computation, why
      \begin{equation*} \pi_2(p, q) = -(7pq - 2p - 3q + 1). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Go through the process of part (a) for Player 2 to find her worst case \(m_2(q) = \min_{0 \leq p \leq 1} \pi_2(p, q)\) as a piecewise function of \(q\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Sketch the graph of \(m_2(q)\text{,}\) and use it to find Player 2’s maximin strategy \(q^\dagger\) and her maximin payoff \(m_2^\dagger\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    For i: the game is zero-sum, and you already know Player 1’s payoff. For ii: this time hold \(q\) fixed and look at the sign of \(\dfrac{\partial \pi_2}{\partial p}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Write down the maximin equilibrium \((p^\dagger, q^\dagger)\text{,}\) and check that \(m_1^\dagger + m_2^\dagger = 0\text{.}\) Then, without solving any indifference equations, use Theorem 5.6 to find a Nash equilibrium of this game and each player’s payoff there (hint: this should be quick!!). Say which parts of the theorem you used. Finally, compare with Task 5.7.6.a: what changed when the players were allowed to mix?
    🔗

    8. Triple Rock-Paper-Scissors.

    In ordinary Rock-Paper-Scissors (Problem 5.12.4 in the book), paper beats rock, scissors beats paper, and rock beats scissors. The winner gets \(1\text{,}\) the loser gets \(-1\text{,}\) and a tie gives both players \(0\text{.}\) In Triple Rock-Paper-Scissors, a win with rock is worth three times as much: when rock beats scissors, the winner gets \(3\) and the loser gets \(-3\text{.}\) All other payoffs are the same.
    🔗
    In this exercise, we’ll show that Triple Rock-Paper-Scissors has exactly one Nash equilibrium, using the same idea as One-Card Two-Round Poker (Checkpoint 5.5.2).
    🔗

    (a)

    Briefly explain why Triple Rock-Paper-Scissors is a zero-sum game. (This is what will let us use the interchangeability property later.)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose \((\sigma_1^*, \sigma_2^*)\) is a Nash equilibrium in which Player 2 uses all three of his strategies. Write \(\sigma_1^* = ar + bp + cs\text{.}\) Player 2’s expected payoffs against \(\sigma_1^*\) are
    \begin{align*} \pi_2(\sigma_1^*, r) \amp = -b + 3c,\\ \pi_2(\sigma_1^*, p) \amp = a - c,\\ \pi_2(\sigma_1^*, s) \amp = -3a + b. \end{align*}
    (You don’t need to derive these, but you can check them against the payoff matrix for practice if you want.) Use the Fundamental Theorem, with Player 2’s indifference equations, to find \(\sigma_1^*\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\sigma_1^* = \frac{1}{5}r + \frac{3}{5}p + \frac{1}{5}s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Notice that your \(\sigma_1^*\) uses all three of Player 1’s strategies. Because they are all active, we can just use Player 1’s indifference equations to solve for \(\sigma_2^* \text{.}\) Write \(\sigma_2^* = xr + yp + zs\text{.}\) Player 1’s expected payoffs against \(\sigma_2^*\) are:
    \begin{align*} \pi_1(r, \sigma_2^*) \amp = -y + 3z,\\ \pi_1(p, \sigma_2^*) \amp = x - z,\\ \pi_1(s, \sigma_2^*) \amp = -3x + y. \end{align*}
    Use Player 1’s indifference equations to find \(\sigma_2^*\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Parts (b) and (c) show that if Player 2 uses all three strategies, then so does Player 1, and the only Nash equilibrium of this kind is \((\sigma_1^*, \sigma_2^*)\text{.}\) Use the interchangeability property (Checkpoint 5.5.1) to explain why this implies that Triple Rock-Paper-Scissors has no other Nash equilibria.
    🔗
    Hint.
    This is the same argument as the poker uniqueness activity (Checkpoint 5.5.2), with parts (b) and (c) playing the role of the “fact from 5.4.” Follow the same steps, first for Player 1 and then with the players switched.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    In ordinary Rock-Paper-Scissors, the only Nash equilibrium has both players use \(\frac{1}{3}r + \frac{1}{3}p + \frac{1}{3}s\text{.}\) In Triple Rock-Paper-Scissors, paper is played most often (\(\frac{3}{5}\) of the time), even though it’s rock’s win that got bigger. Speculate why (just informally, you don’t need any mathematical justification).
    🔗
    🔗

    9. Tit for Tat.

    This is based on Problem 5.12.6 in the book. Al’s and Bob’s toy stores each set their prices for two weeks in a row. Each store has three strategies:
    🔗
    • \(h\text{:}\) Always charge high prices.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(l\text{:}\) Always charge low prices.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(t\text{:}\) Tit for tat. Charge high prices the first week. The next week, do whatever the other store did the week before.
      🔗
      🔗
    The total payoffs over the two weeks are:
    🔗

    (a)

    \((t, t)\) is a Nash equilibrium. Recall that the Fundamental Theorem only requires an inactive strategy to do no better than the active ones. Sometimes an inactive strategy ties the active ones and does exactly as well. Then the player is indifferent to this inactive strategy, so they could mix that strategy in without changing their payoff. If the other player’s strategy is still a best response after that, we get new Nash equilibria! This is the book’s advice in Section 5.6, The Ultimatum Minigame: a tie for an inactive strategy is a hint that there are more Nash equilibria in which that strategy is active too. (In the Ultimatum Minigame, this is how the book found the equilibria \((f,\ qa + (1-q)r)\text{.}\))
