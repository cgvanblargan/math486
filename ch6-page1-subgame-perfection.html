    Section 6.1 Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibria

    Back in Section 3.7, we found three Nash equilibria of Big Monkey and Little Monkey: \((w, cw)\text{,}\) \((c, ww)\text{,}\) and \((w, cc)\text{.}\) In this chapter, we’ll see that some Nash equilibria make more sense than others, and we’ll define a stronger kind of equilibrium that tells them apart.
    🔗

    Section 6.1, Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibria.

    Read Section 6.1.
    🔗
    This section is short but dense, since it’s mostly definitions. We’ll work through them with an example right after.
    🔗
    🔗
    Part of the definition of a subgame deals with information sets, which only matter in games with incomplete information. We’ll focus on games with complete information, where things are simpler: every node that isn’t a terminal node is the start of a subgame. So let’s explore these ideas further in the Big Monkey and Little Monkey game.
    🔗
    A strategy profile is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium if, for every subgame, the restriction of the strategy profile to that subgame is a Nash equilibrium of that subgame. Notice that when we look at a subgame, moves at nodes outside the subgame are disregarded in the restricted strategies.
    🔗
    Here is the game tree for Big Monkey and Little Monkey, with Big Monkey moving first. We’ve labeled Little Monkey’s two nodes \(n_1\) (after Big Monkey waits) and \(n_2\) (after Big Monkey climbs).
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.1.2.

    How many subgames does this game have?
    🔗
    • 2
    • Close! You probably counted the subgames starting at \(n_1\) and \(n_2\text{.}\) The whole game, which starts at the root, also counts as a subgame.
    • 3
    • Correct. There is one subgame starting at the root (the whole game), one starting at \(n_1\) (after Big Monkey waits), and one starting at \(n_2\) (after Big Monkey climbs).
    🔗
    Now we’ll use the widget below to explain why \((w, cw)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium. The moves of \((w, cw)\) are drawn thick. Click each of the three nodes to see the subgame that starts there, what \((w, cw)\) restricts to in that subgame, and whether the restriction is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    We showed that in each of the three subgames, the restriction of \((w, cw)\) to the subgame is a Nash equilibrium of that subgame. So \((w, cw)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium.
    🔗

    Notice what happened in the subgame at \(n_1\text{.}\) Here is its table again, with the outcome of the restricted strategy profile boxed:
    🔗
    Little Monkey waits and gets 0, but climbing would give her 1. So the restriction of \((c, ww)\) to this subgame is not a Nash equilibrium. (It is a Nash equilibrium in the other two subgames.) That means \((c, ww)\) is not a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Little Monkey’s plan to wait if Big Monkey waits is what Section 1.6 called a threat: it would hurt Big Monkey, but it would also hurt Little Monkey. \((c, ww)\) is still a Nash equilibrium of the whole game, because Big Monkey climbs, so \(n_1\) is never reached and Little Monkey never has to carry out the threat. Subgame perfection catches it, because it checks every subgame, including the ones that are never reached.

    Checkpoint 6.1.9.

    \((w, cc)\) is a Nash equilibrium of the whole game, but it is not subgame perfect. In which subgame is its restriction not a Nash equilibrium?
    🔗
    • The subgame at \(n_1\text{,}\) after Big Monkey waits.
    • Not quite. At \(n_1\text{,}\) \(cc\) restricts to \(c\text{,}\) and climbing gives Little Monkey 1, which is better than the 0 she’d get from waiting. So that restriction is a Nash equilibrium.
    • The subgame at \(n_2\text{,}\) after Big Monkey climbs.
    • Correct. At \(n_2\text{,}\) \(cc\) restricts to \(c\text{,}\) and climbing gives Little Monkey 3, but waiting would give her 4. Her plan to climb if Big Monkey climbs is a promise: it would help Big Monkey, at a cost to Little Monkey.
    🔗

    Section 6.3, Subgame Perfect Equilibria and Backward Induction.

    Read Section 6.3.
    🔗
    The main point is in the first paragraph: backward induction finds subgame perfect Nash equilibria, because it chooses the best move at every node, whether or not that node is reached. Notice that \((w, cw)\text{,}\) the subgame perfect equilibrium we found above, is exactly the backward induction answer from Chapter 1. You can skim the paragraph about chess.
    🔗
    🔗
    The last paragraph of Section 6.3 explains why we care. In games that go on forever, there’s no last move to start backward induction from, but we can still start the game at any point and ask whether the players’ plans make sense from there. That’s how we’ll use subgame perfection for repeated games on the next page.
