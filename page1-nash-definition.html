    Section 3.1 The Nash Equilibrium and Two Important Examples

    It’s time to learn about one of the biggest concepts in game theory: the Nash equilibrium!
    🔗

    Section 3.1, Big Monkey and Little Monkey 3 and the Definition of Nash Equilibria.

    Read Section 3.1.
    🔗
    This section introduces the definition of a Nash equilibrium. It’s essentially a strategy profile where no player has an incentive to change their strategy, which is where the term “equilibrium” comes from.
    🔗
    🔗
    We’ll take a moment to rewrite all the notation just introduced, so you have it in one place for reference. It might also be helpful to reread it here now.
    🔗
    We write \(s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\) for the strategy profile we are testing to determine whether it’s an equilibrium. The profile \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium provided:
    🔗
    • For every \(s_1 \in S_1\text{,}\) \(\pi_1(s_1^*, s_2^*, \ldots, s_n^*) \geq \pi_1(s_1, s_2^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\) (in the compact notation: \(\pi_1(s^*) \geq \pi_1(s_1, s^*_{-1})\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    • For every \(s_2 \in S_2\text{,}\) \(\pi_2(s_1^*, s_2^*, \ldots, s_n^*) \geq \pi_2(s_1^*, s_2, s_3^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\) (\(\pi_2(s^*) \geq \pi_2(s_2, s^*_{-2})\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    • And so on, down to
      🔗
      🔗
    • For every \(s_n \in S_n\text{,}\) \(\pi_n(s_1^*, \ldots, s_n^*) \geq \pi_n(s_1^*, \ldots, s_{n-1}^*, s_n)\) (\(\pi_n(s^*) \geq \pi_n(s_n, s^*_{-n})\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    Let’s put the first bullet point into words: if player 1 alone switches to some other strategy \(s_1\text{,}\) while everyone else stays exactly where they are, then player 1’s payoff will not go up.
    🔗
    The quickest way to write the definition is with the more compact version: \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium if for each \(i = 1, \ldots, n\) it holds that \(\pi_i(s^*) \geq \pi_i(s_i, s^*_{-i})\) for every \(s_i \in S_i\text{.}\) However, you should also have a good sense of what this means conceptually in words, which is tested in the check below!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1.1.

    Suppose \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium of some game. Which of the following says what that means?
    🔗
    • If the players are all at \(s^*\text{,}\) then every player is getting the highest payoff available to them anywhere in the game.
    • This is much stronger than the definition. A player’s payoff depends on what everyone else does, so the highest payoff available to them somewhere in the game may not be reachable by anything they can do themselves. We’ll see a game shortly where both players would rather be somewhere else entirely.
    • If the players are all at \(s^*\text{,}\) then no player can raise their own payoff by changing strategy on their own, with everyone else staying put.
    • Correct. Notice the two things this sentence is careful about: the change is made by a single player, and everyone else is held fixed.
    • Each player’s strategy in \(s^*\) gives them a higher payoff than their other strategies, no matter what the other players do.
    • That’s the definition of a dominant strategy from Chapter 2, not of a Nash equilibrium. The difference is the phrase “no matter what the other players do.” The Nash condition only compares payoffs while the other players are held fixed at \(s^*_{-i}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Section 3.1 also defines a strict Nash equilibrium, which is the same idea with \(\geq\) replaced by \(\gt\text{.}\) Note that every strict Nash equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium, but not every Nash equilibrium is strict.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1.2.

    Suppose \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium. What else must be true for \(s^*\) to be a strict Nash equilibrium?
    🔗
    • At least one of the inequalities in the definition is strict, meaning some player would do strictly worse by changing their strategy on their own.
    • Not quite. Strict means every inequality is strict, not just one of them. A profile where one player would do strictly worse by deviating, but another player has a tie, is still a Nash equilibrium, just not a strict one.
    • It must be the only Nash equilibrium of the game.
    • Strictness is about the inequalities at this one profile, not about how many equilibria the game has. You’ll see shortly that Stag Hunt has two Nash equilibria, and both of them are strict.
    • Each inequality in the definition is strict. That is, every player, changing to any other strategy of their own while everyone else stays put, gets a strictly lower payoff.
    • Correct. Every inequality, for every player and every alternative strategy that player has.
    🔗
    Now let’s actually practice using the definition. Section 3.2 finds Nash equilibria by inspection, which means checking the definition for every strategy profile. The two widgets below will walk you through all four profiles of each of the first two examples in that section. The first one is the familiar Prisoner’s Dilemma! After you answer each question, press “Next profile” at the bottom to move on to the next strategy profile.
    🔗
    As you just saw, the Prisoner’s Dilemma has exactly one Nash equilibrium, and it leads to a bad outcome for both executives. Keep that in mind as you work through the next game, which behaves quite differently! This next example is “Stag Hunt,” and more context about the setup will come in the reading that follows.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1.5.

    You’ve now found the Nash equilibria of two games by inspection. The Prisoner’s Dilemma had one, \((\text{talk}, \text{talk})\text{,}\) paying \((-6,-6)\text{,}\) even though both executives would rather have \((-1,-1)\text{.}\) Stag Hunt had two, and both hunters prefer \((\text{stag}, \text{stag})\) to \((\text{hare}, \text{hare})\text{.}\) Taken together, what do these two results tell you about Nash equilibria?
    🔗
    • A Nash equilibrium may or may not be good for the players. It is a profile the game can get stuck at, because no player can improve their own payoff by changing strategy alone.
    • Correct, and this is the one statement that covers both games at once. “Stuck” is the right word: the definition says nothing about whether an equilibrium is a good outcome, only that no single player can walk away from it and do better.
    • A Nash equilibrium is the outcome that is best for all the players.
    • The Prisoner’s Dilemma rules this out. Both executives would rather have \((-1,-1)\) than the equilibrium payoff \((-6,-6)\text{,}\) and yet the equilibrium is where the game gets stuck.
    • A Nash equilibrium is an outcome that no player would rather trade for a different one.
    • Again the Prisoner’s Dilemma is the counterexample. Both executives would happily trade \((-6,-6)\) for \((-1,-1)\text{.}\) What they cannot do is get there by changing strategy on their own, and that’s the only thing the definition asks about.
    🔗
    Let’s think about how this all relates to the strictly dominant strategies we saw in Chapter 2. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, \(\text{talk}\) strictly dominates \(\text{refuse}\) for both executives, so eliminating dominated strategies would have found \((\text{talk}, \text{talk})\) too, which is exactly the Nash equilibrium!
    🔗
    On the other hand, Stag Hunt has no dominant strategies at all, which might seem odd since \((\text{stag}, \text{stag})\) gives a payoff of \((2,2)\text{,}\) the best possible outcome for both players! Check on your own, using the definition, that \(\text{stag}\) is not a dominant strategy. On top of that, Stag Hunt has a second Nash equilibrium, which gives a worse payoff of \((1,1)\) to both players.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1.6.

    Chicken, Battle of the Sexes, and Stag Hunt all have two Nash equilibria. What separates the first two games from Stag Hunt?
    🔗
    • The equilibria in Chicken and Battle of the Sexes are not strict, while the ones in Stag Hunt are.
    • All of these equilibria are strict. In Battle of the Sexes, for instance, if Alice leaves the concert while Bob stays there, her payoff drops from \(2\) to \(0\text{,}\) a strict decrease. Strictness isn’t what distinguishes these games.
    • In Stag Hunt both players prefer the same one of the two equilibria, while in Chicken and Battle of the Sexes each player prefers a different one.
    • Correct. In Stag Hunt the players agree about which equilibrium they want and the difficulty is getting there together. In Chicken and Battle of the Sexes they disagree, so there’s a genuine conflict over which equilibrium should happen. That’s why Stag Hunt is called a pure coordination game and the other two are not.
    • In Chicken and Battle of the Sexes, a player can reach their preferred equilibrium by changing strategy on their own.
    • If that were true, the profile they started at would not have been a Nash equilibrium in the first place. That’s exactly what the definition rules out. Getting from one equilibrium to the other requires both players to move.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1.7.

    Section 3.2.4 ends by asking you a question directly. If you were Alice, which event would you go to, and why? There’s no single right answer here! Notice that the definition of Nash equilibrium gives you no help at all in choosing between the two equilibria, which is a gap in the theory rather than a gap in your understanding. Chapter 5 comes back to it.
