    Section 6.3 The Folk Theorem

    On the last page, we found that cooperating forever is a Nash equilibrium of the repeated Wine Merchant game when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{.}\) Are there other Nash equilibria? The Folk Theorem says there are a lot of them. (A “folk theorem” is a result that everyone seems to know, but nobody knows who proved it first.)
    🔗

    Section 6.8.1, Payoff Vectors.

    Read Section 6.8 up through Subsection 6.8.1.
    🔗
    This subsection describes all the payoff pairs that the two players can get in a single round of the game, if they use mixed strategies. The four-step construction is easier to see than to read, so use the interactive below alongside it.
    🔗
    🔗
    In the interactive, the Wine Merchant game uses the book’s labels: \((a, b) = (1, 1)\text{,}\) \((c, d) = (-4, 4)\text{,}\) \((e, f) = (4, -4)\text{,}\) and \((g, h) = (0, 0)\text{.}\) Here \(p\) is the probability that the Merchant sells good wine, and \(q\) is the probability that Bernard pays. The slider \(q\) places \(r(q)\) on one edge and \(s(q)\) on another (steps 1 and 2 in the book), and \(p\) slides the point along the dashed segment between them (step 3). The shaded triangle is the set of all possible payoff vectors. The darker part is where both payoffs are positive, just like Figure 6.8.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.3.2.

    Both players flip a fair coin each round to decide whether to cooperate, so \(p = q = \frac{1}{2}\text{.}\) What is the payoff vector? Check your answer with the interactive.
    🔗
    • \(\left(\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4}\right)\)
    • Correct. Each of the four outcomes happens with probability \(\frac{1}{4}\text{,}\) so the payoff vector is \(\frac{1}{4}\big((1,1) + (-4,4) + (4,-4) + (0,0)\big) = \left(\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4}\right)\text{.}\)
    • \(\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)\)
    • Not quite. That’s halfway between \((1,1)\) and \((0,0)\text{,}\) but all four outcomes are possible here, each with probability \(\frac{1}{4}\text{.}\)
    • \((0, 0)\)
    • Not quite. The \(-4\) and \(4\) cancel out, but the \((1,1)\) outcome is still there.
    🔗

    Section 6.8.2, Minimax Payoffs.

    Read Section 6.8.2.
    🔗
    This is short, but read it slowly. There’s a trap here, explained below.
    🔗
    🔗
    Warning: minimax is not maximin! In Section 5.5 we met the maximin payoff. The minimax payoff in this section sounds almost the same, but it’s a different idea.
    🔗
    • Maximin (Section 5.5): you choose first, expecting the worst. For each of your strategies, find its worst case, then pick the best of those. This is the most you can guarantee yourself.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Minimax (Section 6.8.2): your opponent chooses first, trying to hurt you, and you best-respond. For each of the opponent’s strategies, find your best response, then take the worst of those. This is the harshest punishment the opponent can force on you.
      🔗
      🔗
    The two can be different. In Matching Pennies, if we only allow pure strategies, your maximin payoff is \(-1\) (any coin you choose can be beaten), but your minimax payoff is \(+1\) (once you see your opponent’s coin, you can always match it).
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.3.3.

    Build the formula for Player 1’s minimax payoff \(m_1\) by putting the blocks in order. Here \(s_i\) are Player 1’s pure strategies and \(t_j\) are Player 2’s. Not every block belongs!
    🔗
    Hint.
    The operation on the inside happens first. Player 1’s best response comes first, and then Player 2 picks the strategy that makes that best response as bad as possible.

    Checkpoint 6.3.4.

    Recall Chicken from Section 3.2.3:
    🔗
    What is Teenager 1’s minimax payoff \(m_1\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(-1\)
    • Correct. If Teenager 2 goes straight, Teenager 1’s best response is to swerve, for \(-1\text{.}\) If Teenager 2 swerves, Teenager 1’s best response is to go straight, for 1. The worst of these is \(-1\text{.}\)
    • \(-2\)
    • Not quite. \(-2\) is the worst payoff in Teenager 1’s column, but minimax assumes Teenager 1 best-responds. If Teenager 2 goes straight, Teenager 1 would swerve rather than crash.
    • 1
    • Not quite. 1 is Teenager 1’s best response payoff when Teenager 2 swerves. But Teenager 2 gets to pick the strategy that hurts Teenager 1 the most, so take the minimum over Teenager 2’s choices.
    🔗

    Sections 6.8.3 and 6.8.4, The Folk Theorem.

    Read Sections 6.8.3 and 6.8.4. Read the statement of Theorem 6.2 and the proof of part (1) carefully.
    🔗
    You can skip the proof of part (2), which starts with “We will not show (2) in general.” The idea is all you need: the players take turns among the four outcomes in just the right proportions, so the average payoff is \((v_1, v_2)\text{.}\) If anyone ever deviates, the other player punishes them forever, holding them down to their minimax payoff.
    🔗
    🔗
    In plain words, the Folk Theorem says: any payoff vector where both players get more than their minimax payoffs can be the average payoff of a Nash equilibrium, as long as the players care enough about the future. For the Wine Merchant, that’s the whole dark region of the triangle.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.3.6.

    Read the statement of part (1) again, and then the proof. Does the proof actually show what the statement says? Look closely at the words “in every round.”

    Example 6.3.7. The Cheating Merchant.

    Here’s an equilibrium that the Folk Theorem promises, and that might surprise you. Suppose the players use the following strategies:
    🔗
    • The Merchant sells good wine five times, then sells bad wine once, and repeats this forever.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Bernard pays every single time, even when he gets bad wine.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If either player ever deviates from this plan, both defect forever.
      🔗
      🔗
    Over each cycle of six rounds, the Merchant gets \(1+1+1+1+1+4 = 9\) and Bernard gets \(1+1+1+1+1-4 = 1\text{.}\) So the average payoffs are \(\left(\frac{3}{2}, \frac{1}{6}\right)\text{.}\) Both are above the minimax payoff 0, so by the Folk Theorem this can be a Nash equilibrium for large enough \(\delta\text{.}\) Checking every possible deviation, it is a Nash equilibrium when \(\delta\) is at least about \(0.97\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Bernard knowingly accepts being cheated once every six rounds. Why? Because if he ever steals, the relationship ends, and he gets 0 forever after. Getting cheated occasionally still beats that, on average.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.3.8.

    Plain cooperation needs \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{,}\) but the cheating merchant equilibrium needs \(\delta\) close to \(0.97\text{.}\) In which round is Bernard most tempted to steal?
    🔗
    • The round when he gets bad wine.
    • Close, but not quite. Stealing bad wine gets him 0 instead of \(-4\text{,}\) but the round before is worse.
    • The round just before he gets bad wine.
    • Correct. Stealing then gets him 4 right away, and the very next thing cooperation has to offer him is the \(-4\) round. So the future has to matter a lot to keep him honest.
    • The round just after he gets bad wine.
    • Not quite. Right after being cheated, Bernard has five cooperative rounds ahead of him before the next \(-4\text{,}\) so cooperating looks relatively good.
    🔗
    Try to find the cheating merchant’s payoff vector, \(\left(\frac{3}{2}, \frac{1}{6}\right)\text{,}\) on the triangle in the interactive above. It’s on the edge between \((1,1)\) and \((4,-4)\text{,}\) which makes sense, since the only outcomes that happen are \((c, c)\) and \((d, c)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.3.9.

    The Folk Theorem says that almost any outcome can be a Nash equilibrium of a repeated game, including outcomes where one player keeps getting cheated. Is this good news or bad news for game theory? Think about what it means for using game theory to predict what people will do.
