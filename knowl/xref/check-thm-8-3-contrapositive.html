Checkpoint 8.1.11.

Consider a symmetric game where each player has three pure strategies, \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\text{.}\) Suppose \(\sigma = p_1 a + p_2 b + p_3 c\) with \(p_1, p_2, p_3 \gt 0\text{,}\) so \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\) are all active pure strategies in \(\sigma\text{.}\) Suppose also that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a Nash equilibrium. You also find that \((a, a)\) is a Nash equilibrium. What can you conclude about \(\sigma\text{?}\)
🔗
  • \(\sigma\) is not an evolutionarily stable state.
  • Right. If \(\sigma\) were evolutionarily stable, then since all pure strategies are active in \(\sigma\text{,}\) Theorem 8.3 would say \((\tau, \tau)\) is not a Nash equilibrium for any \(\tau \neq \sigma\text{.}\) But \(\tau = a\) gives a Nash equilibrium \((a, a)\text{,}\) so \(\sigma\) can’t be evolutionarily stable.
  • \(\sigma\) is an evolutionarily stable state, because \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
  • Not quite. Being a Nash equilibrium is necessary for evolutionary stability (condition (1) of Theorem 8.1), but it is not enough. Think about what Theorem 8.3 says about \((a, a)\text{.}\)
  • Both \(\sigma\) and \(a\) are evolutionarily stable states.
  • Not quite. By Theorem 8.3, if \(\sigma\) were evolutionarily stable, there would be no other evolutionarily stable states, and \((a, a)\) couldn’t even be a Nash equilibrium.
