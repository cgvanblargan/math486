Exercise 9. A Duopoly Where the Firms Have Different Costs.

(a) The payoff functions.

Determine the payoff function for each firm.
🔗
Solution.
\begin{align*} \pi_1(q_1,q_2) \amp = \bigl[150 - q_1 - q_2\bigr]q_1 - 9q_1 = (141 - q_2)q_1 - q_1^2,\\ \pi_2(q_1,q_2) \amp = \bigl[150 - q_1 - q_2\bigr]q_2 - 4q_2 = (146 - q_1)q_2 - q_2^2. \end{align*}
🔗
🔗

(b) Firm 2’s best response.

Find \(b(q_1)\text{.}\)
🔗
Solution.
With \(q_1\) held fixed, \(\pi_2\) is a quadratic in \(q_2\) with \(q_2^2\) coefficient \(-1 \lt 0\text{,}\) so it is an upside-down parabola and its critical point is the global maximum. There is no boundary to check, since \(q_2\) ranges over all real numbers.
🔗
\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial \pi_2}{\partial q_2} = 146 - q_1 - 2q_2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b(q_1) = 73 - \tfrac{1}{2}q_1. \end{equation*}
🔗
🔗

(c) Firm 1’s payoff after substitution.

Simplify \(\pi_1\bigl(q_1, b(q_1)\bigr)\) to a function of \(q_1\) alone.
🔗
Solution.
\begin{equation*} \pi_1\bigl(q_1, b(q_1)\bigr) = \bigl(141 - (73 - \tfrac{1}{2}q_1)\bigr)q_1 - q_1^2 = 68q_1 - \tfrac{1}{2}q_1^2. \end{equation*}
🔗
🔗

(d) The rational outcome.

Report \(q_1^*\text{,}\) \(q_2^*\text{,}\) the market price, and each firm’s profit.
🔗
Solution.
Again an upside-down parabola (the \(q_1^2\) coefficient is \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\)), so the critical point is the maximum: \(\dfrac{d}{dq_1}\bigl(68q_1 - \tfrac{1}{2}q_1^2\bigr) = 68 - q_1 = 0\text{,}\) so \(q_1^* = 68\text{.}\)
🔗
  • Quantities: \(q_1^* = 68\) and \(q_2^* = b(68) = 39\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  • Market price: \(p^* = 150 - 68 - 39 = 43\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  • Firm 1’s profit: \(\pi_1(q_1^*,q_2^*) = (43-9)(68) = 2312\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  • Firm 2’s profit: \(\pi_2(q_1^*,q_2^*) = (43-4)(39) = 1521\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
🔗
🔗

(e) Does the 2-to-1 split survive?

Compare \(q_1^*/q_2^*\) and \(\pi_1/\pi_2\) to \(2\text{,}\) and say which assumption of Section 1.11 fails.
🔗
Solution.
\(\dfrac{q_1^*}{q_2^*} = \dfrac{68}{39} \approx 1.74\) and \(\dfrac{\pi_1}{\pi_2} = \dfrac{2312}{1521} \approx 1.52\text{.}\) Neither is \(2\text{,}\) and they are not equal to each other.
🔗
Section 1.11 assumes both firms have the same unit cost \(c\text{.}\) Here \(c_1 = 9\) and \(c_2 = 4\text{,}\) so that assumption fails. Firm 2’s cost advantage partly offsets Firm 1’s first-mover advantage, leaving Firm 1 ahead by less than the usual factor of two.
