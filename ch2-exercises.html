    1. Hawk and Dove.

    The Hawk-Dove game is a two-player game used to analyze animal behavior in territorial conflicts among animals of the same species. There are two strategies available to each player.
    🔗
    • hawk (\(h\)): fight aggressively until you prevail or are injured.
      🔗
      🔗
    • dove (\(d\)): bluff by displaying, but retreat if the other player begins to fight.
      🔗
      🔗
    We use two parameters, both positive real numbers.
    🔗
    • \(v\text{:}\) the value of the territory or object being fought over.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(w\text{:}\) the cost of getting injured in fighting.
      🔗
      🔗
    This leads to the payoff matrix shown below. The factor of \(\frac{1}{2}\) when both players use the same strategy indicates an expected payoff, assuming each player has an equal chance of prevailing.
    🔗
    Throughout this exercise, assume that \(w\) is some fixed positive value, and that \(v\) can be any positive real number. In particular, \(v\) need not be an integer or even a rational number.
    🔗
    Your solutions should demonstrate your reasoning. It is not enough to state an answer with no explanation, or without some work that demonstrates your thinking.
    🔗

    (a)

    Use payoff notation to indicate the two inequalities that must be satisfied so that \(d\) is strictly dominated by \(h\) for player 1. Since the game is symmetric, we only need to consider the perspective of player 1.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use the result from part (a) to find all values of \(v > 0\) such that \(d\) is strictly dominated by \(h\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (c)

    Find all values of \(v > 0\) such that the resulting game has no dominated strategy, either strictly or weakly.
    🔗

    (d)

    Find all values of \(v > 0\) such that the resulting game has a strategy that is weakly, but not strictly, dominated. Indicate which strategy is dominated.

    3. The Common Pasture.

    Prior to the British agricultural revolution in the 17th century, most agriculture in Britain occurred on common land.
    🔗
    Consider a common pasture where \(N\) individuals graze their cows. The pasture can productively support \(kN\) cows. If the total number of cows exceeds \(kN\text{,}\) the pasture becomes degraded and will support fewer cows.
    🔗
    Suppose that each individual currently grazes \(k\) cows. We will assume that each individual has two strategies available.
    🔗
    • The responsible strategy (\(R\)): continue grazing \(k\) cows.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The irresponsible strategy (\(I\)): graze \(k+1\) cows.
      🔗
      🔗
    Each cow in the pasture above the \(kN\) limit imposes a cost \(c > 0\) to the community in terms of degradation. This cost is shared equally by all individuals. Thus, for every cow above \(kN\) in the pasture, each individual experiences a cost of \(\frac{c}{N}\text{.}\) Each individual also realizes a profit \(p\) for each of their cows that graze in the pasture.
    🔗
    Assume \(\frac{c}{N} \lt p \lt c\text{.}\) That is, the cost of grazing an extra cow, to the community as a whole, is greater than the profit from the cow, but each individual’s share of the cost is less than the profit.
    🔗

    (a)

    Let \(\pi_i(s_i, m)\) denote the payoff to player \(i\) when \(m\) other players have chosen to graze \(k+1\) cows, where \(m \in \{0, 1, \ldots, N-1\}\text{.}\) Write out the payoff to player \(i\) for each of their own strategy choices. That is, determine the payoffs \(\pi_i(R, m)\) and \(\pi_i(I, m)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use these payoffs to prove that \(R\) is strictly dominated by \(I\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    If every player uses their dominant strategy \(I\text{,}\) what is the payoff to each player? How does this compare with the payoff if every player instead plays their dominated strategy \(R\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    How does this game compare with the prisoner’s dilemma game?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Locating Two Banks.

    Two competing banks each plan to open a branch along the main avenue of a particular city. The avenue has 99 blocks and each bank can open their new branch in any one of the 99 blocks. It is possible that they could each choose the same block.
    🔗
    We will make the following assumptions.
    🔗
    • Each of the 99 blocks is home to exactly 100 residents, and each resident will become a customer of one of the two bank branches.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The 99 blocks can be represented as regions along a line, as shown in Figure 2.5.4.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Each resident will become a customer of the bank that is closest to the block where they live.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If a particular block is equidistant from both banks, we will assume half the residents from that block become customers of one bank, and half become customers of the second bank.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The payoff to each bank is the total number of new customers.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The players \(A\) and \(B\) will simultaneously choose where to build their respective banks. Neither knows in advance where the other is choosing to locate.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Each player has the strategy set \(S_i = \{1, 2, \ldots, 99\}\text{,}\) denoting their location choices.
      🔗
      🔗
    As an example of the customer counts, suppose bank \(A\) is in block 20 and bank \(B\) is in block 60. Then all the residents from blocks 1 through 39 will become customers of bank \(A\text{,}\) all of the residents from blocks 41 through 99 will become customers of bank \(B\text{,}\) and the residents of block 40 will be divided between the two banks. Bank \(A\) will have 3950 new customers and bank \(B\) will have 5950 new customers.
    🔗
    In solving this problem, please do not write out a \(99 \times 99\) matrix game. It is also not necessary to determine payoff functions for each bank. Instead, you might begin by fixing the location of one bank and thinking about how the payoff of the other bank changes as the location of that bank varies.
    🔗

    (a)

    Argue that for \(A\) the strategy \(s_A = 2\) is not dominated by \(s_A = 3\text{.}\) Recalling the definition of a dominated strategy, you need to show that there is at least one choice for player \(B\) such that player \(A\) does better by choosing \(s_A = 2\) instead of \(s_A = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    It doesn’t matter that we have chosen \(A\) here. The problem is symmetric and the same result will hold for \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Argue that \(s_A = 1\) is strictly dominated by \(s_A = 2\text{.}\) Because the game is symmetric in the two players, an argument for \(A\) also settles the case for \(B\text{,}\) so you only need to do this once.
    🔗
    You are not asked to repeat the computation showing that \(s_i = 99\) is strictly dominated by \(s_i = 98\text{.}\) Instead, describe a correspondence between block numbers that turns the argument you just gave into the argument for \(s_i = 99\text{,}\) and say why payoffs are unaffected by it.
    🔗
    Hint.
    To show that \(s_A = 2\) strictly dominates \(s_A = 1\text{,}\) fix player \(A\) at block 1, compare with \(A\) at block 2, and break into three cases according to where \(B\) locates:
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle s_B = 1\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle s_B = 2\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle s_B \geq 3\)
      🔗
      🔗
    In the third case, the increase in \(A\)’s payoff from moving to block 2 is the same for every value of \(s_B\) in that case.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Following the work in the previous step we eliminate the strictly dominated strategies 1 and 99 from each player’s strategy set. Briefly explain, without going through the explicit computations you did in part (b), why after eliminating blocks 1 and 99 as strategy choices, \(s_i = 2\) is strictly dominated by \(s_i = 3\) and \(s_i = 98\) is strictly dominated by \(s_i = 97\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Also address: in part (a) you showed that \(s_A = 2\) is not dominated by \(s_A = 3\) in the original game. What changed?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    We can continue this process of iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, eventually arriving at the reduced game where \(S_i = \{49, 50, 51\}\text{.}\) Check that in this reduced game, \(s_i = 49\) and \(s_i = 51\) are both strictly dominated by \(s_i = 50\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \(s_A = 49\text{,}\) show the explicit computations supporting your reasoning in each of the three cases for \(B\)’s choice: \(s_B = 49\text{,}\) \(s_B = 50\text{,}\) and \(s_B = 51\text{.}\) Then explain why the argument for \(s_A = 51\) is similar. Because the game is symmetric in the two players, the same argument works for \(B\text{,}\) so you do not need to repeat it.
    🔗
    Note that although we have eliminated all other locations as strategy choices for each bank, we have not eliminated the locations themselves. All of these customers still exist. We have only eliminated choices on where to locate each bank.
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Based on the results from part (d), is \((50, 50)\) a dominant strategy equilibrium of the original game? Briefly explain.
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    In the original game, before any strategies are eliminated, does \(s_i = 50\) strictly dominate every other strategy available to player \(i\text{?}\) Explain why or why not.
