    Read through the setup, but you can skim the algebra: the computations follow the same steps as Microsoft vs. Apple, just with letters instead of numbers. Take note of the result at the end and of Figure 10.3, which we summarize below!
    🔗
    Note: Figure 10.3 has a typo. It labels two of the pictures “(3)”. The bottom-right picture, with \(a \lt c\) and \(d \lt b\text{,}\) is case (4).
    🔗
    🔗
    Alright, now for a quick summary! After substituting \(p_2 = 1 - p_1\text{,}\) the replicator system becomes the single equation
    \begin{equation*} \dot{p}_1 = p_1(1 - p_1)\bigl((a - c) + (d - b)p_1 - (d - b)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    Its equilibria are:
    • \(p_1 = 0\text{,}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(p_1 = 1\text{,}\) and
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle p_1^* = \frac{d - b}{(a - c) + (d - b)}\text{,}\) provided \(p_1^*\) is between 0 and 1.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    For \(0 \lt p_1 \lt 1\text{,}\) the factors \(p_1\) and \(1 - p_1\) are positive, so the sign of \(\dot{p}_1\) comes from the last factor. That factor is linear in \(p_1\text{,}\) so it changes sign at most once. This gives the four possible cases shown in Figure 10.3.
    🔗

    Comparing with Theorem 8.5.

    These are exactly the four cases of Theorem 8.5 from Chapter 8 (see Section 8.4, Evolutionary Stability with Two Pure Strategies (Part 1)), and two things match up.
    🔗
    First, the formula for the interior equilibrium \(p_1^*\) is the same as formula (8.5) in the book. So the interior equilibrium of the replicator system is exactly the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium \(\sigma^* = p_1^* s_1 + (1 - p_1^*)s_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Second, here are the four cases, with the stable equilibria of the replicator system next to the evolutionarily stable states from Theorem 8.5:
    🔗
    🔗
    The last two columns match in every row. (These four cases assume \(a \neq c\) and \(b \neq d\text{.}\)) As the book puts it at the end of Section 10.3: in all cases, the asymptotically stable equilibria correspond to evolutionarily stable population states. So for games with two pure strategies, the static idea from Chapter 8 (a state that can’t be invaded) and the dynamic idea from this chapter (a state the population returns to) give the same answer. This is what we saw for Microsoft vs. Apple, which is in case (3).

    Hawks and Doves.

    Recall the game of Hawks and Doves from Section 8.4.3. Animals fight over a resource using one of two strategies: Hawk (\(h\)), which fights until injured or until the opponent retreats, and Dove (\(d\)), which displays hostility but retreats if the opponent won’t. The parameters are
    • \(v\) = value of what they are fighting over,
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(w\) = cost of injury,
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(t\) = cost of protracted display,
      🔗
      🔗
    where \(v\text{,}\) \(w\text{,}\) and \(t\) are all positive and \(v \lt w\text{.}\) The payoff matrix is
    🔗
    🔗
    Before reading the book’s analysis, let’s see how much we can figure out from Figure 10.3 alone. Take \(s_1 = h\) and \(s_2 = d\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 10.2.4.

    Which case of Figure 10.3 is Hawks and Doves in?
    🔗
    • Case (1): \(a \gt c\) and \(d \lt b\)
    • Not quite. Here \(a = \frac{v - w}{2}\) and \(c = 0\text{.}\) Since \(v \lt w\text{,}\) \(a\) is negative, so \(a \lt c\text{:}\) a hawk does worse than a dove against a hawk.
    • Case (2): \(a \lt c\) and \(d \gt b\)
    • Not quite. \(a \lt c\) is right, but compare \(d\) and \(b\text{:}\) \(d = \frac{v}{2} - t\) and \(b = v\text{.}\) Since \(t \gt 0\text{,}\) \(d \lt b\text{.}\)
    • Case (3): \(a \gt c\) and \(d \gt b\)
    • Not quite. Case (3) would mean a hawk does better than a dove against a hawk. But \(a = \frac{v - w}{2} \lt 0 = c\text{,}\) since the cost of injury is larger than the value of the resource.
    • Case (4): \(a \lt c\) and \(d \lt b\)
    • Right. \(a = \frac{v - w}{2} \lt 0 = c\) because \(v \lt w\text{,}\) and \(d = \frac{v}{2} - t \lt v = b\) because \(t \gt 0\text{.}\) Each strategy is the best response to the other.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 10.2.5.

    Knowing only which case Hawks and Doves is in, what can we conclude about the evolution of the population?
    🔗
    • From any starting state with \(0 \lt p_1 \lt 1\text{,}\) the population tends toward the mixed state \(\sigma^* = p_1^* h + (1 - p_1^*)d\text{.}\)
    • Right. In case (4), \(p_1^*\) is the only stable equilibrium, and both pure states are unstable. This agrees with Theorem 8.5, which says \(\sigma^*\) is evolutionarily stable.
    • Hawks take over the population.
    • Not quite. In case (4), the all-hawk state \(p_1 = 1\) is unstable. When almost everyone is a hawk, doves do better, because they avoid costly injuries.
    • Doves take over the population.
    • Not quite. In case (4), the all-dove state \(p_1 = 0\) is unstable. When almost everyone is a dove, a hawk takes the resource from every dove it meets.
    • It depends on the starting state: some populations end up all hawks and some end up all doves.
    • Not quite. That describes case (3), with a tipping point. In case (4), the arrows on both sides point toward \(p_1^*\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Section 10.4, Hawks and Doves Revisited.

    Read Section 10.4.
    🔗
    The book confirms our conclusion by computing directly. Make sure you get the gist of each step. The algebra in step 3 can be skimmed.
    1. Compute the payoffs \(\pi_1(h, \sigma)\text{,}\) \(\pi_1(d, \sigma)\text{,}\) and \(\pi_1(\sigma, \sigma)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Write the replicator equation for \(\dot{p}_1\text{.}\) (The book also writes \(\dot{p}_2\text{,}\) but we don’t need it.)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Substitute \(p_2 = 1 - p_1\) and factor out \(1 - p_1\text{,}\) to get \(\dot{p}_1 = \frac{1}{2}(1 - p_1)\bigl(v + 2t - p_1(w + 2t)\bigr)p_1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Find the equilibria: \(p_1 = 0\text{,}\) \(p_1 = 1\text{,}\) and \(p_1^* = \frac{v + 2t}{w + 2t}\text{,}\) which is the mixed Nash equilibrium from Section 8.4.3.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Use the phase portrait (Figure 10.4) to determine stability: \(p_1^*\) is stable.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Notice the book’s last sentence: we could have gotten this phase portrait from case (4) of Figure 10.3, which is exactly what you just did.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    So far, every population has tended towards an equilibrium. To finish, let’s take a quick look at what can happen with three strategies. We won’t go into the mathematical techniques used in this section, so this is just a quick look to get the main idea!

    Section 10.5, Side-blotched Lizards.

    Skim Section 10.5. This section is here to show you something new: with three strategies, a population doesn’t have to settle down at an equilibrium at all. Read the background on the lizards and look at the payoff matrix. You can skip the rest of the math. With three strategies, the replicator system has two variables, and we won’t be doing multivariable analysis in this class. Then look at Figure 10.5 and read the conclusion at the end of the section.
    🔗
    🔗
    Figure 10.5 shows the direction the population moves from each state in the triangle of possible population states. The arrows circle around the interior equilibrium \(\sigma^* = \frac{1}{3}O + \frac{1}{3}Y + \frac{1}{3}B\text{.}\) The book shows that the population cycles forever. It goes from mostly orange-throats, to mostly yellow-stripes, to mostly blue-throats, and back again! This behavior is observed in real lizard populations.
    🔗
    This is a new kind of behavior. With two pure strategies, every population tends to an equilibrium. With three, a population might never settle down at all. Here \(\sigma^*\) is a Nash equilibrium but not an evolutionarily stable state, and studying the dynamics shows what the population actually does.
