    These are the answers to the questions, not fully worked solutions. Enough is shown that you can tell whether your own work got to the correct conclusion.

    1. Subgame Perfect Equilibria Practice.

    (a) The normal form.

    Write the game in normal form.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 2: \(xx\) Player 2: \(xy\) Player 2: \(yx\) Player 2: \(yy\)
    Player 1: \(U\) \((2, 3)\) \((2, 3)\) \((0, 1)\) \((0, 1)\)
    Player 1: \(D\) \((1, 0)\) \((3, 2)\) \((1, 0)\) \((3, 2)\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) \((U, xx)\) is a Nash equilibrium.

    Show that \((U, xx)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Against \(xx\text{,}\) \(U\) is Player 1’s best response (2 vs. 1). Against \(U\text{,}\) \(xx\) is Player 2’s best response (3, the most she can get).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Checking each subgame.

    Decide whether \((U, xx)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Whole game: a Nash equilibrium, by part (b).
    🔗
    Player 2: \(xx\) Player 2: \(xy\) Player 2: \(yx\) Player 2: \(yy\)
    Player 1: \(U\) \(\boxed{(2, 3)}\) \((2, 3)\) \((0, 1)\) \((0, 1)\)
    Player 1: \(D\) \((1, 0)\) \((3, 2)\) \((1, 0)\) \((3, 2)\)
    Subgame at \(n_1\text{:}\) \(xx\) restricts to \(x\text{.}\) A Nash equilibrium, since \(3 \gt 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Subgame at \(n_1\) Player 2: \(x\) Player 2: \(y\)
    Payoffs \(\boxed{(2, 3)}\) \((0, 1)\)
    Subgame at \(n_2\text{:}\) \(xx\) restricts to \(x\text{.}\) Not a Nash equilibrium, since \(y\) gives Player 2 a payoff of 2, more than 0.
    🔗
    Subgame at \(n_2\) Player 2: \(x\) Player 2: \(y\)
    Payoffs \(\boxed{(1, 0)}\) \((3, 2)\)
    So \((U, xx)\) is not a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) Backward induction and the threat.

    Find the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium, compare it with \((U, xx)\text{,}\) and explain the threat.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The subgame perfect Nash equilibrium is \((D, xy)\text{,}\) with outcome \((3, 2)\text{.}\) The outcome of \((U, xx)\) is \((2, 3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Playing \(x\) at \(n_2\) is a threat because it hurts Player 1 (1 instead of 3) and also hurts Player 2 (0 instead of 2). Player 2 benefits: it gets Player 1 to choose \(U\text{,}\) so Player 2 gets 3 instead of 2. It only works if Player 1 believes it: if Player 1 chooses \(D\text{,}\) Player 2’s best move at \(n_2\) is \(y\text{,}\) which gives Player 1 a payoff of 3.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Trigger and Tit for Tat.

    (a) The trigger strategy in words.

    Explain in words what it means for Player 1 to use \(\sigma\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 1 starts out cooperating, and keeps cooperating as long as Player 2 cooperates. As soon as Player 2 defects once, Player 1 defects in every round from then on, no matter what Player 2 does after that.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) \(\pi_1(\sigma, \tau)\) and \(\pi_2(\sigma, \tau)\).

    Find \(\pi_1(\sigma, \tau)\) and \(\pi_2(\sigma, \tau)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both players play \(c\) in every round, so \(\pi_1(\sigma, \tau) = \pi_2(\sigma, \tau) = \frac{5}{1 - \delta}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) \(\pi_1(\tau, \nu)\) and \(\pi_2(\tau, \nu)\).

    Find \(\pi_1(\tau, \nu)\) and \(\pi_2(\tau, \nu)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    round 0 round 1 round 2 round 3 round 4 \(\cdots\)
    Player 1’s move (\(\tau\)) \(c\) \(d\) \(c\) \(d\) \(c\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 2’s move (\(\nu\)) \(d\) \(c\) \(d\) \(c\) \(d\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 1’s payoff \(-8\) \(8\) \(-8\) \(8\) \(-8\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 2’s payoff \(8\) \(-8\) \(8\) \(-8\) \(8\) \(\cdots\)
    \(\pi_1(\tau, \nu) = \frac{-8}{1 + \delta}\) and \(\pi_2(\tau, \nu) = \frac{8}{1 + \delta}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) When \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a Nash equilibrium.

    Find \(\delta_0\) such that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a Nash equilibrium whenever \(\delta \geq \delta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\delta_0 = \frac{3}{8}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Tit for Tat Against Other Strategies.

    (a) \((\sigma, \sigma)\) compared with \((\tau, \tau)\).

    Compare the payoffs for \((\sigma, \sigma)\) and \((\tau, \tau)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    They are the same: both players play \(c\) in every round, so each gets \(\frac{5}{1 - \delta}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Switching from \(\tau\) to \(\nu\).

    Find \(\delta_0\) such that \(\pi_1(\nu, \tau) \leq \pi_1(\tau, \tau)\) whenever \(\delta \geq \delta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\pi_1(\nu, \tau) = \frac{8}{1 + \delta}\text{,}\) and \(\delta_0 = \frac{3}{13}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Switching from \(\tau\) to \(\beta\).

    Find \(\delta_0\) such that \(\pi_1(\beta, \tau) \leq \pi_1(\tau, \tau)\) whenever \(\delta \geq \delta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    round 0 round 1 round 2 round 3 round 4 \(\cdots\)
    Player 1’s move (\(\beta\)) \(d\) \(d\) \(d\) \(d\) \(d\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 2’s move (\(\tau\)) \(c\) \(d\) \(d\) \(d\) \(d\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 1’s payoff \(8\) \(0\) \(0\) \(0\) \(0\) \(\cdots\)
    \(\pi_1(\beta, \tau) = 8\text{,}\) and \(\delta_0 = \frac{3}{8}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Tit for Tat Is a Nash Equilibrium, but Not Subgame Perfect.

    (a) Payoffs during an excursion.

    Fill in Player 1’s payoff (with discount factor) in each round of an excursion.
    🔗
    Solution.
    round 0 \(\cdots\) round \(k-1\) round \(k\) round \(k+1\) \(\cdots\) round \(k+m-1\) round \(k+m\) round \(k+m+1\)
    Player 1’s move \(c\) \(d\) \(d\) \(\cdots\) \(d\) \(c\) \(c\)
    Player 2’s move (\(\tau\)) \(c\) \(c\) \(d\) \(\cdots\) \(d\) \(d\) \(c\)
    Player 1’s payoff (with discount factor) \(5, 5\delta, \ldots, 5\delta^{k-1}\) \(8\delta^k\) \(0\) \(\cdots\) \(0\) \(-8\delta^{k+m}\) \(5\delta^{k+m+1}\)
    Player 1’s payoff (with discount factor) if they had kept using \(\tau\) \(5, 5\delta, \ldots, 5\delta^{k-1}\) \(5\delta^k\) \(5\delta^{k+1}\) \(\cdots\) \(5\delta^{k+m-1}\) \(5\delta^{k+m}\) \(5\delta^{k+m+1}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) The excursion doesn’t help.

    Show that the excursion does not improve Player 1’s payoff in rounds \(k\) through \(k + m\) when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{8}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Canceling \(\delta^k\text{,}\) we need \(8 - 8\delta^m \leq 5(1 + \delta + \cdots + \delta^m)\text{.}\) When \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{8}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 5(1 + \delta + \cdots + \delta^m) \amp = \frac{5}{1 - \delta}\left(1 - \delta^{m+1}\right) \amp \amp \text{(finite geometric series)}\\ \amp \geq 8\left(1 - \delta^{m+1}\right) \amp \amp \left(\tfrac{5}{1 - \delta} \geq 8 \text{ since } \delta \geq \tfrac{3}{8}\right)\\ \amp \geq 8\left(1 - \delta^m\right) \amp \amp \left(\delta^{m+1} \leq \delta^m\right)\\ \amp = 8 - 8\delta^m. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Every way of switching away from \(\tau\).

    Explain why several excursions are worse than \(\tau\text{,}\) and which earlier part handles defecting forever.
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. After each excursion the game starts over, so each excursion can be compared with \(\tau\) on its own rounds. By part (b), each one gives Player 1 less than cooperating would, and every other round is the same as with \(\tau\text{.}\) So several excursions give Player 1 less than \(\tau\text{.}\)

    5. Contributing to a Common Fund.

    (a) The payoff function.

    Find the payoff function \(\pi_i(x_i, x_{-i})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \pi_i(x_i, x_{-i}) = 1 - x_i + \frac{3}{4}(x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Contributing nothing is strictly dominant.

    Show that \(x_i \gt 0\) is strictly dominated by \(x_i = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\pi_i = 1 - \frac{1}{4}x_i + \frac{3}{4}(\text{sum of the others' contributions})\text{,}\) which decreases as \(x_i\) increases, whatever the others contribute. So \(x_i = 0\) gives the largest payoff.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Three payoffs.

    Find Player \(i\)’s payoff in each of the three situations.
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Everyone contributes \(y\text{:}\) \(1 - y + \frac{3}{4}(4y) = 1 + 2y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Player \(i\) contributes 0 and the others contribute \(y\text{:}\) \(1 + \frac{3}{4}(3y) = 1 + \frac{9}{4}y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Everyone contributes 0: \(1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) When \((\sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y)\) is a Nash equilibrium.

    Find \(\delta_0\) such that \((\sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y)\) is a Nash equilibrium whenever \(\delta \geq \delta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Solve
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1 + 2y}{1 - \delta} \geq 1 + \frac{9}{4}y + \frac{\delta}{1 - \delta} \end{equation*}
    to get \(\delta_0 = \frac{1}{9}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e) Subgame perfection.

    Decide whether \((\sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium when \(\delta \geq \delta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Yes, when \(\delta \geq \frac{1}{9}\text{.}\) Everyone contributes \(y\text{,}\) so the subgame looks like the whole game with every payoff multiplied by \(\delta^k\text{,}\) and part (d) applies.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Yes. Everyone contributes 0 forever, and by part (b) contributing 0 is a best response in every round.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Yes, when \(\delta \geq \frac{1}{9}\text{,}\) since every subgame falls into one of the two cases.
      🔗
      🔗
