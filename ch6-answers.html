    Exercises 6.5 Answers to Ch. 6 Exercises

    Answers to the exercises of this chapter. Click an exercise to open it, click a part to open that part, then click Solution to reveal the answer.
    🔗
    These are the answers to the questions, not fully worked solutions. Enough is shown that you can tell whether your own work got to the correct conclusion.
    🔗

    1. Subgame Perfect Equilibria Practice.

    (a) The normal form.

    Write the game in normal form.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 2: \(xx\) Player 2: \(xy\) Player 2: \(yx\) Player 2: \(yy\)
    Player 1: \(U\) \((2, 3)\) \((2, 3)\) \((0, 1)\) \((0, 1)\)
    Player 1: \(D\) \((1, 0)\) \((3, 2)\) \((1, 0)\) \((3, 2)\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) \((U, xx)\) is a Nash equilibrium.

    Show that \((U, xx)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Against \(xx\text{,}\) Player 1 gets 2 from \(U\) and 1 from \(D\text{,}\) so \(U\) is a best response. Against \(U\text{,}\) Player 2 gets 3 from \(xx\text{,}\) which is the most she can get (\(xy\) also gives 3, and \(yx\) and \(yy\) give 1), so \(xx\) is a best response. So \((U, xx)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Checking each subgame.

    Decide whether \((U, xx)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    There are three subgames. In each table, the outcome of \((U, xx)\) restricted to the subgame is boxed.
    🔗
    The whole game. The restriction is \((U, xx)\) itself, which is a Nash equilibrium by part (b).
    🔗
    Player 2: \(xx\) Player 2: \(xy\) Player 2: \(yx\) Player 2: \(yy\)
    Player 1: \(U\) \(\boxed{(2, 3)}\) \((2, 3)\) \((0, 1)\) \((0, 1)\)
    Player 1: \(D\) \((1, 0)\) \((3, 2)\) \((1, 0)\) \((3, 2)\)
    The subgame at \(n_1\text{.}\) \(xx\) restricts to \(x\text{.}\) This is a Nash equilibrium, since \(x\) gives Player 2 a payoff of 3, and \(y\) gives only 1.
    🔗
    Subgame at \(n_1\) Player 2: \(x\) Player 2: \(y\)
    Payoffs \(\boxed{(2, 3)}\) \((0, 1)\)
    The subgame at \(n_2\text{.}\) \(xx\) restricts to \(x\text{.}\) This is not a Nash equilibrium, since \(x\) gives Player 2 a payoff of 0, but \(y\) would give her 2.
    🔗
    Subgame at \(n_2\) Player 2: \(x\) Player 2: \(y\)
    Payoffs \(\boxed{(1, 0)}\) \((3, 2)\)
    So \((U, xx)\) is not a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) Backward induction and the threat.

    Find the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium, compare it with \((U, xx)\text{,}\) and explain the threat.
    🔗
    Solution.
    At \(n_1\text{,}\) Player 2 plays \(x\) (3 is more than 1). At \(n_2\text{,}\) she plays \(y\) (2 is more than 0). Knowing this, Player 1 compares \(U\text{,}\) which gives \((2, 3)\text{,}\) with \(D\text{,}\) which gives \((3, 2)\text{,}\) and chooses \(D\text{.}\) So the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium is \((D, xy)\text{,}\) with outcome \((3, 2)\text{.}\) The outcome of \((U, xx)\) is \((2, 3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In \((U, xx)\text{,}\) Player 2’s plan to play \(x\) at \(n_2\) is a threat: it would hurt Player 1 (1 instead of 3), and it would also hurt Player 2 (0 instead of 2). Player 2 benefits from it: it convinces Player 1 to choose \(U\text{,}\) so Player 2 gets 3 instead of 2.
    🔗
    The threat only works if Player 1 believes it. If Player 1 chooses \(D\) anyway, Player 2’s best move at \(n_2\) is \(y\text{,}\) which gives Player 1 a payoff of 3. So a Player 1 who doesn’t believe the threat chooses \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Trigger and Tit for Tat.

    (a) The trigger strategy in words.

    Explain in words what it means for Player 1 to use \(\sigma\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 1 starts out cooperating, and keeps cooperating as long as Player 2 cooperates. As soon as Player 2 defects once, Player 1 defects in every round from then on, no matter what Player 2 does after that.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) \(\pi_1(\sigma, \tau)\) and \(\pi_2(\sigma, \tau)\).

    Find \(\pi_1(\sigma, \tau)\) and \(\pi_2(\sigma, \tau)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both strategies start with \(c\text{,}\) and each keeps playing \(c\) as long as the other does, so both players play \(c\) in every round and get 5 each round. So
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \pi_1(\sigma, \tau) = \pi_2(\sigma, \tau) = 5 + 5\delta + 5\delta^2 + \cdots = \frac{5}{1 - \delta}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) \(\pi_1(\tau, \nu)\) and \(\pi_2(\tau, \nu)\).

    Find \(\pi_1(\tau, \nu)\) and \(\pi_2(\tau, \nu)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    In round 0, Player 1 plays \(c\) and Player 2 plays \(d\text{.}\) After that, each player copies the other, so they take turns defecting:
    🔗
    round 0 round 1 round 2 round 3 round 4 \(\cdots\)
    Player 1’s move (\(\tau\)) \(c\) \(d\) \(c\) \(d\) \(c\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 2’s move (\(\nu\)) \(d\) \(c\) \(d\) \(c\) \(d\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 1’s payoff \(-8\) \(8\) \(-8\) \(8\) \(-8\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 2’s payoff \(8\) \(-8\) \(8\) \(-8\) \(8\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 1’s payoffs form a geometric series with \(a = -8\) and \(r = -\delta\text{,}\) and Player 2’s with \(a = 8\) and \(r = -\delta\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \pi_1(\tau, \nu) \amp = -8 + 8\delta - 8\delta^2 + \cdots = \frac{-8}{1 + \delta},\\ \pi_2(\tau, \nu) \amp = 8 - 8\delta + 8\delta^2 - \cdots = \frac{8}{1 + \delta}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) When \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a Nash equilibrium.

    Find \(\delta_0\) such that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a Nash equilibrium whenever \(\delta \geq \delta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\delta_0 = \frac{3}{8}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    With \((\sigma, \sigma)\text{,}\) Player 1 gets \(\frac{5}{1 - \delta}\text{.}\) If Player 1 first defects in round \(k\text{,}\) they get \(8\delta^k\) in round \(k\text{,}\) and after that Player 2 defects forever, so the best Player 1 can do is defect too and get 0 in every later round. From round \(k\) on, this gives \(8\delta^k\text{,}\) compared with \(\frac{5\delta^k}{1 - \delta}\) from cooperating. Canceling \(\delta^k\text{,}\) defecting doesn’t help when
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 8 \leq \frac{5}{1 - \delta}, \quad\text{that is,}\quad 8 - 8\delta \leq 5, \quad\text{so}\quad \delta \geq \frac{3}{8}. \end{equation*}
    By symmetry, the same is true for Player 2.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Tit for Tat Against Other Strategies.

    (a) \((\sigma, \sigma)\) compared with \((\tau, \tau)\).

    Compare the payoffs for \((\sigma, \sigma)\) and \((\tau, \tau)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    They are the same. In both profiles, both players play \(c\) in every round, so each player gets \(\frac{5}{1 - \delta}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Switching from \(\tau\) to \(\nu\).

    Find \(\delta_0\) such that \(\pi_1(\nu, \tau) \leq \pi_1(\tau, \tau)\) whenever \(\delta \geq \delta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\delta_0 = \frac{3}{13}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    With \((\nu, \tau)\text{,}\) Player 1 defects in round 0 and Player 2 cooperates, and then they take turns: Player 1’s payoffs are \(8, -8, 8, -8, \ldots\text{,}\) so \(\pi_1(\nu, \tau) = \frac{8}{1 + \delta}\text{.}\) We need
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{8}{1 + \delta} \leq \frac{5}{1 - \delta}, \quad\text{that is,}\quad 8 - 8\delta \leq 5 + 5\delta, \quad\text{so}\quad \delta \geq \frac{3}{13}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Switching from \(\tau\) to \(\beta\).

    Find \(\delta_0\) such that \(\pi_1(\beta, \tau) \leq \pi_1(\tau, \tau)\) whenever \(\delta \geq \delta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\delta_0 = \frac{3}{8}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    round 0 round 1 round 2 round 3 round 4 \(\cdots\)
    Player 1’s move (\(\beta\)) \(d\) \(d\) \(d\) \(d\) \(d\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 2’s move (\(\tau\)) \(c\) \(d\) \(d\) \(d\) \(d\) \(\cdots\)
    Player 1’s payoff \(8\) \(0\) \(0\) \(0\) \(0\) \(\cdots\)
    So \(\pi_1(\beta, \tau) = 8\text{.}\) We need
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 8 \leq \frac{5}{1 - \delta}, \quad\text{that is,}\quad 8 - 8\delta \leq 5, \quad\text{so}\quad \delta \geq \frac{3}{8}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Tit for Tat Is a Nash Equilibrium, but Not Subgame Perfect.

    (a) Payoffs during an excursion.

    Fill in Player 1’s payoff (with discount factor) in each round of an excursion.
    🔗
    Solution.
    round 0 \(\cdots\) round \(k-1\) round \(k\) round \(k+1\) \(\cdots\) round \(k+m-1\) round \(k+m\) round \(k+m+1\)
    Player 1’s move \(c\) \(d\) \(d\) \(\cdots\) \(d\) \(c\) \(c\)
    Player 2’s move (\(\tau\)) \(c\) \(c\) \(d\) \(\cdots\) \(d\) \(d\) \(c\)
    Player 1’s payoff (with discount factor) \(5, 5\delta, \ldots, 5\delta^{k-1}\) \(8\delta^k\) \(0\) \(\cdots\) \(0\) \(-8\delta^{k+m}\) \(5\delta^{k+m+1}\)
    Player 1’s payoff (with discount factor) if they had kept using \(\tau\) \(5, 5\delta, \ldots, 5\delta^{k-1}\) \(5\delta^k\) \(5\delta^{k+1}\) \(\cdots\) \(5\delta^{k+m-1}\) \(5\delta^{k+m}\) \(5\delta^{k+m+1}\)
    In round \(k\text{,}\) Player 1 defects while Player 2 still cooperates, for 8. In rounds \(k+1\) through \(k+m-1\text{,}\) both defect, for 0. In round \(k+m\text{,}\) Player 1 cooperates while Player 2 defects, for \(-8\text{.}\) In round \(k+m+1\text{,}\) both cooperate again, for 5.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) The excursion doesn’t help.

    Show that the excursion does not improve Player 1’s payoff in rounds \(k\) through \(k + m\) when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{8}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    From the table, we need \(8\delta^k - 8\delta^{k+m} \leq 5\delta^k + 5\delta^{k+1} + \cdots + 5\delta^{k+m}\text{.}\) Canceling \(\delta^k\text{,}\) this is \(8 - 8\delta^m \leq 5(1 + \delta + \cdots + \delta^m)\text{.}\) When \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{8}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 5(1 + \delta + \cdots + \delta^m) \amp = \frac{5}{1 - \delta}\left(1 - \delta^{m+1}\right) \amp \amp \text{(finite geometric series)}\\ \amp \geq 8\left(1 - \delta^{m+1}\right) \amp \amp \left(\tfrac{5}{1 - \delta} \geq 8 \text{ since } \delta \geq \tfrac{3}{8}\right)\\ \amp \geq 8\left(1 - \delta^m\right) \amp \amp \left(\delta^{m+1} \leq \delta^m\right)\\ \amp = 8 - 8\delta^m. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Every way of switching away from \(\tau\).

    Explain why several excursions are worse than \(\tau\text{,}\) and which earlier part handles defecting forever.
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Since the game starts over after each excursion (with payoffs multiplied by a power of \(\delta\)), each excursion can be compared with \(\tau\) on its own rounds. By part (b), each excursion gives Player 1 less than cooperating would in those rounds. (It is strictly less: in the last step, \(\delta^{m+1} \lt \delta^m\text{.}\)) In all the other rounds, Player 1 gets the same as with \(\tau\text{.}\) So adding up, several excursions give Player 1 less than \(\tau\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Task 6.4.3.c: from round \(k\) on, defecting forever is \(\beta\) against \(\tau\) with every payoff multiplied by \(\delta^k\text{,}\) and that part showed \(\beta\) does no better than \(\tau\) when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{8}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) \((\tau, \tau)\) is not subgame perfect.

    In the subgame after \((c, d)\) in round 0, compare \(\tau\) and \(\zeta\) for Player 1.
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. With \(\tau\text{,}\) the players take turns defecting:
      🔗
      round 1
      (after \((c, d)\))      		 round 2 round 3 round 4 \(\cdots\)
      Player 1’s move (\(\tau\)) \(d\) \(c\) \(d\) \(c\) \(\cdots\)
      Player 2’s move (\(\tau\)) \(c\) \(d\) \(c\) \(d\) \(\cdots\)
      Player 1’s payoff (with discount factor) \(8\delta\) \(-8\delta^2\) \(8\delta^3\) \(-8\delta^4\) \(\cdots\)
      Player 1’s payoff is a geometric series with \(a = 8\delta\) and \(r = -\delta\text{:}\) \(8\delta - 8\delta^2 + 8\delta^3 - \cdots = \frac{8\delta}{1 + \delta}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. With \(\zeta\text{,}\) both players cooperate in every round of the subgame:
      🔗
      round 1
      (after \((c, d)\))      		 round 2 round 3 round 4 \(\cdots\)
      Player 1’s move (\(\zeta\)) \(c\) \(c\) \(c\) \(c\) \(\cdots\)
      Player 2’s move (\(\tau\)) \(c\) \(c\) \(c\) \(c\) \(\cdots\)
      Player 1’s payoff (with discount factor) \(5\delta\) \(5\delta^2\) \(5\delta^3\) \(5\delta^4\) \(\cdots\)
      Player 1’s payoff is \(5\delta + 5\delta^2 + \cdots = \frac{5\delta}{1 - \delta}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\frac{5\delta}{1 - \delta} \gt \frac{8\delta}{1 + \delta}\) exactly when \(5(1 + \delta) \gt 8(1 - \delta)\text{,}\) that is, when \(\delta \gt \frac{3}{13}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. No. For \(\delta \gt \frac{3}{13}\text{,}\) Player 1 does better in this subgame by switching from \(\tau\) to \(\zeta\text{,}\) so the restriction of \((\tau, \tau)\) is not a Nash equilibrium. So \((\tau, \tau)\) is not subgame perfect. In particular, this holds for every \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{8}\text{,}\) where \((\tau, \tau)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Contributing to a Common Fund.

    (a) The payoff function.

    Find the payoff function \(\pi_i(x_i, x_{-i})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \pi_i(x_i, x_{-i}) = 1 - x_i + \frac{3}{4}(x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Contributing nothing is strictly dominant.

    Show that \(x_i \gt 0\) is strictly dominated by \(x_i = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Collecting the \(x_i\) terms, \(\pi_i = 1 - \frac{1}{4}x_i + \frac{3}{4}(\text{sum of the others' contributions})\text{.}\) Each dollar Player \(i\) contributes costs them 1 and returns only \(\frac{3}{4}\) to them. So for any contributions by the others, \(\pi_i(0, x_{-i}) - \pi_i(x_i, x_{-i}) = \frac{1}{4}x_i \gt 0\) whenever \(x_i \gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Three payoffs.

    Find Player \(i\)’s payoff in each of the three situations.
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Everyone contributes \(y\text{:}\) \(1 - y + \frac{3}{4}(4y) = 1 + 2y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Player \(i\) contributes 0 and the others contribute \(y\text{:}\) \(1 + \frac{3}{4}(3y) = 1 + \frac{9}{4}y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Everyone contributes 0: \(1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) When \((\sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y)\) is a Nash equilibrium.

    Find \(\delta_0\) such that \((\sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y)\) is a Nash equilibrium whenever \(\delta \geq \delta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\delta_0 = \frac{1}{9}\text{,}\) for every \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If everyone uses \(\sigma_y\text{,}\) Player \(i\) gets \(1 + 2y\) every round, for \(\frac{1 + 2y}{1 - \delta}\text{.}\) If Player \(i\) contributes 0 in round 0, they get \(1 + \frac{9}{4}y\) in round 0, and then everyone else contributes 0 forever, so the best Player \(i\) can do is contribute 0 too and get 1 in every later round, for \(1 + \frac{9}{4}y + \frac{\delta}{1 - \delta}\text{.}\) We need
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1 + 2y}{1 - \delta} \geq 1 + \frac{9}{4}y + \frac{\delta}{1 - \delta}. \end{equation*}
    Multiplying by \(1 - \delta\) gives \(1 + 2y \geq \left(1 + \frac{9}{4}y\right)(1 - \delta) + \delta = 1 + \frac{9}{4}y(1 - \delta)\text{,}\) so \(2y \geq \frac{9}{4}y(1 - \delta)\text{.}\) Dividing by \(y\) gives \(8 \geq 9(1 - \delta)\text{,}\) so \(\delta \geq \frac{1}{9}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e) Subgame perfection.

    Decide whether \((\sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium when \(\delta \geq \delta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Yes. Nobody has contributed less than \(y\text{,}\) so \(\sigma_y\) tells everyone to contribute \(y\) at \(m\text{,}\) just as at the start of the game. The subgame looks exactly like the whole game, with every payoff multiplied by \(\delta^k\text{,}\) so by part (d) the restriction is a Nash equilibrium when \(\delta \geq \frac{1}{9}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Yes. Someone has contributed less than \(y\text{,}\) so \(\sigma_y\) tells everyone to contribute 0 in every round of the subgame. By part (b), contributing 0 is strictly dominant in every round, and the other players contribute 0 forever no matter what Player \(i\) does, so contributing 0 every round is a best response. So the restriction is a Nash equilibrium.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Yes. Every subgame falls into one of the two cases, and in both the restriction is a Nash equilibrium. So \((\sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y, \sigma_y)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium when \(\delta \geq \frac{1}{9}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
