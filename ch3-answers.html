    Exercises 3.6 Answers to Ch. 3 Exercises

    Answers to the exercises of this chapter. Click an exercise to open it, click a part to open that part, then click Solution to reveal the answer.
    🔗
    These are the answers to the questions, not fully worked solutions. Enough is shown that you can tell whether your own work got to the correct conclusion.
    🔗

    1. The Nash Equilibrium Definition.

    (a) Nash equilibrium, in notation.

    State mathematically what it means for \(s^*\) to be a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For each \(i = 1, \ldots, N\) and every \(s_i \in S_i\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \pi_i(s^*) \geq \pi_i(s_i, s^*_{-i}). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Not a Nash equilibrium.

    State mathematically what it means for \(s^*\) to not be a Nash equilibrium, and explain it in words.
    🔗
    Solution.
    There is some player \(i\) and some \(s_i \in S_i\) with
    \begin{equation*} \pi_i(s_i, s^*_{-i}) \gt \pi_i(s^*). \end{equation*}
    In words: at least one player can strictly improve by changing strategy alone. One such player and one such strategy are enough.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Three profiles in a \(3 \times 3\) game.

    Determine whether \((a, x)\text{,}\) \((b, y)\text{,}\) and \((c, z)\) are Nash equilibria, and whether each equilibrium is strict.
    🔗
    Solution.
    • \((a, x)\) is a strict Nash equilibrium. If player 1 switches, their payoff drops from 4 to 2 or 1. If player 2 switches, their payoff drops from 3 to 1 or 2.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \((b, y)\) is not a Nash equilibrium. Player 1 can switch to \(c\) and get 6 instead of 5. (Player 2 can also improve, by switching to \(z\) for 6, but one player is enough.)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \((c, z)\) is a Nash equilibrium, but not strict. Neither player can do better by switching, but player 2 gets 3 at either \(x\) or \(z\text{,}\) so switching to \(x\) does not make them strictly worse off.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Water Pollution with Five Firms.

    (a) Each number of polluters.

    For each \(k = 0, \ldots, 5\text{,}\) determine whether profiles with exactly \(k\) polluters are Nash equilibria.
    🔗
    Solution.
    • \(k = 0\) or \(1\text{:}\) Not a Nash equilibrium. A purifier can switch to polluting, and the lake stays clean, so their payoff rises from \(-1\) to \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(k = 2\text{:}\) A Nash equilibrium. A polluter who switches to purifying drops from \(0\) to \(-1\text{.}\) A purifier who switches to polluting fouls the lake and drops from \(-1\) to \(-3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(k = 3\text{:}\) Not a Nash equilibrium. A polluter can switch to purifying, which cleans the lake, so their payoff rises from \(-3\) to \(-1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(k = 4\text{:}\) Not a Nash equilibrium. The purifier can switch to polluting. The lake stays fouled either way, so their payoff rises from \(-4\) to \(-3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(k = 5\text{:}\) A Nash equilibrium. A firm that switches to purifying leaves 4 polluters, so the lake stays fouled and its payoff drops from \(-3\) to \(-4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) All Nash equilibria.

    Describe all Nash equilibria and count them.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Every profile in which exactly 2 firms pollute, plus the profile in which all 5 pollute. That is \(\binom{5}{2} + 1 = 11\) Nash equilibria.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Locating Two Banks, Revisited.

    (a) \((50, 50)\) is a Nash equilibrium.

    Show directly that \((50, 50)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    At \((50, 50)\) each bank has \(4950\text{.}\) If \(A\) moves to any \(p \neq 50\text{,}\) \(A\) gets at most \(4900\) (achieved at \(p = 49\) or \(p = 51\)). So \(\pi_A(p, 50) \leq 4900 \lt 4950\text{,}\) and by symmetry the same holds for \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Profiles with \(p \lt q\).

    Show that no profile \((p, q)\) with \(p \lt q\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    • \(q \geq p + 2\text{:}\) Bank \(A\) can move to \(p + 1\text{.}\) It is still to the left of bank \(B\text{,}\) and it gains 50 customers.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(q = p + 1\text{:}\) Bank \(A\) has \(100p\) customers and bank \(B\) has \(100(99 - p)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      • If \(p \leq 49\text{,}\) bank \(A\) has at most \(4900\text{.}\) It can move onto block \(q\) and get \(4950\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • If \(p \geq 50\text{,}\) bank \(B\) has at most \(4900\text{.}\) It can move onto block \(p\) and get \(4950\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Profiles with \(p = q \neq 50\).

    Show that no profile \((p, p)\) with \(p \neq 50\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Each bank has \(4950\text{.}\) If \(p \lt 50\text{,}\) \(A\) moves to \(p + 1\) and gets blocks \(p+1\) through \(99\text{,}\) for \(100(99 - p) \geq 5000\text{.}\) If \(p \gt 50\text{,}\) \(A\) moves to \(p - 1\) and gets \(100(p - 1) \geq 5000\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Bidding the Resource.

    (a) \((1, 1)\).

    Show that \((1, 1)\) is a Nash equilibrium, and decide whether it is strict.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\pi_1(1, 1) = 0\text{.}\) Any \(s_1 \lt 1\) gives \(s_1 \lt s_2\text{,}\) so \(\pi_1(s_1, 1) = 0 \leq 0\text{.}\) The same holds for player 2 by symmetry, so \((1, 1)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    It is not strict: deviations give exactly \(0\text{,}\) not strictly less.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) \(s_2 \lt s_1 \lt 1\).

    Show that player 2 can improve.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 2 currently gets \(0\text{.}\) Switching to any \(s_2'\) with \(s_1 \lt s_2' \leq 1\) makes player 2 the winner, with payoff \((1 - s_1) + (1 - s_2') \geq 1 - s_1 \gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) \(s_2 \lt s_1 = 1\).

    Explain why player 2’s deviation no longer works, and show the profile is still not a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 2 cannot bid more than 1, and tying at \(1\) gives player 2 a payoff of \(1 - 1 = 0\text{,}\) which is no improvement.
    🔗
    Player 1 improves instead: currently \(\pi_1 = 1 - s_2\text{.}\) Switching to any \(s_1'\) with \(s_2 \lt s_1' \lt 1\) keeps player 1 the winner, with payoff \((1 - s_1') + (1 - s_2) \gt 1 - s_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) \(s_1 = s_2 \lt 1\).

    Show there are no Nash equilibria with \(s_1 = s_2 \lt 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Write \(s_1 = s_2 = s\text{,}\) so player 1 gets \(1 - s\text{.}\) Switching to any \(s_1'\) with \(s \lt s_1' \lt 1\) gives player 1 \((1 - s_1') + (1 - s) \gt 1 - s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Hawk and Dove, Revisited.

    (a) Dominated strategies.

    Identify any dominated strategies when \(0 \lt v \lt w\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Neither strategy is dominated. If the other player plays \(h\text{,}\) then \(d\) is better, since \(0 \gt \frac{v-w}{2}\text{.}\) If the other player plays \(d\text{,}\) then \(h\) is better, since \(v \gt \frac{v}{2}\text{.}\) Each strategy does better against something, so neither one dominates the other, weakly or strictly.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) All Nash equilibria.

    Find all Nash equilibria when \(0 \lt v \lt w\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The only Nash equilibria are \((h, d)\) and \((d, h)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Explaining in words.

    Explain informally why neither animal wants to switch.
    🔗
    Solution.
    At \((h, d)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    • The hawk gets the whole territory without a fight. Switching to dove would mean only getting to share it.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The dove retreats and avoids injury. Switching to hawk would start a fight, and the risk of injury costs more than the territory is worth.
      🔗
      🔗
    \((d, h)\) is the same argument with the players swapped.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

      🔗
      🔗
    • \((B, B, B)\) with payoffs \((30, 25, 30)\)
      🔗

    These are the only cells with all three payoffs boxed.

    7. Pick a Number.

    (a) Everyone choosing \(k\).

    Show that every player choosing \(k\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The average is \(k\text{,}\) so the target is \(\frac{3k}{4}\text{.}\) Every player is the same distance from it, so all tie and \(\pi_i(k, \bar{k}) = 4\text{.}\) Since 4 is the largest possible payoff, \(\pi_i(s_i, \bar{k}) \leq 4 = \pi_i(k, \bar{k})\) for every \(s_i\text{,}\) so this is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) When it is strict.

    For which \(k\) is the equilibrium strict?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The equilibrium is strict only when \(k = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(k = 1\text{:}\) The target is \(\frac{3}{4}\text{.}\) Every other choice is larger, so it is farther from the target, and the player’s payoff drops from 4 to 0. So the equilibrium is strict.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(k = 2\text{:}\) The target is \(\frac{3}{2}\text{.}\) Choosing 1 is just as close to the target as 2, so the player still ties and gets 4. So the equilibrium is not strict.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(k \geq 3\text{:}\) The integer closest to the target \(\frac{3k}{4}\) is not \(k\text{.}\) A player who switches to it wins outright and still gets 4. So the equilibrium is not strict.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. A First-Price Auction.

    (a) The Nash equilibrium.

    Show that \(b_1 = v_2\) and \(b_i = v_i\) for \(i \geq 2\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We need to check that no player can improve their payoff by switching their bid on their own.
    🔗
    • Player 1: Since \(h_1 = v_2\text{,}\) player 1 ties player 2’s bid and wins because of the lower index. Player 1’s payoff is \(v_1 - v_2 \geq 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      • Bidding higher: Player 1 still wins, but pays more, so the payoff goes down.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Bidding lower: Player 1 loses the auction, and the payoff becomes \(0\text{,}\) which is no better than \(v_1 - v_2\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
    • Player \(i \geq 2\text{:}\) Player \(i\) loses the auction, so their payoff is \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      • Bidding lower: Player \(i\) still loses, and the payoff stays \(0\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Bidding higher: Player 1 bids \(v_2\) and wins ties, so to win, player \(i\) must bid \(b_i \gt v_2\text{.}\) Then the payoff is \(v_i - b_i \lt v_i - v_2 \leq 0\text{,}\) which is worse than \(0\text{.}\) A higher bid that does not win still pays \(0\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
    No player can improve, so this is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Bidding above your value.

    Show \(b_i \gt v_i\) is weakly dominated by \(b_i' = v_i\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We need to check both conditions in the definition of a weakly dominated strategy.
    🔗
    • Never worse: If player \(i\) bids \(v_i\text{,}\) the payoff is always \(0\text{,}\) because winning pays \(v_i - v_i = 0\) and losing pays \(0\text{.}\) If player \(i\) bids \(b_i \gt v_i\text{,}\) the payoff is either \(v_i - b_i \lt 0\) (a win) or \(0\) (a loss). So \(\pi_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \geq \pi_i(b_i, b_{-i})\) for every \(b_{-i}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Sometimes strictly better: Suppose every other player bids \(0\text{.}\) Then the bid \(b_i\) wins, and player \(i\) gets \(v_i - b_i \lt 0\text{,}\) while bidding \(v_i\) gives \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Bidding exactly your value.

    Show \(b_i = v_i\) is weakly dominated by any \(0 \lt b_i' \lt v_i\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We need to check both conditions in the definition of a weakly dominated strategy.
    🔗
    • Never worse: If player \(i\) bids \(v_i\text{,}\) the payoff is always \(0\text{.}\) If player \(i\) bids \(b_i'\) with \(0 \lt b_i' \lt v_i\text{,}\) the payoff is either \(v_i - b_i' \gt 0\) (a win) or \(0\) (a loss). So \(\pi_i(b_i', b_{-i}) \geq \pi_i(v_i, b_{-i})\) for every \(b_{-i}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Sometimes strictly better: Suppose every other player bids \(0\text{.}\) Then the bid \(b_i'\) wins, and player \(i\) gets \(v_i - b_i' \gt 0\text{,}\) while bidding \(v_i\) gives \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
