    Section 1.6 Continuous Games and Stackelberg’s Duopoly, Part 2

    We’re going to continue with the same example from the previous page, but we’ll make the model more realistic by refusing to let production and price be negative. This is covered in Subsection 1.10.2, and it means we’ll have to consider boundary cases when looking for a maximum.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.2, The Second Model.

    Read the rest of Section 1.10 (that is, Subsection 1.10.2).
    🔗
    The first model let production levels and prices go negative, which isn’t something that happens in an actual market. This subsection fixes that. As you read, keep track of how the fix makes things more complicated mathematically.
    🔗
    There’s a lot in common with Part 1, but we have to address some nuanced points now that the model carries further restrictions. You can move through those quickly on a first reading; we’ll go over them more carefully in the notes below.
    🔗
    🔗
    The general procedure is the same as in Part 1, but some of the reasoning is hard to follow in the book, so we’ll break it down further here. Start with the two piecewise profit functions. Here is Firm 1’s, labeled:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \pi_1(s,t) = \begin{cases} \overbrace{\bigl[20 - 2(s+t)\bigr]s - 4s}^{\substack{\text{exactly the payoff from the first model:} \\ \text{price is still } 20 - 2(s+t) \text{ in this range}}} \amp \text{if } 0 \leq s+t \lt 10, \\[4ex] \underbrace{-4s}_{\substack{\text{price has bottomed out at } 0, \text{ so revenue is } 0 \cdot s = 0; \\ \text{profit is } 0 - 4s, \text{ a pure loss of the production cost}}} \amp \text{if } s+t \geq 10. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    Firm 2’s is the mirror image, with \(t\) in the role of \(s\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \pi_2(s,t) = \begin{cases} \bigl[20 - 2(s+t)\bigr]t - 4t \amp \text{if } 0 \leq s+t \lt 10, \\[1ex] -4t \amp \text{if } s+t \geq 10. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    The book brushes over some of the reasoning, so let’s take a closer look at Firm 2’s payoff function. Drag the slider below. The shaded region marks the production levels that actually earn Firm 2 a profit. Push \(s\) upward and watch what happens to it.
    🔗
    Notice what happens when you compare \(s \lt 8\) to \(s \geq 8\text{:}\)
    🔗
    • When \(s \lt 8\text{:}\) the shaded region is there. Firm 2 earns a positive profit for any \(t\) with \(0 \lt t \lt 8 - s\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • When \(s \geq 8\text{:}\) the shaded region is gone. There is no production level left that earns Firm 2 a profit.
      🔗
      🔗
    The book gives a brief explanation of why this happens, which we’ll examine in a moment. For now, see if you can use the observation above to determine Firm 2’s best response in the following scenarios.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.6.2.

    Firm 1 produces \(s = 9\text{.}\) What is Firm 2’s best response?
    🔗
    • \(t = 0\text{.}\) Since \(9 \geq 8\text{,}\) Firm 2’s profit on each unit is \(16 - 18 - 2t = -2 - 2t\text{,}\) negative for every \(t\text{.}\) Every unit loses money, so producing nothing and earning \(0\) is the best available outcome.
    • Correct. Firm 2 isn’t producing nothing as a strategic move, it’s producing nothing because every alternative loses money.
    • \(t = 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}(9) = -0.5\text{,}\) from the best-response formula.
    • This applies the \(s \lt 8\) formula outside the range where it’s valid, and negative production is exactly what the second model rules out. It is, though, a good way to see why the second case exists: when the parabola’s peak lands at a negative \(t\text{,}\) the best allowed choice is the nearest point of the domain, \(t = 0\text{.}\)
    • \(t = 4\text{,}\) the largest value the best-response function takes.
    • \(t = 4\) is \(b(0)\text{,}\) Firm 2’s best response when Firm 1 produces nothing. That’s the opposite end of the range from \(s = 9\text{.}\) Check which case \(s = 9\) falls into before picking a value of \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.6.3.

    Now Firm 1 produces \(s = 7\text{.}\) What is Firm 2’s best response?
    🔗
    • \(t = 0.5\text{,}\) since \(7 \lt 8\) puts us in the first case, where \(b(s) = 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s = 4 - 3.5\text{.}\)
    • Correct. The profitable window here is narrow, running only from \(t = 0\) to \(t = 8 - 7 = 1\text{,}\) but it isn’t empty, and Firm 2’s best response sits right in the middle of it. Set the slider to \(7\) and compare.
    • \(t = 0\text{,}\) since \(s = 7\) is close enough to \(8\) that the price is already at Firm 2’s cost.
    • Close isn’t the same as reaching it. At \(s = 7\) with Firm 2 producing nothing, the price is \(20 - 14 = 6\text{,}\) still above the cost of \(4\text{.}\) There’s a small profit to be had, and Firm 2 takes it. The corner solution only kicks in at \(s = 8\) exactly.
    • \(t = 1\text{,}\) the right-hand edge of the profitable region.
    • \(t = 8 - s = 1\) is where profit falls back to zero, not where it’s largest. Producing there earns Firm 2 nothing at all. The maximum is at the peak of the parabola, halfway between the two places profit equals zero.
    🔗
    Now let’s justify the two cases mathematically, since the book explains this only briefly. Look at \(\pi_2\) for \(0 \leq t \lt 10 - s\text{,}\) and factor the \(t\) out of it instead of multiplying everything through:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \pi_2(s,t) \;=\; \underbrace{\bigl[20 - 2(s+t)\bigr]}_{\text{price } p} t \;-\; \underbrace{4}_{\substack{\text{unit} \\ \text{cost}}} t \;=\; \underbrace{\bigl[\,20 - 2(s+t) - 4\,\bigr]}_{\text{profit on each unit sold}} \cdot \underbrace{t}_{\substack{\text{units} \\ \text{sold}}}. \end{equation*}
    Written this way, Firm 2’s profit is just profit per unit times number of units. Now suppose \(s \geq 8\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{20 - 2(s+t) - 4}_{\text{profit on each unit}} \;=\; 16 - 2s - 2t \;\leq\; 16 - 16 - 2t \;=\; -2t \;\leq\; 0. \end{equation*}
    So Firm 2 loses money on every unit it makes, and producing more only pushes the price further below cost, making the per-unit loss worse. The best Firm 2 can do is produce nothing and break even at \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In contrast, when \(s \lt 8\), the price stays above \(4\) and Firm 2 does have room to profit. Multiplying out the top piece and substituting the fixed \(s\) gives the function the book graphs in Figure 1.11:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \pi_2(s,t) = \begin{cases} (16 - 2s)t - 2t^2 \amp \text{if } 0 \leq t \lt 10 - s, \\[1ex] -4t \amp \text{if } t \geq 10 - s. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    That top piece is exactly the \(\pi_2\) from the first model, and it’s an upside-down parabola, so the derivative test applies to it just as it did there. The maximum sits at \(t = 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Putting the two cases together gives Firm 2’s best-response function:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} b(s) = \begin{cases} \overbrace{4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s}^{\substack{\text{the peak of the parabola: the same best} \\ \text{response as in the first model}}} \amp \text{if } 0 \leq s \lt 8, \\[5ex] \underbrace{0}_{\substack{\text{no profitable production level exists, since every } t \gt 0 \\ \text{loses money, so Firm 2 produces nothing and breaks even}}} \amp \text{if } s \geq 8. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    That second case is a corner solution: the best choice sits at the edge of the domain rather than where a derivative vanishes. It’s the first one in this chapter.
    🔗
    Now that we know Firm 2’s best-response function \(b(s)\text{,}\) we proceed as before by maximizing Firm 1’s payoff function \(\pi_1(s, b(s))\text{.}\) This time that means dealing with piecewise functions, since \(\pi_1\) and \(b(s)\) are both piecewise. Before doing any computation, though, we can narrow down where the maximum has to live.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.6.4.

    Without computing anything, can you tell which case the optimal \(s^*\) falls into: \(0 \leq s \lt 8\text{,}\) or \(s \geq 8\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(0 \leq s^* \lt 8\text{.}\) If Firm 1 produced \(s \geq 8\) it would drive the price down to its own cost of \(4\) or below, so it would earn nothing or lose money. Since Firm 1 can earn a positive profit with a smaller \(s\text{,}\) the maximum can’t be out there.
    • Correct. The threshold \(s = 8\) was defined by the price hitting \(4\text{,}\) and both firms have that same unit cost, so what kills Firm 2’s profit at \(s \geq 8\) kills Firm 1’s too. This is why the book says it won’t bother computing \(\pi_1(s, b(s))\) for \(s \geq 8\text{.}\)
    • \(s^* \geq 8\text{,}\) since that drives Firm 2 out of the market entirely and leaves Firm 1 as the only producer.
    • It’s true that Firm 2 produces nothing when \(s \geq 8\text{,}\) but look at what it costs Firm 1 to get there. Being the only seller is worthless if the price you had to create is at or below your own cost of \(4\text{.}\) Firm 1 wants profit, not market share.
    • \(s^* \geq 8\text{,}\) because profit is price times quantity, so producing more units always earns more.
    • Profit is margin times quantity, not price times quantity, and the margin shrinks as production rises. Past \(s = 8\) the margin is zero or negative, so extra units make things worse rather than better.
    • There’s no way to tell in advance. Both pieces have to be maximized and the two results compared.
    • That’s always a safe fallback, but here one piece can be ruled out by an argument rather than a computation: at \(s \geq 8\) the price is at or below Firm 1’s own cost of \(4\text{,}\) so its profit there can’t be positive.
    🔗
    So we only need to work on \(0 \leq s \lt 8\text{.}\) There’s still one thing to check before writing down a formula: \(\pi_1\) is itself piecewise, split at \(s + t = 10\text{,}\) so we need to know which piece we land on once \(t\) is replaced by \(b(s)\text{.}\) For \(0 \leq s \lt 8\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} s + b(s) \;=\; s + 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s \;=\; 4 + \tfrac{1}{2}s \;\lt\; 4 + \tfrac{1}{2}(8) \;=\; 8 \;\lt\; 10. \end{equation*}

    Checkpoint 1.6.5.

    What does the computation above tell us?
    🔗
    • Total production \(s + b(s)\) never reaches \(10\) on this range, so \(\pi_1\) always stays on its first piece. We can use \(\bigl[20 - 2(s+t)\bigr]s - 4s\) and get \(\pi_1(s, b(s)) = 8s - s^2\text{,}\) exactly as in the first model.
    • Correct, and this is the payoff for pinning down Firm 2’s best response first. Once \(t = b(s)\text{,}\) total production is capped at \(8\text{,}\) so the second piece of \(\pi_1\) is unreachable and the piecewise complication never actually bites.
    • The price stays positive, but we still have to maximize the second piece of \(\pi_1\) separately and compare.
    • The second piece applies only when \(s + t \geq 10\text{,}\) and the computation shows \(s + b(s) \lt 8\) for every \(s\) in this range. There’s no \(s\) left for that piece to describe, so there’s nothing to compare against.
    • Firm 2 always produces less than Firm 1.
    • That happens to be false at small \(s\) (at \(s = 0\text{,}\) Firm 2 produces \(4\)), and in any case it isn’t what the computation is about. The quantity being bounded is the total \(s + b(s)\text{,}\) which is what decides which piece of \(\pi_1\) is in force.
    • It tells us directly that \(s^* = 4\text{.}\)
    • Not yet. This only establishes which formula to maximize. Finding \(s^*\) still takes a derivative, which is the next step.
    🔗
    From here the work is identical to Part 1: maximizing \(\pi_1(s, b(s)) = 8s - s^2\) on \(0 \leq s \lt 8\) gives \(s^* = 4\text{,}\) and Firm 2 answers with \(t^* = b(4) = 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}(4) = 2\text{.}\) The price is \(p^* = 20 - 2(4+2) = 8\text{,}\) and the profits are \(16\) for Firm 1 and \(8\) for Firm 2.
    🔗
    Notice that the more realistic model produces the exact same answer as the first one! Here’s a quick recap of the two places the restrictions forced us to do extra work:
    🔗
    • Finding \(b(s)\text{.}\) For \(s \geq 8\text{,}\) Firm 1 has already pushed the price down to \(4\) or below, so every unit Firm 2 makes loses money and its best response is \(b(s) = 0\text{.}\) For \(s \lt 8\) there’s still a profitable window, and on that window \(\pi_2\) is the same function as in the first model, and we find the same answer \(b(s) = 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Maximizing \(\pi_1(s, b(s))\text{.}\) We ruled out \(s \geq 8\) for the same reason as above: the price is at or below Firm 1’s own cost of \(4\text{,}\) so producing there loses money too. On \(0 \leq s \lt 8\) we found \(s + b(s) = 4 + \tfrac{1}{2}s \lt 10\text{,}\) which keeps \(\pi_1\) on its first piece, the same formula as in the first model. So the maximization is the one we already did, and it gives the same \(s^*\text{,}\) \(t^*\text{,}\) price, and profits.
      🔗
      🔗

    Section 1.11, Stackelberg’s Model of Duopoly 2.

    Skim Section 1.11.
    🔗
    This section redoes both models in a more general setting: the price rule becomes \(p = \alpha - \beta(s+t)\) and the unit cost becomes \(c\text{.}\) The procedure is exactly the one you just used, so there’s no need to read every line carefully. What you should come away able to do is adapt the method from Section 1.10 when the price and cost are something other than \(20 - 2(s+t)\) and \(4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    One observation worth pulling out of that section: the general answer is \(s^* = \dfrac{\alpha - c}{2\beta}\) and \(t^* = \dfrac{\alpha - c}{4\beta}\text{,}\) with profits \(\dfrac{(\alpha-c)^2}{8\beta}\) and \(\dfrac{(\alpha-c)^2}{16\beta}\text{.}\) Firm 1 produces twice as much and earns twice as much no matter what \(\alpha\text{,}\) \(\beta\text{,}\) and \(c\) are, so the 2-to-1 split we found wasn’t an accident of our particular numbers. It’s what moving first is worth in any model of this shape.
