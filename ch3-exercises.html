    2. Water Pollution with Five Firms.

    This is a larger version of the Water Pollution game from Section 3.3, Water Pollution 1. Five firms share a lake. Each firm chooses to purify its wastewater, at a cost of 1, or to pollute, at no cost. If 3 or more firms pollute, the lake is fouled and every firm pays an additional cleanup cost of 3. A firm’s payoff is the negative of its total cost. For example:
    🔗
    • If only firm 1 pollutes, the lake stays clean. Firm 1 gets \(0\text{,}\) and firms 2 through 5 each get \(-1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If firms 1 through 4 pollute, the lake is fouled. Firms 1 through 4 each get \(-3\text{,}\) and firm 5 gets \(-1 - 3 = -4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    There are \(2^5 = 32\) strategy profiles, but you should not check them one at a time. Since all firms are treated the same, the analysis of a profile depends only on how many firms pollute.
    🔗

    (a)

    Let \(k\) be the number of polluting firms. For each \(k = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\text{,}\) determine whether the profiles with exactly \(k\) polluters are Nash equilibria. Justify each answer.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Describe all Nash equilibria of the game, and determine how many there are.
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Locating Two Banks, Revisited.

    Return to the two competing banks of Exercise 2.5.4: each bank chooses a block \(s_i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 99\}\) along a 99-block avenue, each block has 100 residents, residents use the closer bank, and a block equidistant from both banks splits its residents evenly. The payoff to each bank is its total number of customers. In that exercise we used iterated elimination to show that \((50, 50)\) is a dominant strategy equilibrium.
    🔗
    As before, please do not write out a \(99 \times 99\) matrix. Fix one bank’s location and think about how the other bank’s payoff changes as it moves.
    🔗

    (a)

    Show directly, without using iterated elimination, that \((50, 50)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗

    (b)

    Show that any strategy profile \((p, q)\) where \(p \lt q\) is not a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Split into two cases: the banks are not adjacent (\(q \geq p + 2\)), and the banks are adjacent (\(q = p + 1\)).

    4. Bidding the Resource.

    Each of two players has 1 (divisible) unit of a resource. Player 1 chooses some amount \(s_1 \in [0, 1]\text{,}\) and player 2 simultaneously chooses some amount \(s_2 \in [0, 1]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The player that chooses the higher amount wins the total of what remains, \((1 - s_1) + (1 - s_2)\text{.}\) The player that chooses the lower amount gets a payoff of zero, forfeiting the remaining amount to the other player. In the case of a tie, they split what remains. The payoffs are:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \pi_1(s_1, s_2) = \begin{cases} (1 - s_1) + (1 - s_2), \amp s_1 \gt s_2 \\ 1 - s_1, \amp s_1 = s_2 \\ 0, \amp s_1 \lt s_2 \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \pi_2(s_1, s_2) = \begin{cases} (1 - s_1) + (1 - s_2), \amp s_2 \gt s_1 \\ 1 - s_2, \amp s_1 = s_2 \\ 0, \amp s_2 \lt s_1 \end{cases} \end{equation*}

    (a)

    Show that \(s_1 = s_2 = 1\) is a Nash equilibrium. Is it a strict Nash equilibrium? Explain.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Show that there are no Nash equilibria where \(s_2 \lt s_1 \lt 1\text{,}\) by showing that player 2 can improve.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Now suppose \(s_2 \lt s_1 = 1\text{.}\) Explain why player 2’s deviation from part (b) no longer works, and show that the profile is still not a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Show that there are no Nash equilibria where \(s_1 = s_2 \lt 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark. There are infinitely many strategy profiles in this game, and parts (a) through (d) break them into exactly four cases. The cases we did not explicitly consider are handled through symmetry. For example, part (b) also shows there are no Nash equilibria where \(s_1 \lt s_2 \lt 1\text{.}\) So \((1, 1)\) is the only Nash equilibrium of this game.
