    Section 2.2 Dominated Strategies

    Section 2.3, Dominated Strategies.

    Read Section 2.3.
    🔗
    The book introduces the notion of “strict” and “weak” dominance of strategies. We will test your understanding of the definitions from the reading.
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s take a second look at the payoff matrix that is analyzed in Section 2.3. For reference, it is:
    🔗
    The interactive below walks through every pair of strategies in this matrix, one at a time. For each pair, read the two strategies’ payoffs across the matrix yourself, then decide whether one strictly dominates the other, weakly dominates it, or neither before moving on. Work through all seven to make sure you understand some of the nuances of the two definitions!
    🔗
    Two of those pairs are worth pinning down before we move on.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.3.

    Compare Player 2’s strategies c and d. Against a, both give \(2\text{;}\) against b, both give \(1\text{.}\) What is the relationship between them?
    🔗
    • Neither weakly dominates the other.
    • Correct. Weak dominance has two requirements: at least as good in every column, and strictly better in at least one. Here c and d clear the first requirement in both directions but fail the second in both directions, since there is no column where either one is strictly better.
    • Each weakly dominates the other, since neither is ever worse than the other.
    • Not quite. “Never worse” is only half of the definition. Weak dominance also requires being strictly better in at least one column, and there’s no column here where c beats d or d beats c. So neither one weakly dominates.
    • c weakly dominates d, because c comes first in the matrix and the payoffs are tied.
    • Not quite. Nothing in the definition depends on the order the strategies are written in. Ties in every column mean neither strategy weakly dominates the other.
    • c strictly dominates d, since strict dominance only requires not doing worse.
    • Not quite — that’s a weaker condition than strict dominance, which requires a strictly better payoff in every column. Tied payoffs rule out strict dominance immediately.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.4.

    Now compare Player 2’s strategies e and f. Against a, e gives \(4\) and f gives \(3\text{;}\) against b, e gives \(1\) and f gives \(2\text{.}\) What is the relationship between them?
    🔗
    • Neither dominates the other, in either sense.
    • Correct, and notice this is a different reason than the c and d case. There, the two strategies were tied everywhere. Here each strategy wins a column: e is better against a, f is better against b. Either way there is no domination, but the way it fails is not the same.
    • e weakly dominates f, since e’s best payoff \(4\) is larger than f’s best payoff \(3\text{.}\)
    • Not quite. Dominance is checked column by column, comparing what the two strategies give against the same choice by Player 1. Against b, f gives \(2\) and e gives \(1\text{,}\) so e is not always at least as good.
    • f weakly dominates e, since f gives Player 2 a higher payoff in the column that actually gets played.
    • Not quite. Dominance is a property of the matrix on its own, checked against every column, with no assumption about which one Player 1 will choose. That’s exactly what makes it a useful starting point; you can apply it before knowing anything about the other player’s behavior.
    🔗

    Section 2.5, Global Warming.

    Read Section 2.5.
    🔗
    In this example, the book finds a strictly dominant strategy, which leads to a type of social dilemma.
    🔗
    🔗
    Now that you’ve read it, let’s slow down on the mathematical derivations to clear up any confusion. Nothing below is new material; we are just breaking the equations apart and labeling where each piece came from.
    🔗
    The book’s payoff function packs three separate ideas into one line, so before we use it, let’s label them:
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \underbrace{\pi_i(x_1,\ldots,x_{10})}_{\substack{\text{country } i\text{'s payoff when the}\\ \text{countries spend } x_1,\ldots,x_{10}}} \;=\; \overbrace{\tfrac{1}{10}}^{\substack{\text{country } i \text{ receives one}\\ \text{tenth of the benefits}}} \cdot \underbrace{2(x_1+\cdots+x_{10})}_{\substack{\text{total benefits are twice the}\\ \text{countries' total expenditures}}} \;-\; \overbrace{x_i}^{\substack{\text{what country } i\\ \text{spends itself}}}\\ \phantom{\pi_i(x_1,\ldots,x_{10})} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{5}(x_1+\cdots+x_{10}) - x_i. \end{gather*}
    So a country’s payoff is its share of the benefits minus what it spent. Notice the asymmetry that drives everything here: a country pays for all of its own expenditure, but only collects a tenth of the benefit that expenditure creates.
    🔗
    Let’s take another look at the part that shows spending \(0\) strictly dominates any \(x_1 > 0\) for Player 1 (the same argument works for any other country \(i\)). Fix any choice of \(x_2,\ldots,x_{10}\) by the other countries, and set up the difference in payoffs, labeled:
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \underbrace{\pi_1(0, x_2, \ldots, x_{10})}_{\text{Player 1's payoff when Player 1 spends } 0} - \underbrace{\pi_1(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{10})}_{\text{Player 1's payoff when Player 1 spends } x_1 > 0} \end{gather*}
    Expanding both terms with the formula above:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \pi_1(0, x_2, \ldots, x_{10}) - \pi_1(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{10}) \amp = \left[\tfrac{1}{5}(0 + x_2 + \cdots + x_{10}) - 0\right] - \left[\tfrac{1}{5}(x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_{10}) - x_1\right]\\ \amp = -\tfrac{1}{5}x_1 + x_1\\ \amp = \tfrac{4}{5}x_1 \;>\; 0 . \end{align*}
    The last step uses \(x_1 > 0\text{.}\) Since that difference is positive, we can move the subtracted term across and read the result as a comparison of two payoffs:
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \pi_1(0, x_2, \ldots, x_{10}) \;>\; \pi_1(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{10}). \end{gather*}
    And we fixed \(x_2,\ldots,x_{10}\) arbitrarily, so this holds no matter what the other nine countries spend. That is exactly what strict dominance asks for: spending \(0\) gives Player 1 a strictly higher payoff than any \(x_1 > 0\) against every choice by the others. So \(x_1 = 0\) strictly dominates every other strategy, and the same argument applies to every other country.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.5.

    Why does this global warming game count as a version of the Prisoner’s Dilemma?
    🔗
    • Every country has a strictly dominant strategy (spend nothing), but if every country instead spent the “dominated” amount of \(1\text{,}\) every single country would end up better off.
    • Correct. Spending nothing gives every country a payoff of \(0\text{,}\) but if all ten countries spent \(1\text{,}\) every country’s payoff would be \(1\) instead, strictly better. Rational individual behavior (everyone defects) leads to a worse outcome for everyone than if they’d all cooperated, exactly the Prisoner’s Dilemma pattern, just with ten players instead of two.
    • Because each country’s payoff depends on what the other countries do.
    • Not quite, that’s true of almost every game we’ve studied so far, not just Prisoner’s Dilemma-type games. Something more specific has to hold.
    • Because there are more than two players.
    • Not quite, the Prisoner’s Dilemma pattern isn’t about the number of players, the original version had only two.
    🔗

    Section 2.7, Second-Price Auctions (Part 1).

    Read Section 2.7, up through where the book states the payoff formula \(\pi_i(b_1,\ldots,b_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The book goes through all the context on how to get the payoff functions, and this is important to understand before working through the rest of the section. Stop at this point, because we are going to discuss some ideas before finishing it.
    🔗
    🔗
    To clear up any doubts, we restate the payoff function formula, labeling where everything came from. You still need to read the book to understand where all the notation comes from!
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \underbrace{\pi_i(b_1,\ldots,b_n)}_{\text{payoff to player } i \text{ when the bids are } b_1,\ldots,b_n} \;=\; \begin{cases} \overbrace{0}^{\substack{\text{player } i\text{'s bid is below the highest bid of the}\\ \text{others, so she receives nothing and pays nothing}}} & \text{if } b_i < h_i, \\[2ex] \underbrace{v_i - h_i}_{\substack{\text{player } i\text{'s bid is above everyone else's, so she pays}\\ h_i \text{ for an object she values at } v_i}} & \text{if } h_i < b_i. \end{cases} \end{align*}
    Take note that in the second case, player \(i\) pays \(h_i\) (recall \(h_i\) is the highest bid among the other players), not their own bid \(b_i\text{.}\) That’s the defining feature of a second-price auction: the winner pays what the runner-up bid, not their own bid.
    🔗
    Before moving on with the reading, let’s work out why bidding your true value \(v_1\) weakly dominates every other bid. Fix some bid \(b_1 \neq v_1\text{.}\) Weak dominance asks for two conditions, one about every choice by the other players and one about at least one. See if you can state each before revealing it.
    🔗

    Example 2.2.6. Condition (1).

    The first condition compares \(v_1\) with \(b_1\) against every choice of \(b_2,\ldots,b_n\text{.}\) What does it say?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Bidding \(v_1\) must be at least as good as bidding \(b_1\text{,}\) no matter what the others do:
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \pi_1(v_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n) \;\geq\; \pi_1(b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n) \quad \text{for every } b_2,\ldots,b_n. \end{gather*}
    This is a \(\geq\text{,}\) so ties are allowed.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.2.7. Condition (2).

    The second condition only needs one choice of \(b_2,\ldots,b_n\text{.}\) What does it say?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The comparison must be strict for at least one choice by the other players:
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \pi_1(v_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n) \;>\; \pi_1(b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n) \quad \text{for at least one } b_2,\ldots,b_n. \end{gather*}
    That choice may depend on \(b_1\text{:}\) a different rival bid can call for a different choice by the others.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We’ll take the conditions one at a time. Start with condition (1). Whether bidding \(v_1\) wins the auction depends on how the other players’ bids turned out, so the reasoning splits into two cases: \(h_1 < v_1\text{,}\) where bidding your value would win, and \(v_1 < h_1\text{,}\) where it would lose. Let’s do the first one together.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.8.

    Assume the other players’ bids make it so that \(h_1 < v_1\text{,}\) and let \(b_1\) be any other bid you might have made. Does condition (1) for weak dominance hold here, and why?
    🔗
    • Yes. Bidding \(v_1\) wins and pays \(h_1\text{,}\) for a payoff of \(v_1 - h_1 > 0\text{.}\) Any \(b_1\) above \(h_1\) also wins and also pays \(h_1\text{,}\) giving exactly the same payoff. Any \(b_1\) below \(h_1\) loses, giving \(0\text{,}\) which is smaller. So \(v_1\) is never worse.
    • Correct. Notice the comparison splits into a region where the two bids tie and a region where \(v_1\) genuinely wins out. Condition (1) only asks for \(\geq\text{,}\) so the ties are perfectly acceptable.
    • No. Every bid above \(h_1\) gives exactly the same payoff as \(v_1\text{,}\) and a condition about dominance cannot be satisfied by a tie.
    • Not quite. This is condition (1), the \(\geq\) requirement, so ties are fine. It is condition (2) that demands a strict comparison, and it only demands one.
    • Yes, and in fact \(v_1\) is strictly better than every other bid here, since bidding your true value is always optimal.
    • Not quite. Bidding \(v_1\) is never worse, but it is not strictly better than everything: any bid above \(h_1\) wins the same object at the same price \(h_1\text{,}\) so those bids tie with \(v_1\text{.}\)
    • Yes, because bidding higher wins by a wider margin and so earns a larger payoff.
    • Not quite — the conclusion is right but the reason is not. The price you pay is \(h_1\text{,}\) the runner-up’s bid, never your own, so bidding higher does not increase your payoff at all.
    🔗
    On your own, think about why condition (1) also holds in the case \(v_1 < h_1\text{.}\) This appears in the reading later as well. Together, the two cases give condition (1): bidding \(v_1\) is at least as good as bidding \(b_1\) against every choice of \(b_2,\ldots,b_n\) by the other players.
    🔗
    Now for condition (2). Having fixed a bid \(b_1\text{,}\) we need to find one collection of bids by the other players that makes \(v_1\) give Player 1 a strictly better payoff than \(b_1\) does. This also breaks into two cases, but notice they are not the same two cases as before: what matters now is not where \(h_1\) landed, but whether \(b_1\) is an underbid or an overbid. So the cases are \(b_1 < v_1\) and \(v_1 < b_1\text{.}\) We do the first one together in the checkpoint below!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.9.

    Take a bid \(b_1 < v_1\text{.}\) Condition (2) asks us to produce some bids \(b_2,\ldots,b_n\) by the other players making \(v_1\) strictly better than \(b_1\text{.}\) Which choice does the job?
    🔗
    • Bids whose highest is strictly between \(b_1\) and \(v_1\text{,}\) so that \(b_1 < h_1 < v_1\text{.}\) Then \(v_1\) wins the auction for a payoff of \(v_1 - h_1 > 0\text{,}\) while \(b_1\) falls below \(h_1\) and loses, for a payoff of \(0\text{.}\)
    • Correct, and notice how the choice depends on \(b_1\text{:}\) for a different underbid you would slide \(h_1\) somewhere else. That is allowed, since condition (2) only asks that a suitable choice exist for each \(b_1\text{.}\) In words: underbidding costs you an auction you wanted to win, whenever somebody else bids into the gap you left open.
    • Bids whose highest is below \(b_1\text{,}\) so that \(h_1 < b_1 < v_1\text{.}\)
    • Not quite. Then both \(v_1\) and \(b_1\) are above \(h_1\text{,}\) so both win and both pay \(h_1\text{.}\) The payoffs are equal, and condition (2) needs a strict inequality.
    • Bids whose highest is above \(v_1\text{,}\) so that \(b_1 < v_1 < h_1\text{.}\)
    • Not quite. Then both bids are below \(h_1\text{,}\) so both lose the auction and both give a payoff of \(0\text{.}\) Equal again, so this does not satisfy condition (2).
    • No choice works. We saw in the previous checkpoint that \(v_1\) ties with other bids, so a strict inequality is impossible.
    • Not quite. The previous checkpoint found ties against bids above \(h_1\text{,}\) but that was for one fixed \(h_1\text{.}\) Here we are free to move the other players’ bids around, and some positions do produce a strict comparison.
    🔗
    On your own, work out the other case, an overbid \(v_1 < b_1\text{.}\) The idea is the same, but in the other direction: choose the other players’ bids so that \(h_1\) lands strictly between \(v_1\) and \(b_1\text{,}\) then compare what each bid earns you. Together, the two cases give condition (2) for every \(b_1 \neq v_1\text{,}\) and with condition (1) we have that \(v_1\) weakly dominates every other bid.
    🔗
    Now let’s finish off the section!

    🔗
    🔗
    That was quite a bit of case analysis to get to the result on weak dominance for the auction. But the result makes sense, and it shows why second-price auctions are a good method. Your best option is to bid what you think the item is worth, and this holds no matter what your opponents choose. So there are no mind games or worrying about the other bids when you decide what to bid!
