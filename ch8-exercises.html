    1. A Completely Mixed Evolutionarily Stable State.

    Consider the symmetric two-player game below, with payoffs listed as (Player 1, Player 2). Let \(\sigma = \left(\frac{11}{18}, \frac{5}{18}, \frac{2}{18}\right)\text{.}\) In an earlier homework, we showed that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium. Since both players use the same mixed strategy in a symmetric game, this is a symmetric mixed strategy Nash equilibrium.
    🔗

    (a)

    The population state \(\sigma\) is an evolutionarily stable state of the corresponding evolutionary game. (You do not need to check this.) Which theorem from Chapter 8 lets you quickly conclude that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is the only symmetric Nash equilibrium of the game? Check that its hypotheses are satisfied. What does the same theorem tell you about other evolutionarily stable states?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Without computing payoffs or using best responses, quickly deduce that none of \((a, a)\text{,}\) \((b, b)\text{,}\) and \((c, c)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use a theorem from Chapter 8.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    This part and the next are not about the game above. Suppose that in some symmetric game with three pure strategies \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\text{,}\) the profile \((a, a)\) is a strict Nash equilibrium. What can you conclude about the population state \(a\text{?}\) What can you conclude about any population state in which all three pure strategies are active? Cite the theorems you use.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Suppose a symmetric game has two different symmetric Nash equilibria \((\sigma, \sigma)\) and \((\tau, \tau)\text{,}\) and all pure strategies are active in both \(\sigma\) and \(\tau\text{.}\) Explain why neither \(\sigma\) nor \(\tau\) is an evolutionarily stable state.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Theorem 8.3 is useful here.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark: No calculations are needed for this exercise. It is about understanding and applying the relevant definitions and theorems. One important step in using a theorem is checking that its hypotheses are satisfied.

    4. Hawk, Dove, and Owl.

    In earlier assignments, we looked at the hawk-dove game. Here is a variation where each player has three strategies. The players are fighting over valuable territory, and one of them arrived in the territory first. Each player is equally likely to be the one who arrived first.
    🔗
    Each player has three strategies:
    🔗
    • hawk (\(h\)): fight aggressively until you win or are injured.
      🔗
      🔗
    • dove (\(d\)): bluff by displaying, but retreat if the other player begins to fight.
      🔗
      🔗
    • owl (\(o\)): if you arrived in the territory first, play \(h\) (defend your territory). If the other player arrived first, play \(d\) (give up the territory if they fight for it).
      🔗
      🔗
    We use the following parameters:
    🔗
    • \(v\text{:}\) the value of the territory.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(w\text{:}\) the cost of getting injured in a fight.
      🔗
      🔗
    • We assume \(v \lt w\text{:}\) the cost of being injured is greater than the benefit of winning the fight.
      🔗
      🔗
    These assumptions lead to the following payoff matrix, with payoffs listed as (Player 1, Player 2).
    🔗

    (a)

    Show how the payoffs for the strategy profile \((d, o)\) were calculated.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use best responses to find all pure strategy Nash equilibria.
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Rock-Paper-Scissors Has No Evolutionarily Stable State.

    In Rock-Paper-Scissors (Problem 5.12.4 in the book), paper beats rock, scissors beats paper, and rock beats scissors. The winner gets \(1\text{,}\) the loser gets \(-1\text{,}\) and a tie gives both players \(0\text{.}\) Payoffs are listed as (Player 1, Player 2).
    🔗
    Let \(\sigma = \left(\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}\right)\text{,}\) the strategy that plays rock, paper, and scissors equally often.
    🔗

    (a)

    Show that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute the payoff of each pure strategy \(r\text{,}\) \(p\text{,}\) and \(s\) against \(\sigma\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use Theorem 8.1 to show that \(\sigma\) is not an evolutionarily stable state.
    🔗
    Hint.
    It is enough to find one \(\tau \neq \sigma\) for which a condition of Theorem 8.1 fails.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    You may use the fact (Problem 5.12.4 in the book) that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is the only Nash equilibrium of Rock-Paper-Scissors. Using a relevant theorem from this chapter, explain why the corresponding evolutionary game has no evolutionarily stable state at all.
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark: By Theorem 7.1, every symmetric game has a symmetric Nash equilibrium. This exercise shows that nothing similar is true for evolutionarily stable states: being evolutionarily stable is a stronger requirement than being a Nash equilibrium, and some games have no evolutionarily stable state.
