    1. A Completely Mixed Evolutionarily Stable State.

    Consider the symmetric two-player game below, with payoffs listed as (Player 1, Player 2). Let \(\sigma = \left(\frac{11}{18}, \frac{5}{18}, \frac{2}{18}\right)\text{.}\) In an earlier homework, we showed that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium. Since both players use the same mixed strategy in a symmetric game, this is a symmetric mixed strategy Nash equilibrium.
    🔗

    (a)

    The population state \(\sigma\) is an evolutionarily stable state of the corresponding evolutionary game. (You do not need to check this.) Use Theorem 8.3 to show that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is the only symmetric Nash equilibrium of the game.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Explain why the result from the previous part implies that \(\sigma\) is the only evolutionarily stable state of the corresponding evolutionary game.
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark: No calculations are needed for this exercise. It is about understanding and applying the relevant definitions and theorem. One important step in using a theorem is checking that its hypotheses are satisfied.
    🔗

    3. Checking the Conditions of Theorem 8.1.

    Consider the symmetric two-player game below.
    🔗

    (a)

    Find all pure strategy Nash equilibria. You can use best responses to find and indicate them in the usual way.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Let \(\tau = (p, 1 - p)\) denote a mixed strategy. Use Theorem 8.1 and \(\tau\) to decide whether either of the pure strategies used in the Nash equilibria from the previous part is an evolutionarily stable state. For each pure strategy, show that it either satisfies or fails the conditions of Theorem 8.1.
    🔗
    Remark: To satisfy the conditions, they must hold for every \(\tau\text{.}\) To fail them, it is enough to find one \(\tau\) where a condition fails. Also, be careful to distinguish between a population state \(\sigma\) and a profile \((\sigma, \sigma)\) or \((\sigma, \tau)\text{.}\) For example, it does not make sense to say that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is evolutionarily stable.
    🔗

    (c)

    Let \(\sigma = (q, 1 - q)\) with \(0 \lt q \lt 1\text{.}\) Is \(\sigma\) an evolutionarily stable state? Compare the payoffs of \(a\) and \(b\) against \(\sigma\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    For \((\sigma, \sigma)\) to be a Nash equilibrium when \(\sigma\) uses both \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) the strategies \(a\) and \(b\) must do equally well against \(\sigma\text{.}\)

    4. Hawk, Dove, and Owl.

    In earlier assignments, we looked at the hawk-dove game. Here is a variation where each player has three strategies. The players are fighting over valuable territory, and one of them arrived in the territory first. Each player is equally likely to be the one who arrived first.
    🔗
    Each player has three strategies:
    🔗
    • hawk (\(h\)): fight aggressively until you win or are injured.
      🔗
      🔗
    • dove (\(d\)): bluff by displaying, but retreat if the other player begins to fight.
      🔗
      🔗
    • owl (\(o\)): if you arrived in the territory first, play \(h\) (defend your territory). If the other player arrived first, play \(d\) (give up the territory if they fight for it).
      🔗
      🔗
    We use the following parameters:
    🔗
    • \(v\text{:}\) the value of the territory.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(w\text{:}\) the cost of getting injured in a fight.
      🔗
      🔗
    • We assume \(v \lt w\text{:}\) the cost of being injured is greater than the benefit of winning the fight.
      🔗
      🔗
    These assumptions lead to the following payoff matrix, with payoffs listed as (Player 1, Player 2).
    🔗

    (a)

    Show how the payoffs for the strategy profile \((d, o)\) were calculated.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use best responses to find all pure strategy Nash equilibria.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    For each Nash equilibrium from the previous part, determine whether the equilibrium strategy is an evolutionarily stable state of the corresponding evolutionary game.
    🔗
    Hint.
    For each Nash equilibrium, consider:
    🔗
    • Is it symmetric, so that both players use the same strategy? Only a symmetric Nash equilibrium \((s, s)\) can give an evolutionarily stable state \(s\text{.}\)

      🔗
    • If it is symmetric, is it a strict Nash equilibrium? If so, what does that tell you about whether \(s\) is evolutionarily stable?
