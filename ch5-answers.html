    Exercises 5.8 Answers to Ch. 5 Exercises

    Answers to the exercises of this chapter. Click an exercise to open it, click a part to open that part, then click Solution to reveal the answer.
    🔗
    These are the answers to the questions, not fully worked solutions. Enough is shown that you can tell whether your own work got to the correct conclusion.
    🔗

    1. Computing a Mixed Equilibrium.

    (a) The mixed strategy Nash equilibrium.

    Find a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium in which both players use both of their strategies.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\left(\frac{1}{5}a + \frac{4}{5}b,\ \frac{3}{4}x + \frac{1}{4}y\right)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Expected payoffs.

    What is each player’s expected payoff at this equilibrium?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 1 gets \(\frac{3}{2}\) and Player 2 gets \(\frac{28}{5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. A Symmetric \(3 \times 3\) Game.

    (a) The system of equations.

    Set up a system of linear equations whose solution gives a symmetric Nash equilibrium \((\sigma, \sigma)\) with all three strategies active.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Writing \(\sigma = xa + yb + zc\text{,}\) Player 1’s three strategies must earn the same payoff against \(\sigma\text{,}\) and the probabilities must add to \(1\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \pi_1(a, \sigma) \amp = 2x + 6y + 5z\\ \pi_1(b, \sigma) \amp = 3x + 3y + 9z\\ \pi_1(c, \sigma) \amp = 4x + 4y + 7z \end{align*}
    So the system is
    \begin{align*} 2x + 6y + 5z = 3x + 3y + 9z \amp = 4x + 4y + 7z\\ x + y + z \amp = 1 \end{align*}
    (Using \(z = 1 - x - y\) instead gives two equations in \(x\) and \(y\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) The symmetric Nash equilibrium.

    Solve the system and state the Nash equilibrium \((\sigma, \sigma)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((\sigma, \sigma)\) with \(\sigma = \frac{1}{6}a + \frac{1}{2}b + \frac{1}{3}c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Active and Inactive Strategies.

    (a) All three strategies active.

    Show that there is no mixed strategy Nash equilibrium in which all three of Player 1’s strategies are active.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\pi_1(a, \sigma_2) = \pi_1(b, \sigma_2)\) requires \(q = \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) but \(\pi_1(a, \sigma_2) = \pi_1(c, \sigma_2)\) requires \(q = \frac{2}{3}\text{.}\) Both can’t hold, so there is no such equilibrium.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) \(a\) and \(b\) active.

    Find a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium with \(a\) and \(b\) active in \(\sigma_1\text{,}\) \(c\) inactive, and both \(x\) and \(y\) active in \(\sigma_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    • \(\left(\frac{3}{4}a + \frac{1}{4}b,\ \frac{1}{2}x + \frac{1}{2}y\right)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Inequality condition: \(\pi_1(a, \sigma_2) = \pi_1(b, \sigma_2) = 4\) and \(\pi_1(c, \sigma_2) = \frac{7}{2} \leq 4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) \(a\) and \(c\) active.

    Show that there is no mixed strategy Nash equilibrium with \(a\) and \(c\) active in \(\sigma_1\) and \(b\) inactive.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The equality condition gives \(q = \frac{2}{3}\text{.}\) Then \(\pi_1(a, \sigma_2) = \pi_1(c, \sigma_2) = \frac{10}{3}\text{,}\) but \(\pi_1(b, \sigma_2) = \frac{13}{3} \gt \frac{10}{3}\text{,}\) so the inequality condition fails.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. A Two-Stage Prisoner’s Dilemma.

    (a) The missing payoffs.

    Find the payoffs for \((D, T)\) and \((T, D)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((D, T)\) gives \((5, -1)\) and \((T, D)\) gives \((-1, 5)\text{.}\) In stage 1 the defector gets \(5\) and the tit for tat player gets \(-1\text{;}\) in stage 2 both play \(d\) and get \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Pure strategy Nash equilibria.

    Find all pure strategy Nash equilibria.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((D, D)\) and \((T, T)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) The equality condition.

    Show that the equality condition holds for every \(p\) and \(q\) in \((0, 1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\pi_1(C, \sigma_2) = \pi_1(T, \sigma_2) = 6\) and \(\pi_2(\sigma_1, C) = \pi_2(\sigma_1, T) = 6\text{,}\) no matter what \(p\) and \(q\) are.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) The inequality condition.

    Find exactly which values of \(p\) and \(q\) make \((\sigma_1, \sigma_2)\) a Nash equilibrium, and interpret.
    🔗
    Solution.
    • \(\pi_1(D, \sigma_2) = 5 + 5q \leq 6\) gives \(q \leq \frac{1}{5}\text{,}\) and likewise \(\pi_2(\sigma_1, D) = 5 + 5p \leq 6\) gives \(p \leq \frac{1}{5}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • So the Nash equilibria are exactly the profiles with \(0 \lt p \leq \frac{1}{5}\) and \(0 \lt q \leq \frac{1}{5}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • In words: a player can use always cooperate at most \(\frac{1}{5}\) of the time. Any more, and the other player does better by switching to always defect.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. A Simple Poker Game, Revisited.

    (a) The payoff functions.

    Find \(\pi_1(p, q)\) and \(\pi_2(p, q)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} \pi_1(p, q) \amp = 2p(1-q) + q(1-p)\\ \pi_2(p, q) \amp = -2p(1-q) - q(1-p) \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) The partial derivative method.

    Use the partial derivative method to find a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium. Which partial derivatives do you set to \(0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    • Set \(\frac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial p} = 2 - 3q = 0\) and \(\frac{\partial \pi_2}{\partial q} = 3p - 1 = 0\text{,}\) since each player only controls their own variable.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The equilibrium is \(\left(\frac{1}{3}BB + \frac{2}{3}BF,\ \frac{2}{3}B + \frac{1}{3}F\right)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Expected payoffs.

    Find each player’s expected payoff at the Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 1 gets \(\frac{2}{3}\) and Player 2 gets \(-\frac{2}{3}\text{.}\) The payoffs in every cell add to \(0\text{,}\) so \(\pi_2 = -\pi_1\) for every strategy profile.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) Interpreting the equilibrium.

    Interpret the equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    • With the high card, Player 1 always bids.
      🔗
      🔗
    • With the low card, Player 1 bluffs \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the time.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Player 2 bids back \(\frac{2}{3}\) of the time.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Maximins and Theorem 5.6.

    (a) Pure strategies only.

    Find the pure worst cases, the best pure strategies \(s_1\) and \(s_2\text{,}\) and decide whether \((s_1, s_2)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(m_1(a) = \min(3, -1) = -1\text{,}\) \(m_1(b) = \min(-2, 1) = -2\text{,}\) \(m_2(x) = \min(-3, 2) = -3\text{,}\) and \(m_2(y) = \min(1, -1) = -1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(s_1 = a\text{,}\) guaranteeing \(m_1(a) = -1\text{,}\) and \(s_2 = y\text{,}\) guaranteeing \(m_2(y) = -1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. No. Against \(y\text{,}\) Player 1 would rather play \(b\) (\(1 \gt -1\)). The guaranteed payoffs add up to \(-2\text{,}\) not \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) A general zero-sum game.

    Answer each question and say which part of Theorem 5.6 tells you.
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(m_1^\dagger = 5\) by part (2), since Player 1 gets her maximin payoff at the Nash equilibrium \((\sigma_1, \sigma_2)\text{.}\) Then \(m_2^\dagger = -5\) by part (3).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\pi_1(\tau_1, \tau_2) = m_1^\dagger = 5\text{,}\) by part (2).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Yes, by the interchangeability property (which comes from part (1): \(\sigma_1\) and \(\tau_2\) are both maximin strategies). By part (2), \(\pi_2(\sigma_1, \tau_2) = m_2^\dagger = -5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. No. Interchangeability says you can swap halves, not that the halves are equal, and a player can have more than one maximin strategy. For example, in the zero-sum game where both of Player 1’s rows are \((1, -1)\) against \(x\) and \((0, 0)\) against \(y\text{,}\) both \((a, y)\) and \((b, y)\) are Nash equilibria.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Computing a Maximin Equilibrium.

    (a) Player 1’s worst case.

    Fill in the sign analysis and find \(m_1(p)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\dfrac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial q} = 7p - 3\text{,}\) which is negative if \(0 \leq p \lt \frac{3}{7}\text{,}\) zero if \(p = \frac{3}{7}\text{,}\) and positive if \(\frac{3}{7} \lt p \leq 1\text{.}\) So the worst case is at \(q = 1\) for \(p \lt \frac{3}{7}\) and at \(q = 0\) for \(p \gt \frac{3}{7}\text{.}\) Since \(\pi_1(p, 1) = 5p - 2\) and \(\pi_1(p, 0) = 1 - 2p\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} m_1(p) = \begin{cases} 5p - 2 \amp \text{if } 0 \leq p \lt \frac{3}{7}, \\ \frac{1}{7} \amp \text{if } p = \frac{3}{7}, \\ 1 - 2p \amp \text{if } \frac{3}{7} \lt p \leq 1. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Player 1’s maximin strategy.

    Find \(p^\dagger\) and \(m_1^\dagger\text{,}\) and compare with pure strategies.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The graph is an upside-down V with its peak at \((\frac{3}{7}, \frac{1}{7})\text{.}\) So \(p^\dagger = \frac{3}{7}\text{,}\) that is, \(\sigma_1^\dagger = \frac{3}{7}a + \frac{4}{7}b\text{,}\) and \(m_1^\dagger = \frac{1}{7}\text{.}\) This is more than the \(m_1(a) = -1\) that Player 1 could guarantee with pure strategies only.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Player 2’s maximin strategy.

    Explain why \(\pi_2 = -\pi_1\text{,}\) and find \(m_2(q)\text{,}\) \(q^\dagger\text{,}\) and \(m_2^\dagger\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. The game is zero-sum, so the payoffs add up to \(0\) in every cell. Each expected payoff is a weighted average of the cells with the same weights, so \(\pi_1 + \pi_2 = 0\) for mixed strategies too, and \(\pi_2(p, q) = -\pi_1(p, q)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\dfrac{\partial \pi_2}{\partial p} = 2 - 7q\text{,}\) which is positive for \(q \lt \frac{2}{7}\) and negative for \(q \gt \frac{2}{7}\text{.}\) So the worst case is at \(p = 0\) for \(q \lt \frac{2}{7}\) and at \(p = 1\) for \(q \gt \frac{2}{7}\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} m_2(q) = \begin{cases} \pi_2(0, q) = 3q - 1 \amp \text{if } 0 \leq q \lt \frac{2}{7}, \\ -\frac{1}{7} \amp \text{if } q = \frac{2}{7}, \\ \pi_2(1, q) = 1 - 4q \amp \text{if } \frac{2}{7} \lt q \leq 1. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The peak is at \((\frac{2}{7}, -\frac{1}{7})\text{,}\) so \(q^\dagger = \frac{2}{7}\) and \(m_2^\dagger = -\frac{1}{7}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) Using Theorem 5.6.

    Find the maximin equilibrium, use Theorem 5.6, and compare with pure strategies.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The maximin equilibrium is \((p^\dagger, q^\dagger) = (\frac{3}{7}, \frac{2}{7})\text{,}\) and \(m_1^\dagger + m_2^\dagger = \frac{1}{7} - \frac{1}{7} = 0\text{.}\) By part (1) of Theorem 5.6, \((\frac{3}{7}, \frac{2}{7})\) is a Nash equilibrium, and by part (2), the payoffs there are \((\frac{1}{7}, -\frac{1}{7})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    With pure strategies only, each player could guarantee just \(-1\text{,}\) the guarantees added to \(-2\text{,}\) and there was no Nash equilibrium. With mixing, both players can guarantee more, the maximin payoffs add to \(0\text{,}\) and the maximin equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. Triple Rock-Paper-Scissors.

    (a) Zero-sum.

    Why is the game zero-sum?
    🔗
    Solution.
    In every cell, the two payoffs add up to \(0\text{:}\) whatever the winner gets, the loser loses, and ties give both players \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Player 1’s strategy.

    Find \(\sigma_1^*\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 2’s three strategies are active, so \(-b + 3c = a - c = -3a + b\text{.}\) These give \(c = a\) and \(b = 3a\text{,}\) and \(a + b + c = 1\) then gives \(a = \frac{1}{5}\text{.}\) So \(\sigma_1^* = \frac{1}{5}r + \frac{3}{5}p + \frac{1}{5}s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Player 2’s strategy.

    Find \(\sigma_2^*\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The same algebra, with Player 1’s indifference equations \(-y + 3z = x - z = -3x + y\text{,}\) gives \(\sigma_2^* = \frac{1}{5}r + \frac{3}{5}p + \frac{1}{5}s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) Only one Nash equilibrium.

    Explain why there are no other Nash equilibria.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \((\tau_1, \tau_2)\) be any Nash equilibrium. By interchangeability, \((\tau_1, \sigma_2^*)\) is also a Nash equilibrium. In it, Player 2 uses all three strategies, so by part (b), \(\tau_1 = \sigma_1^*\text{.}\) In the same way, \((\sigma_1^*, \tau_2)\) is a Nash equilibrium in which Player 1 uses all three strategies, so by part (c), \(\tau_2 = \sigma_2^*\text{.}\) So \((\tau_1, \tau_2) = (\sigma_1^*, \sigma_2^*)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e) Why paper?

    Speculate why paper is played most often.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Answers will vary. One explanation: rock now beats scissors by a lot, so for rock not to be too tempting, scissors has to be played rarely. Paper’s only weakness is scissors, so when scissors is rare, paper is safe and is played more. And with more paper around, rock is played less.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9. Tit for Tat.

    (a) The tie.

    Check that \(h\) ties with \(t\) against Bob’s \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Against Bob’s \(t\text{,}\) Al gets \(10\) from \(h\) and \(10\) from \(t\) (and \(9\) from \(l\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) The indifference equations.

    Check that both indifference equations hold for every \(p\) and \(q\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\pi_1(h, \tau) = 10q + 10(1-q) = 10\) and \(\pi_1(t, \tau) = 10q + 10(1-q) = 10\text{.}\) Similarly, \(\pi_2(\sigma, h) = \pi_2(\sigma, t) = 10\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) The inequality conditions.

    Find the range of \(p\) and \(q\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\pi_1(l, \tau) = 12q + 9(1-q) = 9 + 3q \leq 10\) gives \(q \leq \frac{1}{3}\text{.}\) Similarly, \(\pi_2(\sigma, l) = 9 + 3p \leq 10\) gives \(p \leq \frac{1}{3}\text{.}\) So \((ph + (1-p)t,\ qh + (1-q)t)\) is a Nash equilibrium for every \(0 \lt p \leq \frac{1}{3}\) and \(0 \lt q \leq \frac{1}{3}\text{:}\) a whole range of equilibria, not just one.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) Swapping halves.

    Why doesn’t \((l, \tau)\) contradict interchangeability?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Interchangeability comes from Theorem 5.6, which only applies to zero-sum games, and this game isn’t zero-sum (for example, \((10, 10)\)). (Against Al’s \(l\text{,}\) Bob’s \(\tau\) earns \(\frac{1}{3}(0) + \frac{2}{3}(3) = 2\text{,}\) while \(l\) earns \(6\text{.}\))
