    Section 4.2 Buying a Used Car

    Here’s our first real example of a game with incomplete information.
    🔗
    A customer wants to know whether a used car is worth the price. The salesman knows whether it is, and she doesn’t. He’d like to sell the car, and he’d also like a reputation for telling the truth, so it isn’t obvious what he should say or whether she should believe him.
    🔗

    Section 4.4, Setting Up the Game.

    Read Section 4.4 from the beginning through the two bulleted lists of strategies (\(gg\text{,}\) \(gb\text{,}\) \(bg\text{,}\) \(bb\) for the salesman, then the same four for the customer). So you will stop when you reach the paragraph beginning “Consider the salesman to be Player 1.”
    🔗
    Read both lists carefully. The two players use the same four pairs of letters, but the letters don’t mean the same thing for each of them.
    🔗
    🔗
    Here’s the game tree, so you have it nearby while you work through the questions below.
    🔗
    Right before the strategy lists, the book explains why backward induction doesn’t work on this game. Let’s make sure that landed.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.2.2.

    Why can’t we solve this game with backward induction?
    🔗
    • When the salesman says “good,” the customer’s best move depends on whether the car is really good. How likely that is depends on the salesman’s strategy, and we haven’t figured that out yet.
    • Correct. Backward induction solves the bottom of the tree first, but the customer’s best move at the bottom depends on what the salesman does higher up. If he always tells the truth, “good” means good. If he always lies, “good” means bad. She can’t pick a move until she knows which kind of salesman she’s dealing with.
    • Because Nature moves first, and backward induction doesn’t work once chance is involved.
    • Chance by itself isn’t the problem. If only Nature’s moves came before the customer’s information set, she’d know the chances of being at each node were \(p\) and \(1-p\text{,}\) and she could use expected value to pick her move. The trouble is that the salesman also makes a choice before she moves.
    • Because the customer doesn’t know \(p\text{.}\)
    • Even if she knows \(p\) exactly, it isn’t enough. The number \(p\) tells her how likely a car like this is to be good. What she needs is how likely it is to be good given that the salesman said so, and that depends on how honest he is.
    🔗
    Since we can’t use backward induction, the book converts the game to normal form using the strategy lists you just read.
    🔗
    First, a quick reminder of what the two letters in a strategy mean:
    \begin{equation*} \text{Salesman: }\; \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{what he says}\\ \text{if the car is good}}} \quad \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{what he says}\\ \text{if the car is bad}}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Customer: }\; \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{what she concludes}\\ \text{if he says good}}} \quad \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{what she concludes}\\ \text{if he says bad}}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now let’s put both players together and follow a strategy profile through the tree. Take \((gg, gb)\text{,}\) where the salesman always says the car is good and the customer always believes him. The salesman is Player 1, so his strategy is listed first.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.2.3.

    Suppose the players use \((gg, gb)\) and Nature makes the car bad. What are the payoffs?
    🔗
    • \((2,0)\)
    • Correct. He says the car is good, she believes him and buys it, so he collects 2 points for the sale. He doesn’t get the truth point, because the car was bad. She’s wrong about the car, so she gets 0.
    • \((3,0)\)
    • He does sell the car, which is worth 2 points. But the extra point is for telling the truth, and he said a bad car was good. Follow the \(b\) branch out of Nature and then the \(g\) branch out of \(S\) to see this on the tree.
    • \((0,1)\)
    • This is what happens if the customer decides the car is bad and walks away. But her strategy here is \(gb\text{,}\) and he said “good,” so she uses her first letter and concludes the car is good.
    🔗
    That’s one of Nature’s two branches. The profile \((gg, gb)\) doesn’t pick out a single path through the tree, though, because Nature could go either way. The widget below puts both paths side by side and asks what we can say about the payoffs.
    🔗
    You just built the salesman’s payoff for one strategy profile out of two paths and their probabilities. In other words, his payoff is the expected value of his payoffs on the two paths:
    \begin{align*} \text{his expected payoff} \amp = p \cdot (\text{payoff if the car is good}) + (1-p) \cdot (\text{payoff if the car is bad})\\ \amp = p(3) + (1-p)(2) = 2 + p. \end{align*}
    The same reasoning works for the customer: \(p(1) + (1-p)(0) = p\text{.}\) So the profile \((gg, gb)\) has payoffs \((2+p,\, p)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    There are sixteen profiles in this game, and every one of them works the same way. The rest of Section 4.4 does exactly that and collects the results into a payoff matrix. Look for \((2+p,\, p)\) in it!
    🔗

    Section 4.4, Building the Payoff Matrix.

    Pick up where you stopped and read through the next three payoff matrices. The third matrix is the first one with \(p\)’s in it. Stop right after it, just before the paragraph beginning “Let us assume \(p \gt \frac{1}{2}\text{.}\)” We’ll find the Nash equilibria together before you finish the reading.
    🔗
    • The book writes down the payoff matrix for a good car and the payoff matrix for a bad car separately.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The third matrix combines them: \(p\) times the first plus \(1-p\) times the second.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Now let’s find the Nash equilibria. We’ll assume \(p \gt \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) which just means the car is usually good. As in Section 3.9, a cell is a Nash equilibrium when both payoffs in it are boxed. In the widget below you’ll box the customer’s best responses one row at a time. Some rows may have more than one best response.
    🔗
    Notice that \((gg, gb)\) is one of the two equilibria. It’s the same profile we traced through the tree earlier.
    🔗

    Section 4.4, Finishing Up.

    Read the rest of Section 4.4. We just worked out the Nash equilibria, so you can move quickly through the boxed matrix. The last two paragraphs reflect on the results.
