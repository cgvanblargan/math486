    Section 4.2 Buying a Used Car

    Here’s our first real example of a game with incomplete information. A customer wants to know whether a used car is worth the price. The salesman knows whether it is, and she doesn’t. He’d like to sell the car, and he’d also like a reputation for telling the truth, so it isn’t obvious what he should say or whether she should believe him.
    🔗

    Section 4.4, Setting Up the Game.

    Read Section 4.4 from the beginning through the two lists of strategies. Stop when you reach the paragraph beginning “Consider the salesman to be Player 1.”
    🔗
    • Nature moves first and decides whether the car is good (with probability \(p\)) or bad (with probability \(1-p\)). The salesman sees Nature’s move. The customer does not.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Pay attention to why backward induction won’t work here, and notice that it’s the second case from Section 4.3: the customer’s information sets have a node in front of them where the salesman made a choice.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Each player gets four strategies, each named by two letters. Read both lists carefully. The letters do not mean the same thing for the two players, which is the subject of the next couple of questions.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Here’s the game tree, so you have it nearby while you work through the questions below.
    🔗
    Both players label their strategies with the same four pairs of letters, \(gg\text{,}\) \(gb\text{,}\) \(bg\text{,}\) and \(bb\text{,}\) but the letters answer different questions. Let’s make sure that’s clear before we go any further.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.2.2.

    What does the salesman’s strategy \(gg\) tell him to do?
    🔗
    • Say the car is good, whether Nature made it good or bad.
    • Correct. His first letter is what he says when the car is good, and his second letter is what he says when the car is bad. Both are \(g\) here, so he says the car is good either way.
    • Say the car is good when it is good, and say it is bad when it is bad.
    • That’s \(gb\text{,}\) the always-tell-the-truth strategy. Check the second letter: in \(gg\) it’s a \(g\text{,}\) so he says “good” even on a bad car.
    • Nature has made the car good on both of its branches.
    • The letters in a strategy describe what the player does, not what Nature did. Nature still picks a good car with probability \(p\) and a bad one with probability \(1-p\text{,}\) no matter which strategy the salesman uses.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.2.3.

    Now the other player. What does the customer’s strategy \(gb\) tell her to do?
    🔗
    • If the salesman says the car is good, conclude it’s good; if he says it’s bad, conclude it’s bad. In other words, always believe him.
    • Correct. Her letters are answers to “what do I do if he says good” and “what do I do if he says bad,” in that order.
    • Conclude the car is good when it really is good, and bad when it really is bad. In other words, get it right every time.
    • This is the one to watch out for. The customer can’t see the car, so she has no strategy that keys off whether it’s actually good. All she observes is what the salesman said, so that’s what her two letters respond to. Compare this with the salesman, whose letters do key off Nature’s move, since he can see it.
    • Conclude the car is good, no matter what the salesman says.
    • That’s her \(gg\) strategy. In \(gb\) the second letter is a \(b\text{,}\) so if he says the car is bad, she goes along with it.
    🔗
    Let’s put the two together and follow a strategy profile through the tree. Take \((gg, gb)\text{,}\) where the salesman always says the car is good and the customer always believes him. Remember the salesman is Player 1, so his strategy is listed first.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.2.4.

    Suppose the players use \((gg, gb)\) and Nature makes the car bad. What are the payoffs?
    🔗
    • \((2,0)\)
    • Correct. He says the car is good, she believes him and buys it, so he collects 2 points for the sale. He doesn’t get the truth point, because the car was bad. She’s wrong about the car, so she gets 0.
    • \((3,0)\)
    • He does sell the car, which is worth 2 points. But the extra point is for telling the truth, and he said a bad car was good. Follow the \(b\) branch out of Nature and then the \(g\) branch out of \(S\) to see this on the tree.
    • \((0,1)\)
    • This is what happens if the customer decides the car is bad and walks away. But her strategy here is \(gb\text{,}\) and he said “good,” so she uses her first letter and concludes the car is good.
    🔗
    That’s one of Nature’s two branches. The profile \((gg, gb)\) doesn’t pick out a single path through the tree, though, because Nature could go either way. The widget below puts both paths side by side and asks what we can say about the payoffs.
    🔗
    You just built the payoff for one strategy profile out of two paths and their probabilities. There are sixteen profiles in this game, and every one of them works the same way. The rest of Section 4.4 does exactly that and collects the results into a payoff matrix.
    🔗

    Section 4.4, Building the Payoff Matrix.

    Pick up where you stopped and read through the paragraph beginning “Let us assume \(p \gt \frac{1}{2}\text{.}\)” Right after that paragraph you’ll see the payoff matrix a second time with boxes already drawn on it. Stop before that one, because you’re about to draw those boxes yourself.
    🔗
    • The book traces the profile \((gg, bg)\) through its two paths, \(ggb\) and \(bgb\text{,}\) the same way we just did for \((gg, gb)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Then comes the shortcut worth remembering: write down the payoff matrix for a good car and the payoff matrix for a bad car separately, then combine them as \(p\) times the first plus \(1-p\) times the second.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The last paragraph you’ll read explains why we assume \(p \gt \frac{1}{2}\) and reminds you how best response boxing works. We covered boxing back in Section 3.9.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Now let’s find the Nash equilibria. In the widget below you’ll box the customer’s best responses one row at a time. Keep in mind that we’re assuming \(p \gt \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) and that some rows may have more than one best response in them.
    🔗
    Notice that \((gg, gb)\) is one of the two equilibria, which is the same profile we traced through the tree earlier.
    🔗

    Section 4.4, Wrapping Up.

    Read the last two paragraphs of Section 4.4. We just did this work, so you can move through it quickly, but there are two things in there worth collecting.
    🔗
    • The two equilibria pay the customer the same amount, but the salesman prefers the first one.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Neither player needs to know the exact value of \(p\text{.}\) Knowing that \(p \gt \frac{1}{2}\) is enough to find the equilibria. That answers the question the section raised at the very beginning, when it said we’d come back to whether anyone needs to know these probabilities.
