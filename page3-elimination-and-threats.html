    Section 3.3 Elimination Revisited, and Threats and Promises Again

    Earlier we saw that in the Prisoner’s Dilemma, \(\text{talk}\) strictly dominates \(\text{refuse}\) for both executives, so eliminating dominated strategies would have landed on \((\text{talk}, \text{talk})\text{,}\) which is exactly the Nash equilibrium we found by inspection. In Section 3.6, you will read some theorems showing this is not a coincidence!! However, the connection between Nash equilibria and iterated elimination also has some nuances to pay attention to.
    🔗

    Section 3.6, Finding Nash Equilibria by Iterated Elimination of Dominated Strategies.

    Read everything before the proof in Section 3.6. Reading the proof is optional. If you are a math major or particularly interested in game theory, you should read the proof.
    🔗
    You’ll be reading about connections between iterated elimination and Nash equilibria in Theorem 3.1 (for iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies) and then in Theorem 3.2 (for iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies). Pay attention to the similarities and differences between the statements.
    🔗
    🔗
    In both theorems, we take a game \(G\text{,}\) run iterated elimination on it, and call the reduced game we end up with \(H\text{.}\) The main question is whether Nash equilibria are preserved along the way, which really splits into two questions:
    🔗
    • Is every Nash equilibrium of \(H\) a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Is every Nash equilibrium of \(G\) a Nash equilibrium of \(H\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    The answers depend on whether you eliminate weakly or strictly dominated strategies! After reading through the statements of Theorems 3.1 and 3.2, answer the following:
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.1.

    You run iterated elimination on a game \(G\) and get a reduced game \(H\text{.}\) What is the difference between having used strict dominance and having used weak dominance?
    🔗
    • With weak dominance, \(H\) may contain a Nash equilibrium that was not a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{.}\)
    • Not quite, and this is the direction that is safe in both cases. Theorem 3.1 says explicitly that every Nash equilibrium of \(H\) is also one of \(G\text{,}\) weak dominance included. The danger runs the other way: an equilibrium of \(G\) can go missing.
    • Either way, every Nash equilibrium of the reduced game \(H\) is also a Nash equilibrium of the original game \(G\text{.}\) But with weak dominance, \(G\) may have equilibria that no longer appear in \(H\text{,}\) while with strict dominance it cannot.
    • Correct. Both theorems give you the “nothing false is introduced” direction. Only strict dominance gives you the “nothing is lost” direction: by statement (2) of Theorem 3.2, an equilibrium of \(G\) never uses an eliminated strategy, so it is still in \(H\text{,}\) and it is still an equilibrium there because players have fewer strategies to deviate to.
    • There is no real difference: either way, \(G\) and \(H\) have exactly the same Nash equilibria.
    • That is true for strict dominance, but not for weak dominance. Weak elimination can delete an equilibrium of \(G\) along the way, and you will see exactly this happen with Big Monkey and Little Monkey in Section 3.7.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.2.

    Statement (2) of Theorem 3.2 says that each strategy eliminated in the course of iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies is not part of any Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{.}\) What does that let you conclude about a strategy profile \(s = (s_1, \ldots, s_n)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • If even one player’s strategy \(s_i\) was eliminated, then the whole profile \(s\) fails to be a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) no matter what the other players are doing.
    • Correct. A single eliminated component is enough to disqualify the entire profile, which is why statement (2) is such a strong claim. It is also what lets us conclude that every Nash equilibrium of \(G\) is built entirely out of surviving strategies, and so is still sitting there in \(H\text{.}\)
    • If \(s\) survives to the reduced game \(H\text{,}\) then \(s\) is a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{.}\)
    • That is not statement (2), and it is not true either. Surviving elimination does not make a profile an equilibrium; plenty of profiles in \(H\) are not equilibria of anything. Statement (3) is the one about profiles, and it applies to Nash equilibria of \(H\text{,}\) not to every profile of \(H\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.3.

    Suppose iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies reduces a game \(G\) all the way down to a single strategy profile \((s_1^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\text{.}\) What can you conclude?
    🔗
    • It is a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) but \(G\) may well have others that the elimination missed.
    • This is the right conclusion for weak elimination, not strict. Under strict elimination nothing gets missed, since statement (2) rules out the eliminated strategies appearing in any equilibrium.
    • It is the unique Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) though it need not be a strict one.
    • Uniqueness is right, but you get strictness for free here as well. Every alternative strategy was eliminated, and the comparisons behind a strict elimination are all strict inequalities.
    • It is a strict Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) and \(G\) has no other Nash equilibria.
    • Correct, and this is the best possible outcome of a reduction. The “no others” half comes from statement (2): any other equilibrium would have to use a strategy that got eliminated, and those cannot appear in equilibria at all.
    🔗
    And here is a quick summary of some of the conclusions!
    🔗
    • With either kind of elimination, every Nash equilibrium of \(H\) is a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{.}\) Elimination never introduces a false equilibrium.
      🔗
      🔗
    • With strict elimination, every Nash equilibrium of \(G\) also survives to \(H\text{,}\) so \(G\) and \(H\) have exactly the same Nash equilibria. With weak elimination, equilibria of \(G\) can be lost along the way.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If strict elimination leaves a single profile, it is the unique Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) and a strict one.
      🔗
      🔗
    Weak elimination can lose you equilibria; strict elimination cannot.
    🔗
    Now we apply these theorems to one of our favorite games :)
    🔗

    Section 3.7, Big Monkey and Little Monkey 4: Threats, Promises, and Commitments Revisited.

    Read Section 3.7.
    🔗
    This is a short section that connects the elimination of weakly dominated strategies in the normal form of Big Monkey and Little Monkey to its Nash equilibria.
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is the matrix again for reference, with Big Monkey moving first, as it appeared in Section 2.10 and in the two elimination interactives from Chapter 2.
    🔗
    Some of the Nash equilibria here can be deduced from the reduced games found in Section 2.11 of the textbook. That is, \((w, cw)\) and \((w, cc)\) are Nash equilibria of the reduced games from Section 2.11, so by Theorem 3.1 they are guaranteed to be Nash equilibria of the original game. However, \((c, ww)\) is another Nash equilibrium!! It did not appear in either reduced game, so this is exactly the kind of equilibrium weak elimination can lose.
    🔗
    The book verifies \((c, ww)\) for us in words, and its argument needs reasoning for both players:
    🔗
    • If Little Monkey commits in advance to waiting no matter what Big Monkey does, then Big Monkey can do no better than to climb.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If Big Monkey climbs, then Little Monkey can do no better than to wait, which is what \(ww\) has her do.
      🔗
      🔗
    Those two sentences together are the definition of a Nash equilibrium applied to this profile. To practice this reasoning, try it out for the other two equilibria in the following example!
    🔗

    Example 3.3.5. Verifying the other two equilibria.

    For each profile, give the argument that it is a Nash equilibrium: one statement about Big Monkey’s incentive and one about Little Monkey’s, in the same style as the book’s argument for \((c, ww)\text{.}\) Cite the payoffs from the matrix above.
    🔗

    (a) The promise, \((w, cc)\).

    Show that \((w, cc)\) is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Solution.
    • If Little Monkey commits in advance to climbing no matter what Big Monkey does, then Big Monkey can do no better than to wait: waiting pays him \(9\) and climbing pays him \(5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If Big Monkey waits, then Little Monkey can do no better than to climb, which is what \(cc\) has her do: across the \(w\) row her payoffs are \(0, 0, 1, 1\text{,}\) and \(cc\) gives her one of the two best.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Backward induction’s answer, \((w, cw)\).

    Show that \((w, cw)\) is a Nash equilibrium. Then compare your argument to the one for \((w, cc)\text{:}\) what changed?
    🔗
    Solution.
    • If Little Monkey commits in advance to \(cw\text{,}\) then Big Monkey can do no better than to wait: waiting pays him \(9\) and climbing pays him \(4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If Big Monkey waits, then Little Monkey can do no better than \(cw\text{:}\) across the \(w\) row her payoffs are \(0, 0, 1, 1\text{,}\) and \(cw\) gives her one of the two best.
      🔗
      🔗
    The only thing that changed is Big Monkey’s alternative payoff, \(4\) instead of \(5\text{,}\) and either way it loses to \(9\text{.}\) Little Monkey’s half is identical: the same row, the same payoffs, and the same tie between \(cw\) and \(cc\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice \((w, cw)\) and \((w, cc)\) are two different strategy profiles, but they pay both players identically and their verifications are nearly the same argument. What distinguishes them?
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.6.

    What is the actual difference between Little Monkey’s strategies \(cw\) and \(cc\text{,}\) given that Big Monkey is playing \(w\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • They differ in what Little Monkey does when Big Monkey waits, which is the branch that actually gets played.
    • Not quite; look at the labels again. Both \(cw\) and \(cc\) begin with \(c\text{,}\) which is Little Monkey’s move in response to Big Monkey waiting. It is the second letter, her response to Big Monkey climbing, where they part ways.
    • They differ only in what Little Monkey would do if Big Monkey climbed. Big Monkey does not climb, so that part of her strategy is never carried out and the two strategies produce identical play.
    • Correct, and this is the heart of Section 3.7. A strategy has to specify a move at every node, including nodes the game never actually reaches. Little Monkey’s promise costs her nothing precisely because it is never tested. This is also the honest answer to the book’s question of why the normal form has equilibria that backward induction does not find, and it is an idea we will return to in a later chapter.
    • Little Monkey prefers one of them, so she would choose that one if given the chance.
    • She is indifferent. Both give her \(1\) when Big Monkey waits. If she strictly preferred one of them, the other could not have been a Nash equilibrium in the first place.
    🔗
    Note that none of these three equilibria is strict, and Little Monkey’s payoffs are the reason. In the \(w\) row she is indifferent between \(cw\) and \(cc\text{;}\) in the \(c\) row she is indifferent between \(ww\) and \(cw\text{.}\) She is never made strictly worse off by deviating.
