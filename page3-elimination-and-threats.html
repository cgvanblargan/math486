    Section 3.3 Elimination Revisited, and Threats and Promises Again

    Back on the first page of this chapter we noticed something in passing. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, \(\text{talk}\) strictly dominates \(\text{refuse}\) for both executives, so eliminating dominated strategies would have landed on \((\text{talk}, \text{talk})\text{,}\) which is exactly the Nash equilibrium we found by inspection. That was left as an observation. Section 3.6 turns it into a theorem, and tells us how far it can be pushed.
    🔗
    This matters more than it might look. We spent all of Chapter 2 eliminating dominated strategies without ever saying what the surviving strategies were for. The answer, it turns out, is that they are where the Nash equilibria live, at least if we are careful about which kind of dominance we used.
    🔗

    Section 3.6, Finding Nash Equilibria by Iterated Elimination of Dominated Strategies.

    Read Section 3.6 up through the end of the paragraph beginning “In contrast,” just after Theorem 3.2.
    🔗
    That gets you both theorem statements and the discussion around them, which is all we will use. The rest of the section is the proof. You may skip the proof entirely. Read it if you are interested, and do come back to it if you ever want to see how an induction on “the \(k\)th strategy eliminated” is set up, but nothing on this page or in your homework depends on it.
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s collect what those two theorems actually say. Both of them start the same way: you take a game \(G\text{,}\) run iterated elimination on it, and call the smaller game you end up with \(H\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Theorem 3.1 covers weak elimination. It says that every Nash equilibrium of \(H\) is still a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) and in particular, if \(H\) is a single strategy profile, then that profile is a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{.}\) That second part is just the statement that a dominant strategy equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium.
    🔗
    Theorem 3.2 covers strict elimination, and it says considerably more. Statement (1) is that the order of elimination does not matter; you reach the same \(H\) no matter how you get there. Statement (2) is that any strategy you eliminated along the way was not part of any Nash equilibrium of \(G\) to begin with. Statement (3) is the same conclusion as Theorem 3.1, that equilibria of \(H\) are equilibria of \(G\text{.}\) And statement (4) says that if \(H\) is a single strategy profile, that profile is a strict Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) and there are no others.
    🔗
    Statement (1) should feel familiar. Back on the last page of Chapter 2 we worked out by hand why an elimination that is available under strict dominance stays available, and why that is exactly what fails for weak dominance. That argument is what statement (1) is asserting in general.
    🔗
    Statement (2) is the genuinely new one, and its idea is worth having even though you are skipping the proof. Suppose \(s_i\) was eliminated because \(t_i\) strictly dominates it, and suppose \(s_i\) shows up somewhere in a strategy profile. Swap \(s_i\) for \(t_i\) and leave everyone else alone. Player \(i\)’s payoff goes up, so that profile was never a Nash equilibrium. Notice where strictness earns its keep: with weak dominance that swap only gets you \(\geq\text{,}\) and \(\geq\) is precisely what a Nash equilibrium is allowed to have.
    🔗
    Now here is the punchline, which the book states in words rather than as a numbered statement, so it is easy to read past. Put statement (2) together with statement (3):
    🔗
    If you use iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, then \(G\) and \(H\) have exactly the same Nash equilibria.
    🔗
    One direction is statement (3) itself. For the other direction, take a Nash equilibrium \(s^*\) of \(G\text{.}\) By statement (2) none of its pieces \(s_i^*\) were ever eliminated, so \(s^*\) is still a strategy profile of the smaller game \(H\text{.}\) And it is still an equilibrium there for a slightly boring reason: \(H\) gives each player fewer strategies to deviate to than \(G\) did, so a profile that survived every deviation in \(G\) certainly survives the shorter list in \(H\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So for strict elimination, shrinking the game costs you nothing at all. Find the equilibria of \(H\) and you have found every equilibrium of \(G\text{.}\) This is the practical reason we bother eliminating in the first place.
    🔗
    For weak elimination, only one direction survives. Nash equilibria of \(H\) are still equilibria of \(G\text{,}\) so nothing false gets introduced, but equilibria of \(G\) can be destroyed on the way down. Weak elimination can lose you equilibria; strict elimination cannot. Keep that sentence somewhere handy, because it is the one result from this section you will actually use.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.1.

    You run iterated elimination on a game \(G\) and get a reduced game \(H\text{.}\) What is the difference between having used strict dominance and having used weak dominance?
    🔗
    • Either way, every Nash equilibrium of \(H\) is one of \(G\text{.}\) But with weak dominance, \(G\) may have equilibria that no longer appear in \(H\text{,}\) while with strict dominance it cannot.
    • Correct. Both theorems give you the “nothing false is introduced” direction. Only strict dominance gives you the “nothing is lost” direction, and that is statement (2) of Theorem 3.2 doing the work.
    • With weak dominance, \(H\) may contain a Nash equilibrium that was not a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{.}\)
    • Not quite, and this is the direction that is safe in both cases. Theorem 3.1 says explicitly that every Nash equilibrium of \(H\) is also one of \(G\text{,}\) weak dominance included. The danger runs the other way: an equilibrium of \(G\) can go missing.
    • With weak dominance you can only eliminate one strategy per player, while with strict dominance you can keep going.
    • Not quite. Nothing limits how many strategies you can eliminate in either case. We eliminated repeatedly with weak dominance all through Chapter 2.
    • With weak dominance the payoffs in the surviving cells change, which is why the equilibria can differ.
    • Not quite. Eliminating a strategy just deletes a row or a column, and every payoff still showing is exactly what it was before. What changes is which comparisons you are still able to make.
    🔗
    The next check is about reading statement (2) carefully, since it is phrased in terms of a single player’s strategy rather than a whole profile.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.2.

    Statement (2) of Theorem 3.2 says that each strategy eliminated in the course of iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies is not part of any Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{.}\) What does that let you conclude about a strategy profile \(s = (s_1, \ldots, s_n)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • If even one player’s strategy \(s_i\) was eliminated, then the whole profile \(s\) fails to be a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) no matter what the other players are doing.
    • Correct. A single eliminated component is enough to disqualify the entire profile, which is why statement (2) is such a strong claim. It is also what lets us conclude that every Nash equilibrium of \(G\) is built entirely out of surviving strategies, and so is still sitting there in \(H\text{.}\)
    • If every player’s strategy \(s_i\) was eliminated, then \(s\) fails to be a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{.}\) If only some were eliminated, the statement says nothing.
    • Not quite, and this reads the statement as weaker than it is. The claim is about each eliminated strategy on its own: any profile containing it is disqualified. One bad component is enough.
    • If \(s\) survives to the reduced game \(H\text{,}\) then \(s\) is a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{.}\)
    • That is not statement (2), and it is not true either. Surviving elimination does not make a profile an equilibrium; plenty of profiles in \(H\) are not equilibria of anything. Statement (3) is the one about profiles, and it applies to Nash equilibria of \(H\text{,}\) not to every profile of \(H\text{.}\)
    • An eliminated strategy \(s_i\) is never a best response to anything, so no player would ever use it.
    • This is stronger than what statement (2) claims, and it is a different kind of statement. Statement (2) rules out eliminated strategies appearing in Nash equilibria; it does not talk about best responses at all. We will get to best responses in the next section.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.3.

    Suppose iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies reduces a game \(G\) all the way down to a single strategy profile \((s_1^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\text{.}\) What can you conclude?
    🔗
    • It is a strict Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) and \(G\) has no other Nash equilibria.
    • Correct, and this is the best possible outcome of a reduction. The “no others” half comes from statement (2): any other equilibrium would have to use a strategy that got eliminated, and those cannot appear in equilibria at all.
    • It is a Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) but \(G\) may well have others that the elimination missed.
    • This is the right conclusion for weak elimination, not strict. Under strict elimination nothing gets missed, since statement (2) rules out the eliminated strategies appearing in any equilibrium.
    • It is the unique Nash equilibrium of \(G\text{,}\) though it need not be a strict one.
    • Uniqueness is right, but you get strictness for free here as well. Every alternative strategy was eliminated, and the comparisons behind a strict elimination are all strict inequalities.
    • It is a Nash equilibrium of the reduced game \(H\text{,}\) but you cannot say anything about \(G\) without checking directly.
    • Not quite. Transferring conclusions from \(H\) back to \(G\) is the entire purpose of these two theorems, so that you do not have to go back and check the original game by hand.
    🔗
    Now we put the theorems to work on a game we know very well, and something interesting happens.

    Section 3.7, Big Monkey and Little Monkey 4: Threats, Promises, and Commitments Revisited.

    Read Section 3.7.
    🔗
    This is a short section with no new machinery in it, and it closes a loop we opened all the way back in Section 1.6. It uses the same normal form matrix you worked with in Chapter 2, and both of the elimination orders you stepped through there. Read it properly rather than skimming; the payoff is conceptual rather than computational.
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is the matrix again for reference, with Big Monkey moving first, as it appeared in Section 2.10 and in the two elimination interactives from Chapter 2.
    🔗
    In Chapter 2 we ran iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies on this matrix in two different orders. The first order took us all the way down to the single profile \((w, cw)\text{.}\) The second order stopped at a \(2 \times 1\) game, leaving both \((w, cw)\) and \((w, cc)\) standing. At the time, the point was that the two orders disagreed. Now we have the vocabulary to say what that disagreement cost us.
    🔗
    Since these were weak eliminations, Theorem 3.1 applies and Theorem 3.2 does not. So we know that \((w, cw)\) and \((w, cc)\) really are Nash equilibria of the original game, and we know we have no guarantee of having found them all. And in fact we have not: \((c, ww)\) is a third Nash equilibrium, and no order of weak elimination will ever produce it.
    🔗
    Those three profiles are worth naming in words, because we have met all of them before under different descriptions.
    🔗
    • \((w, cw)\) is the backward induction outcome from Chapter 1. Big Monkey waits, Little Monkey climbs, and the payoffs are \((9, 1)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \((w, cc)\) contains Little Monkey’s promise from Section 1.6: she will climb even if Big Monkey climbs. The payoffs are again \((9, 1)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \((c, ww)\) contains Little Monkey’s threat from Section 1.6: she will wait even if Big Monkey waits. The payoffs are \((4, 4)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Look at the payoffs in that list. The threat changes the outcome of the game and the promise does not, which is exactly what Section 1.6 told us without the language of Nash equilibria. Hold onto that; we will come back to it at the bottom of the page.
    🔗
    The book verifies \((c, ww)\) for us in words, and its argument has two halves, one per player. If Little Monkey commits in advance to waiting no matter what Big Monkey does, then Big Monkey can do no better than to climb. And if Big Monkey climbs, then Little Monkey can do no better than to wait, which is what \(ww\) has her do. Those two sentences together are the definition of a Nash equilibrium applied to this profile.
    🔗
    You are going to write the same kind of argument for the other two equilibria. Work each one out before revealing the answer.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.5. Verifying the promise, \((w, cc)\).

    Write the two-sentence argument showing that \((w, cc)\) is a Nash equilibrium: one sentence about Big Monkey’s incentive, one about Little Monkey’s. Use the same shape as the book’s argument for \((c, ww)\text{,}\) and cite the payoffs from the matrix above.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Check one player at a time, holding the other one fixed. For Big Monkey, read down the \(cc\) column and compare his two payoffs. For Little Monkey, read across the \(w\) row and compare her four.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.6. Verifying backward induction’s answer, \((w, cw)\).

    Now do the same for \((w, cw)\text{.}\) Then answer a second question: comparing your argument to the one you just wrote for \((w, cc)\text{,}\) what actually changed and what did not?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Big Monkey is now looking down the \(cw\) column instead of the \(cc\) column. Little Monkey is reading across the same row she was before.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    That last observation deserves a name. \((w, cw)\) and \((w, cc)\) are two genuinely different strategy profiles, and yet they pay both players identically and their verifications are the same argument twice. Something must distinguish them, so what is it?
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.7.

    What is the actual difference between Little Monkey’s strategies \(cw\) and \(cc\text{,}\) given that Big Monkey is playing \(w\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • They differ only in what Little Monkey would do if Big Monkey climbed. Big Monkey does not climb, so that part of her strategy is never carried out and the two strategies produce identical play.
    • Correct, and this is the heart of Section 3.7. A strategy has to specify a move at every node, including nodes the game never actually reaches. Little Monkey’s promise costs her nothing precisely because it is never tested. This is also the honest answer to the book’s question of why the normal form has equilibria that backward induction does not find, and it is an idea we will return to in a later chapter.
    • One of them is a strict Nash equilibrium and the other is not.
    • Neither is strict, in fact. In both cases Little Monkey ties: her payoff in the \(w\) row is \(1\) for \(cw\) and \(1\) for \(cc\text{,}\) so switching between them costs her nothing. That tie is the reason neither equilibrium is strict.
    • Little Monkey prefers one of them, so she would choose that one if given the chance.
    • She is indifferent. Both give her \(1\) when Big Monkey waits. If she strictly preferred one of them, the other could not have been a Nash equilibrium in the first place.
    • They differ in what Little Monkey does when Big Monkey waits, which is the branch that actually gets played.
    • Not quite; look at the labels again. Both \(cw\) and \(cc\) begin with \(c\text{,}\) which is Little Monkey’s move in response to Big Monkey waiting. It is the second letter, her response to Big Monkey climbing, where they part ways.
    🔗
    One more thing worth noticing before we move on, since it will matter on the next page. None of these three equilibria is strict, and Little Monkey is the reason every time. In the \(w\) row she is indifferent between \(cw\) and \(cc\text{;}\) in the \(c\) row she is indifferent between \(ww\) and \(cw\text{.}\) She is never made strictly worse off by deviating.
    🔗
    Notice in particular that \(cw\) is among her best replies in both of Big Monkey’s rows. Keep an eye on that when we start drawing boxes in Section 3.9.
