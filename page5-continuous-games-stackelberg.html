    Section 1.5 Continuous Games and Stackelberg’s Duopoly Part 1

    So far, every game we’ve looked at has had a finite menu of choices at each node. In this section we’ll look at what happens when a player can instead choose any real number over an interval. The method is still backward induction, but carrying it out will take some calculus.
    🔗

    Section 1.9, Continuous Games.

    Read Section 1.9.
    🔗
    Pay attention to how backward induction gets adapted here. It’s the same core idea as before, but instead of a finite number of nodes to compare, you have to consider values over an interval. We’ll look at an example before moving on to the next section.
    🔗
    🔗
    In the next section you’ll read about Stackelberg’s model of duopoly, which deals with two firms choosing how much of a good to produce. Firm 1 picks its production level \(s\) first; then Firm 2 observes \(s\) and picks its own production level \(t\text{.}\) Each firm’s profit, \(\pi_1(s,t)\) and \(\pi_2(s,t)\text{,}\) depends on both quantities together, since the two firms are competing in the same market.
    🔗
    For now we’ll take the profit functions as given. In the book’s numerical example they work out to
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \pi_1(s,t) \amp = (16 - 2t)s - 2s^2,\\ \pi_2(s,t) \amp = (16 - 2s)t - 2t^2. \end{align*}
    When you get to the reading you’ll see where those formulas come from, and we’ll take a closer look at them afterward. First, let’s just do the backward induction.
    🔗
    Since Firm 1 moves first, it has to reason about how Firm 2 will rationally respond to every production level Firm 1 could possibly choose. In other words, before Firm 1 picks anything, we need Firm 2’s best-response function \(t = b(s)\text{:}\) the value of \(t\) that maximizes Firm 2’s profit, for each fixed \(s\) Firm 1 might choose.
    🔗

    Example 1.5.1. Refresher: when does setting a derivative to zero find a maximum?

    Below is a calculus review on using derivatives to find a maximum. You can work through it now or come back to it later, though it’s worth at least skimming before going on.
    🔗
    We’re about to maximize a function by setting a derivative equal to zero. Before we do: a critical point isn’t automatically a maximum. What has to be true for that shortcut to be valid, and what does it mean to do this to a function of two variables?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Shape of the function. If the function is concave (its graph curves downward, like an upside-down parabola) over the whole domain, a critical point is guaranteed to be the single global maximum. Otherwise a critical point could instead be a minimum, an inflection point, or just one of several local maxima.
    🔗
    The domain. If the domain has boundaries (a variable can’t go below \(0\text{,}\) say), the true maximum might sit right at that boundary instead of at a point where the derivative is zero. Boundary points have to be checked separately in that case.
    🔗
    Two variables. For a function like \(\pi_2(s,t)\text{,}\) the same idea applies to the partial derivative: setting \(\partial \pi_2/\partial t = 0\) (holding \(s\) fixed) finds a critical point of the one-variable curve you get by slicing the surface at that particular \(s\text{.}\) Everything you know about one-variable max/min problems applies to that slice.
    🔗
    Both conditions hold for us here, which is why the work below is as short as it is. Keep them in mind anyway: when the model gets more realistic in Subsection 1.10.2, the domain grows a boundary and this stops being automatic.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In the interactive below, drag the slider to set Firm 1’s production level \(s\text{.}\) The left panel shows the profit surface \(\pi_2(s,t)\text{,}\) sliced at your chosen \(s\text{;}\) the right panel shows that same slice as a clean 2D curve of \(\pi_2\) versus \(t\text{.}\) Here the curve is concave and the domain is unrestricted, so no boundary check is needed and the point where the tangent line is flat (\(\partial \pi_2/\partial t = 0\)) is guaranteed to be the actual maximum. That peak is Firm 2’s best response \(b(s)\) to your chosen \(s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Watching the peak slide as you drag \(s\) is the whole content of the best-response function: every position of the slider gives one value of \(b(s)\text{.}\) The example below shows how to use calculus to find a formula for \(b(s)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.5.3. Deriving Firm 2’s best response \(b(s)\).

    Hold \(s\) fixed and find the value of \(t\) that maximizes \(\pi_2(s,t) = (16 - 2s)t - 2t^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(s\) held fixed, this is a quadratic in \(t\) whose \(t^2\) coefficient is \(-2\text{,}\) so its graph is an upside-down parabola no matter what \(s\) is. By the refresher above, a critical point is therefore the global maximum, and we can find it by setting the partial derivative equal to zero:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial \pi_2}{\partial t} = 16 - 2s - 4t = 0. \end{equation*}
    Solving for \(t\) gives Firm 2’s best-response function:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} t = b(s) = 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s. \end{equation*}
    This is a strategy, not a single move: it tells Firm 2 what to do at every node it could find itself at. Check it against the interactive by setting the slider to \(s = 4\) and confirming the peak sits at \(t = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.4.

    To find \(b(s)\text{,}\) we held \(s\) fixed and set \(\partial \pi_2/\partial t = 0\text{,}\) then stopped. What justifies stopping there, rather than doing more work to confirm we found a maximum?
    🔗
    • For any fixed \(s\text{,}\) the function \(\pi_2 = (16-2s)t - 2t^2\) is a quadratic in \(t\) with a negative \(t^2\) coefficient, so every slice is an upside-down parabola with one global maximum, and \(t\) ranges over all real numbers, so there’s no boundary to check.
    • Correct. Both conditions from the refresher hold, and notice the \(t^2\) coefficient is \(-2\) regardless of \(s\text{,}\) so this argument covers every slice at once, not just the one currently on your screen.
    • Setting a derivative equal to zero always finds a maximum.
    • Not quite. It finds a critical point, which could be a minimum or an inflection point. We need the concavity to know which one we got.
    • The interactive shows a peak, so we can see the maximum is there.
    • The picture is good evidence, but it only shows finitely many values of \(s\) over a limited window. The concavity argument is what covers every \(s\) at once.
    🔗
    Now that Firm 2’s best response is pinned down for every possible \(s\text{,}\) Firm 1 can work out its own payoff for each \(s\) it might choose. Replacing \(t\) with \(b(s)\) gives \(\pi_1(s, b(s))\text{,}\) a function of \(s\) alone. Find its formula in the checkpoint below.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.5.

    Substitute \(b(s) = 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s\) into \(\pi_1(s,t) = (16 - 2t)s - 2s^2\text{.}\) Which of the following is \(\pi_1\bigl(s, b(s)\bigr)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(\pi_1\bigl(s, b(s)\bigr) = 8s - s^2\)
    • Correct. The coefficient becomes \(16 - 2\left(4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s\right) = 8 + s\text{,}\) so \(\pi_1 = (8+s)s - 2s^2 = 8s + s^2 - 2s^2 = 8s - s^2\text{.}\) Notice that \(t\) is gone entirely: this is a function of \(s\) alone.
    • \(\pi_1\bigl(s, b(s)\bigr) = 8s - 3s^2\)
    • Check how the \(-2\) distributes across \(4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s\text{.}\) Both terms change sign: \(-2\left(4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s\right) = -8 + s\text{,}\) not \(-8 - s\text{.}\)
    • \(\pi_1\bigl(s, b(s)\bigr) = 32 - 8s + \tfrac{1}{2}s^2\)
    • This is what you get by substituting \(b(s)\) into \(\pi_2\text{,}\) not \(\pi_1\text{.}\) That’s a meaningful quantity in its own right (it’s what Firm 2 earns as a function of \(s\)), but Firm 1 is maximizing its own profit, so \(b(s)\) goes into the \(t\) slot of \(\pi_1\text{.}\)
    • \(\pi_1\bigl(s, b(s)\bigr) = 16s - 2s^2\)
    • This is \(\pi_1(s,0)\text{:}\) it drops the \(-2t\) term rather than replacing \(t\) with \(4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s\text{.}\) Firm 2’s production doesn’t disappear, it just becomes a function of \(s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So \(\pi_1\bigl(s, b(s)\bigr) = 8s - s^2\text{.}\) This gives Firm 1’s payoff for each chosen \(s\text{,}\) assuming Firm 2 always answers with its best response \(b(s)\text{.}\) It’s a function of one variable now, and maximizing it is Firm 1’s whole problem. Take a look at its graph below.
    🔗
    The slider shows you roughly where the peak sits. Try pinning it down exactly yourself before opening the solution below. The hint walks through what to do once you have \(s^*\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.5.7. Finding Firm 1’s optimal production level \(s^*\).

    Maximize \(\pi_1(s, b(s)) = 8s - s^2\text{,}\) then work out what both firms actually produce and earn.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Setting \(\frac{d}{ds}\pi_1\bigl(s,b(s)\bigr) = 0\) gives you \(s^*\text{,}\) Firm 1’s production level. From there:
    🔗
    • Plug \(s^*\) into \(b(s) = 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}s\) to get \(t^*\text{,}\) Firm 2’s production level.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Then plug both \(s^*\) and \(t^*\) into the original \(\pi_1(s,t)\) and \(\pi_2(s,t)\) to get each firm’s payoff.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The \(s^2\) coefficient is negative, so this is an upside-down parabola and the derivative test suffices:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{ds}\,\pi_1\bigl(s, b(s)\bigr) = 8 - 2s = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s^* = 4. \end{equation*}
    Firm 2 then responds with \(t^* = b(4) = 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}(4) = 2\text{,}\) the price is \(p^* = 20 - 2(4 + 2) = 8\text{,}\) and the profits are
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \pi_1(s^*,t^*) \amp = (16 - 2 \cdot 2)4 - 2 \cdot 4^2 = 16,\\ \pi_2(s^*,t^*) \amp = (16 - 2 \cdot 4)2 - 2 \cdot 2^2 = 8. \end{align*}
    Firm 1 produces twice as much and earns twice as much. It is better to be the firm that moves first. That’s worth pausing on, since in the Ultimatum Game moving first was also an advantage, but for a completely different reason.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    You’ve now walked through the entire backward induction argument:
    🔗
    1. Solve Firm 2’s problem first, giving the best-response function \(b(s)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Then write out \(\pi_1(s, b(s))\text{,}\) Firm 1’s payoff function assuming Firm 2 always responds rationally to each \(s\) value.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Last, find the \(s^*\) that maximizes \(\pi_1(s, b(s))\text{.}\) Then you know Firm 1 chooses \(s^*\) and Firm 2 chooses \(t^* = b(s^*)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    What we haven’t done is say where \(\pi_1\) and \(\pi_2\) came from in the first place. That’s the reading.
    🔗

    Section 1.10 through Subsection 1.10.1, Stackelberg’s Model of Duopoly.

    Read Section 1.10 up through Subsection 1.10.1. Stop before Subsection 1.10.2.
    🔗
    The backward induction in 1.10.1 is the argument we just did, so you can move through it quickly and use it to check your understanding. What’s genuinely new is the setup before the calculus starts: the price rule, the cost functions, and how those combine into \(\pi_1\) and \(\pi_2\text{.}\) Read that part slowly.
    🔗
    Notice also that the domain here is left unrestricted on purpose: production levels are allowed to be any real numbers, even negative ones. That’s not realistic, and the book says so. It’s a deliberate simplification that keeps the concavity argument clean, with no boundary to check.
    🔗
    🔗
    Now let’s take the profit functions apart. Every Stackelberg-type model in this chapter is built from the same four moves:
    🔗
    1. Price rule. Total production is \(q = s + t\text{,}\) and price falls as \(q\) rises. In this example we have \(p = 20 - 2(s+t)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Revenue. Each firm sells its own quantity at that shared market price: \(ps\) for Firm 1, \(pt\) for Firm 2.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Cost. Both firms have unit cost \(4\text{,}\) so \(c_1(s) = 4s\) and \(c_2(t) = 4t\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Profit is revenue minus cost.
      🔗
      🔗
    Assembling those for Firm 1:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \pi_1(s,t) \;=\; \underbrace{\underbrace{\bigl[\,20 - 2(s+t)\,\bigr]}_{\text{price } p} \cdot \underbrace{s}_{\substack{\text{Firm 1's} \\ \text{quantity}}}}_{\text{revenue}} \;-\; \underbrace{4s}_{\text{cost } c_1(s)} \;=\; (16 - 2t)s - 2s^2. \end{equation*}
    Firm 2’s is the mirror image, with \(t\) in the role of \(s\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \pi_2(s,t) \;=\; \bigl[\,20 - 2(s+t)\,\bigr]t - 4t \;=\; (16 - 2s)t - 2t^2. \end{equation*}
    The single most important thing to see here is that \(s\) and \(t\) enter the price rule together, as a sum. Neither firm controls the price; they control it jointly, and that’s why one firm’s decision affects the other’s profit.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.8.

    Suppose we rerun the model with different numbers. Price falls according to \(p = 30 - 3(s+t)\text{,}\) and both firms now have a unit cost of production of \(6\text{.}\) Which of the following is Firm 2’s profit function, before any simplifying?
    🔗
    • \(\pi_2(s,t) = \bigl[30 - 3(s+t)\bigr]t - 6t\)
    • Correct. Price times Firm 2’s own quantity, minus Firm 2’s own cost. The rival’s production \(s\) shows up only inside the price, which is exactly the structure of the original model.
    • \(\pi_2(s,t) = \bigl[30 - 3(s+t)\bigr](s+t) - 6t\)
    • Not quite. This charges Firm 2 for its own production but credits it with revenue from everything both firms sold. Firm 2 only earns money on the \(t\) units it produced itself.
    • \(\pi_2(s,t) = \bigl[30 - 3t\bigr]t - 6t\)
    • Not quite. Dropping \(s\) from the price rule says Firm 1’s production has no effect on the market price. If that were true there’d be no game here at all, just two unrelated optimization problems.
    • \(\pi_2(s,t) = \bigl[30 - 3(s+t)\bigr]t - 6s\)
    • Not quite, check the cost term. Firm 2 pays to produce its own \(t\) units; Firm 1’s production bill is Firm 1’s problem.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.9.

    Firm 1 never chooses \(t\text{.}\) So why does \(t\) appear in Firm 1’s profit function \(\pi_1(s,t) = (16-2t)s - 2s^2\) at all?
    🔗
    • Both firms sell into the same market at a single price, and that price depends on the total quantity \(s + t\text{.}\) More production by Firm 2 pushes the price down, which lowers what Firm 1 earns on every unit it sells.
    • Correct, and this is the whole reason backward induction is needed here. If \(t\) didn’t appear in \(\pi_1\text{,}\) Firm 1 could just maximize its own profit directly and ignore Firm 2 entirely.
    • Because Firm 1 has to pay part of Firm 2’s production costs.
    • Not quite. Each firm pays only its own costs: \(c_1(s) = 4s\) for Firm 1 and \(c_2(t) = 4t\) for Firm 2. The link between the firms runs through the price, not through costs.
    • Because Firm 1 moves first, so it has to commit to a value of \(t\) on Firm 2’s behalf.
    • Not quite. Firm 1 only chooses \(s\text{.}\) It anticipates \(t\) through the best-response function \(b(s)\text{,}\) but it never chooses \(t\text{,}\) and \(t\) would sit in \(\pi_1\) even if the two firms moved simultaneously.
