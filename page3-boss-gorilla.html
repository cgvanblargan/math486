    Section 4.3 The Travails of Boss Gorilla

    Here’s another game with incomplete information. This time a gorilla shows up to challenge the boss, and the boss can’t tell whether he’s about to fight someone who will beat him.
    🔗

    Section 4.5, The Travails of Boss Gorilla 1.

    Read Section 4.5 up through the boxed payoff matrix and the paragraph right after it. Stop when you get to “We could have made the analysis a little easier.”
    🔗
    The method here is the same as Buying a Used Car, so this should feel familiar. Two things to make sure you follow: how the game tree turns into one matrix for a Tough visitor and one for a Weak visitor, and how those two combine into a single matrix as \(p\) times the first plus \(1-p\) times the second.
    🔗
    🔗
    The book spends its last page and a half explaining why \(cc\) strictly dominates \(lc\text{.}\) We can get there faster. Start with something the book points out early: Tough Gorilla should always challenge, since challenging gets him at least 3 and leaving gets him 0.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.3.1.

    Compare Visiting Gorilla’s strategies \(cc\) and \(lc\) on each of Nature’s two branches. What happens?
    🔗
    • When the visitor is Tough, \(cc\) challenges and \(lc\) leaves, so \(cc\) does strictly better. When the visitor is Weak, both challenge, so they tie.
    • Correct. The two strategies only disagree on the Tough branch, and that’s the branch where challenging is the better move. Against \(f\text{,}\) a Tough challenger wins the fight for 5 while leaving gets 0. Against \(a\text{,}\) he becomes co-boss for 3 while leaving still gets 0. On the Weak branch both strategies say \(c\text{,}\) so whatever happens there happens identically under both.
    • \(cc\) does strictly better on both branches.
    • Check the second letters. Both \(cc\) and \(lc\) tell a Weak visitor to challenge, so they can’t come apart there. They give the same payoff on that branch, not a better one.
    • It depends on whether Boss Gorilla is using \(f\) or \(a\text{.}\)
    • It doesn’t, and that’s what makes this work. On the Tough branch challenging beats leaving against \(f\) (5 versus 0) and against \(a\) (3 versus 0). Dominance has to hold against everything Boss Gorilla might do, and here it does.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.3.2.

    Visiting Gorilla’s actual payoff is the weighted average of his payoffs on the two branches, with weights \(p\) and \(1-p\text{.}\) Given what you just found, why does \(cc\) strictly dominate \(lc\) for every \(p\) with \(0 \lt p \lt 1\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • One branch is strictly better and the other ties, so the weighted average comes out strictly better no matter what the weights are.
    • Correct. Averaging can only hurt you if some branch pulls the other way, and here nothing does. Against \(f\text{,}\) for instance, \(cc\) gives \(p \cdot 5 + (1-p)(-1)\) and \(lc\) gives \(p \cdot 0 + (1-p)(-1)\text{.}\) The second terms are identical and the first favors \(cc\text{,}\) so the whole thing favors \(cc\text{.}\)
    • It only dominates when \(p\) is large enough, since the Tough branch needs enough weight to matter.
    • This would be right if the Weak branch pushed back, but it doesn’t. There’s no size of \(p\) that rescues \(lc\text{,}\) because the best it ever does is tie. Any \(p\) above 0 gives the Tough branch some weight, and that’s all we need.
    • It doesn’t strictly dominate. It just does better on average, which is a weaker statement.
    • The average is the payoff here. Once Nature is part of the game, a strategy’s payoff against \(f\) is a single number, the expected value across Nature’s branches, and \(cc\) beats \(lc\) at that number against both of Boss Gorilla’s strategies. That’s exactly strict dominance.
    🔗

    Section 4.5, The Rest.

    Read the rest of Section 4.5. You can move quickly, since it’s the argument you just made. It also does the same thing for \(cl\) over \(ll\text{.}\)
    🔗
    One warning: the book writes “Strong” a few times where it means “Tough.” Same gorilla.
    🔗
    🔗
    One last thing worth looking at. The matrix tells us \((f, cl)\) isn’t a Nash equilibrium, but it’s the profile students usually expect to be one. Boss Gorilla stands ready to fight, and only the Tough visitors bother him.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.3.3.

    Why isn’t \((f, cl)\) a Nash equilibrium?
    🔗
    • Under \(cl\text{,}\) only Tough visitors challenge. So the only time Boss Gorilla ever gets to fight is against a visitor who beats him, and he’d rather acquiesce.
    • Correct. Look at what his strategy costs him. On the Weak branch the visitor leaves and he collects 6 whether his strategy says \(f\) or \(a\text{,}\) so that branch doesn’t distinguish them at all. The only branch where his choice does anything is the Tough one, where fighting gets him \(-1\) and acquiescing gets him 3. That’s why he’s better off switching by \(4p\text{,}\) for every \(p\text{.}\)
    • Fighting is what keeps the Weak visitors away. If he switches to \(a\) they’ll start challenging too, so he should stay with \(f\text{.}\)
    • This is the tempting one, and it’s worth seeing why it fails. A strategy isn’t a threat he announces ahead of time. It’s what he actually does when a challenge arrives, and to check for a Nash equilibrium we hold the visitor’s strategy fixed at \(cl\) and ask whether Boss Gorilla wants to deviate. Holding \(cl\) fixed, the Weak visitors are leaving regardless. You saw this problem back in Chapter 1 with Big Monkey: a threat you’d never want to carry out isn’t credible.
    • It fails when \(p\) is small, since a Tough visitor is then too unlikely to be worth preparing for.
    • No value of \(p\) saves it. Compare the \(cl\) column: he gets \(6-7p\) from \(f\) and \(6-3p\) from \(a\text{,}\) so acquiescing wins by \(4p\text{,}\) which is positive for every \(p\) in \((0,1)\text{.}\) You may be thinking of the \(cc\) column, which is the one that genuinely depends on \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The equilibrium that survives is \((a, cc)\text{,}\) where everybody challenges and Boss Gorilla gives in. Notice what the Weak visitor is doing there. He challenges knowing he’d lose a fight, because he knows Boss Gorilla won’t start one.
