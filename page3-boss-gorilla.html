    Section 4.3 The Travails of Boss Gorilla

    Here’s another game with incomplete information. It’s fun and there are gorillas.
    🔗

    Section 4.5, The Travails of Boss Gorilla 1.

    Read Section 4.5 up through the boxed payoff matrix and the paragraph right after it. That’s all four payoff tables in the section plus one more paragraph, which finds the Nash equilibrium \((a, cc)\text{.}\) Don’t read past that yet!
    🔗
    The method is the same as in Buying a Used Car, so this should feel familiar. Focus on the matrices: make sure you can follow how the game tree splits into two matrices, one for each type of visitor, and how they combine into a single matrix of expected payoffs in terms of \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Now let’s spend some time understanding these results more intuitively. First, a quick reminder of what the strategies mean:
    \begin{equation*} \text{Visiting Gorilla: }\; \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{what he does}\\ \text{if he's Tough}}} \quad \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{what he does}\\ \text{if he's Weak}}} \qquad c = \text{challenge},\; l = \text{leave} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Boss Gorilla: }\; \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{what he does}\\ \text{if challenged}}} \qquad f = \text{fight},\; a = \text{acquiesce} \end{equation*}
    Boss Gorilla only gets one letter. He only has a decision to make if he’s challenged, and he can’t tell which type of visitor challenged him, so one choice has to cover both cases.
    🔗
    Let’s start with the equilibrium the reading just found.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.3.1.

    The reading found that \((a, cc)\) is a Nash equilibrium when \(p \gt \frac{1}{3}\text{:}\) every visitor challenges, and Boss Gorilla acquiesces. Why does this need \(p\) to be large enough?
    🔗
    • Since every visitor challenges, a challenger could be either type. If Tough visitors are common enough, fighting is too risky, so Boss Gorilla acquiesces. And once he acquiesces, even Weak visitors are happy to challenge.
    • Correct. When everyone challenges, a challenge tells Boss Gorilla nothing about the visitor’s type, so all he has to go on is \(p\text{.}\) Fighting gets him 5 against a Weak visitor but \(-1\) against a Tough one, while acquiescing is a sure 3. Once \(p \gt \frac{1}{3}\text{,}\) the risk of losing isn’t worth it. The Weak gorillas are essentially bluffing: the Tough gorillas make fighting too risky, and the Weak ones take advantage.
    • When \(p\) is large, most visitors are Tough, and Tough visitors should always challenge.
    • True, but that only explains the first letter of \(cc\text{,}\) and Tough visitors challenge no matter what \(p\) is. The real question is why Boss Gorilla lets everyone in, including the Weak visitors. That’s about his best response.
    • When \(p\) is large, Weak visitors are more likely to win a fight.
    • \(p\) doesn’t change who wins. A Weak visitor always loses a fight. What \(p\) changes is how likely it is, from Boss Gorilla’s point of view, that a challenger is Tough.
    🔗
    What if \(p \lt \frac{1}{3}\text{?}\) Then fighting is worth the risk against \(cc\text{,}\) and it turns out none of the eight profiles in the table is a Nash equilibrium. The book comes back to that case in a Chapter 5 problem (Problem 5.12.2), once we have mixed strategies.
    🔗
    The book spends its last page and a half explaining why \(cc\) strictly dominates \(lc\text{,}\) beyond just checking the table. There’s a quicker way to see it, and it’s worth trying on your own before you read theirs. Start with the fact that Tough Gorilla should always challenge: challenging gets him at least 3, and leaving gets him 0.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.3.2.

    Compare Visiting Gorilla’s payoffs from \(cc\) and \(lc\text{,}\) one branch of Nature at a time. Which is true?
    🔗
    • If he’s Tough, \(cc\) is strictly better. If he’s Weak, they tie.
    • Correct. If he’s Tough, \(cc\) says challenge and \(lc\) says leave, and challenging is better. Against \(f\) he wins the fight for 5 instead of 0, and against \(a\) he becomes co-boss for 3 instead of 0. If he’s Weak, both strategies say challenge, so whatever happens there happens identically under both.
    • \(cc\) is strictly better whether he’s Tough or Weak.
    • Check the second letters. Both \(cc\) and \(lc\) tell a Weak visitor to challenge, so they can’t come apart there. They give the same payoff on that branch, not a better one.
    • It depends on whether Boss Gorilla uses \(f\) or \(a\text{.}\)
    • It doesn’t, and that’s what makes this work. On the Tough branch, challenging beats leaving against \(f\) (5 versus 0) and against \(a\) (3 versus 0). Dominance has to hold against everything Boss Gorilla might do, and here it does.
    🔗
    So on one branch \(cc\) is strictly better than \(lc\text{,}\) and on the other they tie. Now let’s put the branches together.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.3.3.

    Visiting Gorilla’s actual payoff is the weighted average of his payoffs on the two branches, with weights \(p\) and \(1-p\text{.}\) Given what you just found, why does \(cc\) strictly dominate \(lc\) for every \(p\) with \(0 \lt p \lt 1\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • One branch is strictly better and the other ties, so the weighted average comes out strictly better no matter what the weights are.
    • Correct. Averaging can only hurt you if some branch pulls the other way, and here nothing does. Against \(f\text{,}\) for instance, \(cc\) gives \(p \cdot 5 + (1-p)(-1)\) and \(lc\) gives \(p \cdot 0 + (1-p)(-1)\text{.}\) The second terms are identical and the first favors \(cc\text{,}\) so the whole thing favors \(cc\text{.}\)
    • It only dominates when \(p\) is large enough, since the Tough branch needs enough weight to matter.
    • This would be right if the Weak branch pushed back, but it doesn’t. There’s no size of \(p\) that rescues \(lc\text{,}\) because the best it ever does is tie. Any \(p\) above 0 gives the Tough branch some weight, and that’s all we need.
    • It doesn’t strictly dominate. It just does better on average, which is a weaker statement.
    • The average is the payoff here. Once Nature is part of the game, a strategy’s payoff against \(f\) is a single number, the expected value across Nature’s branches, and \(cc\) beats \(lc\) at that number against both of Boss Gorilla’s strategies. That’s exactly strict dominance.
    🔗
    There we have it! Against either of Boss Gorilla’s strategies, Visiting Gorilla’s payoff breaks into two pieces:
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{p \cdot (\text{payoff if Tough})}_{\text{bigger for } cc} \;+\; \underbrace{(1-p) \cdot (\text{payoff if Weak})}_{\text{same for both}} \end{equation*}
    The second piece is a tie, and the first piece is bigger for \(cc\text{,}\) so the total is bigger for \(cc\text{.}\) In plain words: \(lc\) makes a Tough gorilla do the wrong thing and gets nothing in return, since it plays exactly like \(cc\) when he’s Weak.
    🔗

    Section 4.5, The Rest.

    Read the rest of Section 4.5.
    🔗
    You can read this quickly, since it’s the argument we just worked out. Notice how much longer the book takes! Its version is also easier to follow now that you’ve reasoned it out yourself. (Heads up: the book says “Strong” in a few places where it means Tough.) The reading also mentions that a similar argument shows \(cl\) strictly dominates \(ll\text{.}\) See if you can quickly reason that one out!
    🔗
    🔗
    One last thing! The boxed table shows that \((f, cl)\) is not a Nash equilibrium. Let’s see why intuitively.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.3.4.

    In the profile \((f, cl)\text{,}\) Boss Gorilla fights whenever he’s challenged, Tough visitors challenge, and Weak visitors leave. Why isn’t this a Nash equilibrium?
    🔗
    • Under \(cl\text{,}\) only Tough visitors challenge. So the only time Boss Gorilla ever gets to fight is against a visitor who beats him, and he’d rather acquiesce.
    • Correct. Look at what his strategy costs him. On the Weak branch the visitor leaves and he collects 6 whether his strategy says \(f\) or \(a\text{,}\) so that branch doesn’t distinguish them at all. The only branch where his choice does anything is the Tough one, where fighting gets him \(-1\) and acquiescing gets him 3. That’s why he’s better off switching by \(4p\text{,}\) for every \(p\text{.}\)
    • Fighting is what keeps the Weak visitors away. If he switches to \(a\) they’ll start challenging too, so he should stay with \(f\text{.}\)
    • This is the tempting one, and it’s worth seeing why it fails. A strategy isn’t a threat he announces ahead of time. It’s what he actually does when a challenge arrives, and to check for a Nash equilibrium we hold the visitor’s strategy fixed at \(cl\) and ask whether Boss Gorilla wants to deviate. Holding \(cl\) fixed, the Weak visitors are leaving regardless. You saw this problem back in Chapter 1 with Big Monkey: a threat you’d never want to carry out isn’t credible.
    • It fails when \(p\) is small, since a Tough visitor is then too unlikely to be worth preparing for.
    • No value of \(p\) saves it. Compare the \(cl\) column: he gets \(6-7p\) from \(f\) and \(6-3p\) from \(a\text{,}\) so acquiescing wins by \(4p\text{,}\) which is positive for every \(p\) in \((0,1)\text{.}\) You may be thinking of the \(cc\) column, which is the one that genuinely depends on \(p\text{.}\)
