    Exercises 4.4 Exercises

    Before you start, we will be using two terms that the book doesn’t use but that you will see in these exercises (and in a lot of other game theory writing). This isn’t actually anything new, we are just using different terminology for things we have been looking at in this chapter.
    🔗
    In this chapter, whenever Nature moves first and some player can’t see that move, we turned the game into a normal form whose entries are expected payoffs: one payoff matrix for each of Nature’s branches, weighted by that branch’s probability and added up. This normal form is called the Bayesian normal form of the game. You have already built two of them: the used car matrix in Section 4.4, Building the Payoff Matrix and the Boss Gorilla matrix in Section 4.5, The Travails of Boss Gorilla 1.
    🔗
    A Bayesian Nash equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium of the Bayesian normal form. Nothing new is involved: you find them with best response, exactly as in Chapter 3. For example, when \(p \gt \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) the Bayesian Nash equilibria of Buying a Used Car are \((gg, gb)\) and \((bb, bg)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Throughout, Player 1 is the row player and Player 2 is the column player, and payoffs are listed in the order (Player 1, Player 2).
    🔗

    1. Two Types of Interaction.

    Two players, Player 1 and Player 2, are about to interact. The interaction can be one of two types, type \(A\) or type \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • Nature chooses the type: type \(A\) with probability \(\frac{3}{4}\) and type \(B\) with probability \(\frac{1}{4}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Player 1 observes the type and then moves up (\(U\)) or down (\(D\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    • Player 2 observes Player 1’s move, but not the type, and then moves left (\(L\)) or right (\(R\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    Here \(U\) and \(D\) stand for up and down, and \(L\) and \(R\) stand for left and right. Each player has four strategies,
    \begin{equation*} S_1 = \{UU, UD, DU, DD\}, \qquad S_2 = \{LL, LR, RL, RR\}, \end{equation*}
    and the two letters mean the following, just as in Buying a Used Car:
    \begin{equation*} \text{Player 1: }\; \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{his move}\\ \text{if the type is } A}} \quad \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{his move}\\ \text{if the type is } B}} \qquad\qquad \text{Player 2: }\; \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{her move}\\ \text{if Player 1 moved } U}} \quad \underbrace{\;\square\;}_{\substack{\text{her move}\\ \text{if Player 1 moved } D}} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Describe Player 1’s strategy \(DU\) in words.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Describe Player 2’s strategy \(LR\) in words.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What are the payoffs for the strategy profile \((DU, LR)\) when the interaction is of type \(A\text{?}\) When it is of type \(B\text{?}\) What is the expected payoff to each player?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Construct the Bayesian normal form of this game by filling in the \(4 \times 4\) payoff matrix below. In your work: first find the payoff matrix when the type is \(A\) and the payoff matrix when the type is \(B\text{.}\) Then use this to construct the Bayesian normal form of this game (as done in the book).
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Use best response to find all Bayesian Nash equilibria of the game.
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Beliefs and Expected Payoffs.

    Consider the following game in normal form.
    🔗
    A player usually doesn’t know for sure what the other player will do, but they can have beliefs: a probability for each of the other player’s strategies. We write \(\theta_i\) for a belief about player \(i\)’s strategies, so it is held by the other player. Here:
    🔗
    • \(\theta_1 = \left(\frac{2}{3}, \frac{1}{3}\right)\) is Player 2’s belief about Player 1: Player 2 thinks Player 1 will play \(x\) with probability \(\frac{2}{3}\) and \(y\) with probability \(\frac{1}{3}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\theta_2 = \left(\frac{1}{7}, \frac{2}{7}, \frac{4}{7}\right)\) is Player 1’s belief about Player 2: Player 1 thinks Player 2 will play \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(c\) with probabilities \(\frac{1}{7}\text{,}\) \(\frac{2}{7}\text{,}\) \(\frac{4}{7}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Given a player’s belief, we can compute the expected payoff from one of their strategies. A strategy that gives a player the highest expected payoff, given their belief, is a best response to that belief.
    🔗

    (a)

    Compute \(\pi_1(x, \theta_2)\) and \(\pi_1(y, \theta_2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Compute \(\pi_2(\theta_1, a)\text{,}\) \(\pi_2(\theta_1, b)\text{,}\) and \(\pi_2(\theta_1, c)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use your answers to the previous two parts to find Player 1’s best response(s) to the belief \(\theta_2\) and Player 2’s best response(s) to the belief \(\theta_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    3. A Simple Poker Game.

    Two players play a simple poker game with a deck of only two cards, a high card (\(H\)) and a low card (\(L\)). The deck is shuffled and one card is dealt to Player 1, so Player 1 is equally likely to get either card. Player 2 does not receive a card.
    🔗
    • Player 1 looks at his card and then either bids (\(B\)) or folds (\(F\)). If he folds, the game ends: Player 1 gets \(-2\) and Player 2 gets \(2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If Player 1 bids, Player 2 either bids or folds. She knows Player 1 has bid, but she does not know his card. If she folds, Player 1 gets \(2\) and Player 2 gets \(-2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If both players bid, Player 1 shows his card. With the high card, Player 1 gets \(4\) and Player 2 gets \(-4\text{.}\) With the low card, Player 1 gets \(-4\) and Player 2 gets \(4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Player 1’s strategies are \(BB\text{,}\) \(BF\text{,}\) \(FB\text{,}\) and \(FF\text{,}\) where the first letter is what he does with the high card and the second is what he does with the low card. Player 2 only makes one decision, so her strategies are just \(B\) and \(F\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Draw the game in extensive form. Start with a node for Nature that determines which card Player 1 gets (high or low), and show Player 2’s information set (connect nodes together with a dotted line if they are in the same information set).
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Construct the Bayesian normal form of this game by filling in the payoff matrix below.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Show that \(FB\) and \(FF\) are both strictly dominated, and say which of Player 1’s strategies dominates each one. Then explain in words why this makes sense: what do these two strategies have in common?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Eliminate \(FB\) and \(FF\text{,}\) and use best response on the \(2 \times 2\) game that is left to explain why this game has no Bayesian Nash equilibria. Why is it enough to look at the smaller game?
    🔗
    🔗
    In Chapter 5 we will extend the definition of a Nash equilibrium to include mixed strategies. We will then see that this game does have a Nash equilibrium after all, and the book’s One-Card Two-Round Poker (Section 5.4) is a longer version of this same game.

    4. The Travails of Boss Gorilla 2.

    This is Problem 4.7.3 in the book, a variant of Section 4.5, The Travails of Boss Gorilla 1. It is well known that a fraction \(p\) of gorillas can beat Boss Gorilla in a fight, and the remaining fraction \(1-p\) will lose to him. But this time, when a Visiting Gorilla appears, neither gorilla knows which kind of visitor he is. They only know the probability \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Boss Gorilla is Player 1 and Visiting Gorilla is Player 2. The payoffs come from Section 4.5, with Boss Gorilla’s listed first.
    🔗
    Because of the information sets, each gorilla has only two strategies. Visiting Gorilla can challenge (\(c\)) or leave (\(l\)), and Boss Gorilla can fight if challenged (\(f\)) or acquiesce if challenged (\(a\)).
    🔗

    (a)

    Find the Bayesian normal form of the game, a \(2 \times 2\) payoff matrix with entries in terms of \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use best response to find the Bayesian Nash equilibria in each of three cases: \(0 \lt p \lt \frac{1}{6}\text{,}\) \(\frac{1}{6} \lt p \lt \frac{1}{3}\text{,}\) and \(\frac{1}{3} \lt p \lt 1\text{.}\) For each equilibrium, describe in a sentence what the gorillas do.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Speculate. In Section 4.5, where the visitor knows whether he would win, the game has no Bayesian Nash equilibrium (in pure strategies) when \(p \lt \frac{1}{3}\text{.}\) In this version, where nobody knows, you found an equilibrium for every \(p\text{.}\) Why do you think the visitor’s knowledge makes this difference? There is no single right answer here: a reasonable idea, explained in a few sentences, is what we’re looking for.
    🔗
    If you’re stuck, these questions might help you get started:
    🔗
    • In Section 4.5, suppose Boss Gorilla always fights when challenged. Which visitors would still challenge him? Now suppose he always acquiesces. Which visitors would challenge him then?
      🔗
      🔗
    • So in Section 4.5, when Boss Gorilla is challenged, does the kind of visitor he’s likely facing depend on what he does? Is that also true in this version?
