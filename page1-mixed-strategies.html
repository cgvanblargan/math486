    Section 5.1 Mixed Strategies and the Fundamental Theorem

    Chapter 5 begins our study of mixed strategy Nash equilibria. And the mathematics starts to get a bit more interesting!
    🔗

    Section 5.1, Mixed Strategies and the Definition.

    Read Section 5.1 from the beginning up to Theorem 5.1 (Nash’s Existence Theorem). Stop right before it.
    🔗
    Take a minute to check the book’s claim that Tennis has no (pure strategy) Nash equilibrium, using best responses. After that, most of the reading is new notation for mixed strategies. We’ll go through it below and relate it to the game of Tennis.
    🔗
    🔗
    Here’s the Tennis payoff matrix with each player’s probabilities written in the margins. This is how the book draws it in Section 5.2.
    🔗
    So a mixed strategy for the server looks like
    \begin{equation*} \sigma_1 = pb + (1-p)f \end{equation*}
    and a mixed strategy for the receiver looks like
    \begin{equation*} \sigma_2 = qb + (1-q)f, \end{equation*}
    where \(p\) and \(q\) can be any numbers from \(0\) to \(1\text{.}\) Each choice of \(p\) gives a different mixed strategy for the server, and each choice of \(q\) gives a different one for the receiver.
    🔗
    Also, \(.6b + .4f\) isn’t arithmetic; you can’t add strategies!! It’s just a compact way to denote the mixed strategy of serving to the backhand 60% of the time and the forehand 40% of the time.
    🔗
    The checkpoint below will check your understanding of the terms “pure” and “active” strategy.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.2.

    Consider two mixed strategies for the server, \(\sigma_1 = .6b + .4f\) and \(\tau_1 = 1b + 0f\) (we can shorten this to \(\tau_1 = b\)). Which is true?
    🔗
    • Both \(b\) and \(f\) are active in \(\tau_1\text{,}\) since both show up in \(1b + 0f\text{.}\)
    • Showing up isn’t enough. \(f\) has probability \(0\) in \(\tau_1\text{,}\) so it’s never used and isn’t active.
    • \(\tau_1\) is a mixed strategy, so it can’t be a pure strategy.
    • Pure strategies are special mixed strategies, the ones with only one active strategy. \(\tau_1\) is both mixed and pure.
    • Both \(b\) and \(f\) are active in \(\sigma_1\text{.}\) Only \(b\) is active in \(\tau_1\text{,}\) so \(\tau_1\) is the pure strategy \(b\text{.}\)
    • Correct. A strategy is active if its probability is more than \(0\text{,}\) and a mixed strategy is pure when only one strategy is active. So everything from Chapter 3 is still here: pure strategies are just special mixed strategies.
    • Only \(b\) is active in \(\sigma_1\text{,}\) since she uses it more than half the time.
    • Active just means the probability is more than \(0\text{.}\) The server uses \(f\) 40% of the time, so \(f\) is active too.
    🔗
    Now let’s break down equation (5.2). In Tennis, the server’s expected payoff adds up all four cells of Table 5.1.1, each weighted by how likely we are to land in it:
    \begin{equation*} \pi_1(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) = \underbrace{pq}_{\substack{\text{prob. server goes } b\\ \text{and receiver expects } b}} \cdot \underbrace{(.4)}_{\substack{\text{server's payoff}\\ \text{in that cell}}} + p(1-q)(.8) + (1-p)q(.7) + (1-p)(1-q)(.1) \end{equation*}
    Try it out below. Click any cell to see which term of the sum it matches.
    🔗
    The general version says the same thing:
    \begin{equation*} \pi_i(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_n) = \sum_{\text{all } (s_1, \ldots, s_n)} \underbrace{p_{s_1} p_{s_2} \cdots p_{s_n}}_{\substack{\text{prob. that } s_1, \ldots, s_n\\ \text{all happen at once}}} \; \underbrace{\pi_i(s_1, \ldots, s_n)}_{\substack{\text{Player } i\text{'s payoff}\\ \text{when they do}}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Equation (5.6) is the same sum, just grouped by Player \(i\)’s strategies. In Tennis, group the four cells by the server’s row:
    \begin{equation*} \pi_1(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) = p \cdot \pi_1(b, \sigma_2) + (1-p) \cdot \pi_1(f, \sigma_2) \end{equation*}
    Here \(\pi_1(b, \sigma_2)\) is what the server gets if she always serves to the backhand while the receiver keeps mixing. The general version:
    \begin{equation*} \pi_i(\sigma) = \sum_{\text{all } s_i \in S_i} \underbrace{p_{s_i}}_{\substack{\text{how often}\\ i \text{ plays } s_i}} \; \underbrace{\pi_i(s_i, \sigma_{-i})}_{\substack{\text{what } s_i \text{ earns her against}\\ \text{everyone else's mixing}}} \end{equation*}
    So a player’s payoff from a mixed strategy is a weighted average of what her pure strategies earn. This gives the expected payoff of a mixed strategy!!
    🔗
    Finally, notice the definition of a Nash equilibrium is the same as the Chapter 3 definition; we just allow mixed strategies now. A mixed strategy profile \(\sigma^*\) is a Nash equilibrium if for each player \(i\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{\pi_i(\sigma^*)}_{\substack{i\text{'s payoff when everyone}\\ \text{plays their equilibrium strategy}}} \;\geq\; \underbrace{\pi_i(\sigma_i, \sigma^*_{-i})}_{\substack{i\text{'s payoff if she alone}\\ \text{switches to another mixed strategy}}} \quad \text{for every mixed strategy } \sigma_i. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Let’s test a specific profile in Tennis: \(\sigma_1 = .6b + .4f\) and \(\sigma_2 = .7b + .3f\text{.}\) Section 5.2 will show where these numbers come from.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.4.

    The receiver uses \(\sigma_2 = .7b + .3f\text{.}\) Using Table 5.1.1, which computation gives \(\pi_1(b, \sigma_2)\text{,}\) the server’s expected payoff if she always serves to the backhand?
    🔗
    • \(.7(.6) + .3(.2) = .48\)
    • You used the second number in each cell, which is the receiver’s payoff. \(\pi_1\) is the server’s payoff, so use the first numbers.
    • \(.7(.4) + .3(.8) = .52\)
    • Correct. Read across the server’s \(b\) row using her payoffs (the first numbers), weighted by the receiver’s probabilities.
    • \(.6(.4) + .4(.8) = .56\)
    • You weighted by \(.6\) and \(.4\text{,}\) which are the server’s own probabilities. If she always serves to the backhand, what’s left to average over is what the receiver does, so use \(.7\) and \(.3\text{.}\)
    • \(.7(.4) + .3(.7) = .49\)
    • You read down the \(b\) column. The server’s strategies are the rows, so \(\pi_1(b, \sigma_2)\) lives in the \(b\) row.
    🔗
    The same computation on the \(f\) row gives \(\pi_1(f, \sigma_2) = .7(.7) + .3(.1) = .52\text{,}\) which is the same number!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.5.

    We know \(\pi_1(b, \sigma_2) = .52\) and \(\pi_1(f, \sigma_2) = .52\text{.}\) For any \(p\text{,}\) what is \(\pi_1(pb + (1-p)f, \sigma_2)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(.52p\)
    • That only counts the serves to the backhand. The other \(1-p\) of the time she serves to the forehand, which also earns \(.52\text{.}\)
    • \(1.04\)
    • She can’t collect both. Each serve goes one way or the other, so her payoff is a weighted average, not a sum.
    • \(.52\) when \(p = .6\text{,}\) and less for other values of \(p\text{.}\)
    • Tempting, but try \(p = 0\) or \(p = 1\text{.}\) She still gets \(.52\text{.}\) Against \(q = .7\text{,}\) her choice of \(p\) doesn’t change her payoff at all.
    • \(.52\)
    • Correct. By (5.6) in the book, it’s \(p(.52) + (1-p)(.52) = .52\text{.}\) Averaging two equal numbers gives that number back, no matter the weights.
    🔗
    So there is no way for the server to improve her payoff when the receiver plays \(\sigma_2\text{.}\) The receiver’s side works the same way. Against \(p = .6\text{,}\) both of her pure strategies earn her \(.48\text{,}\) so no mix does better for her either. That means \((.6b + .4f,\ .7b + .3f)\) is a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium!
    🔗

    Section 5.1, Nash’s Theorem and the Fundamental Theorem.

    Read from Theorem 5.1 through the paragraph right after Theorem 5.2, the one beginning “This theorem just says.”
    🔗
    You can skip the proof of Theorem 5.2 (but read it if you’re interested!). Before moving on, we’ll apply Theorem 5.2 to the Tennis game. Then we’ll use Tennis to reason out the next few theorems before reading them.
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice that condition (1) of Theorem 5.2 is exactly what we just found in Tennis 🤯. The server’s active strategies both earn \(.52\) (with the receiver fixed at her equilibrium strategy), and similarly the receiver’s both earn \(.48\text{.}\) What about condition (2)?
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.6.

    Do we need to check condition (2) of Theorem 5.2 for the Tennis equilibrium \((.6b + .4f,\ .7b + .3f)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • No. Condition (2) is about inactive strategies, and both players use both strategies, so there aren’t any.
    • Correct. Condition (2) only matters when someone leaves a strategy unused. In Tennis, condition (1) is all there is to check.
    • No, because condition (1) already holds.
    • Right answer, wrong reason. Condition (1) says nothing about inactive strategies. The real reason is that there aren’t any here. If the server never served to the backhand, we’d have to check condition (2) too.
    • Yes. Every Nash equilibrium has to satisfy both conditions.
    • Both conditions do have to hold, but condition (2) only says something when a player has an inactive strategy. Here both players use both strategies, so there’s nothing to check.
    🔗
    Now let’s reason out the next three theorems before reading them.
    🔗

    Example 5.1.7. The server’s payoff at the Tennis equilibrium.

    Earlier we found that against the receiver’s equilibrium strategy \(\sigma_2 = .7b + .3f\text{,}\) both of the server’s pure strategies earn the same amount:
    \begin{equation*} \pi_1(b, \sigma_2) = .52 \quad\text{and}\quad \pi_1(f, \sigma_2) = .52 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    1. What is the server’s payoff at the equilibrium, where she plays \(.6b + .4f\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What is her payoff if she switches to any other mix \(pb + (1-p)f\text{,}\) with the receiver still at \(\sigma_2\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    Both come straight from (5.6), which says her payoff is a weighted average of what \(b\) and \(f\) earn:
    🔗
    1. \(\pi_1(.6b + .4f,\ \sigma_2) = .6(.52) + .4(.52) = .52\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\pi_1(pb + (1-p)f,\ \sigma_2) = p(.52) + (1-p)(.52) = .52\text{,}\) no matter what \(p\) is. (This is the same thing you found in the \(.52\) checkpoint!)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    That’s Theorem 5.3. At a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium, all of a player’s active strategies earn the same amount, call it \(K\) (for the server in Tennis, \(K = .52\)). Then:
    1. Her equilibrium payoff is \(K\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Any mix of her active strategies also earns \(K\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Next, pure strategies are special mixed strategies. So, we can consider whether a strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium both in the sense of Chapter 3 and in the sense of this chapter, where we also have mixed strategies. Let’s connect them with the checkpoint below!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.8.

    Suppose \((s_1^*, s_2^*)\) is a Nash equilibrium in the Chapter 3 sense, so Player 1 can’t improve her payoff by switching to any pure strategy. Could she improve by switching to some mixed strategy \(\tau_1\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • Yes. A mix can combine the strengths of several pure strategies.
    • Against a fixed opponent, a mix is just a weighted average of its pure strategies’ payoffs. An average can never beat the largest thing you’re averaging.
    • You’d have to check every possible mix to know.
    • That’s exactly the work (5.6) saves you. Every mix is a weighted average of pure strategy payoffs, so checking the pure strategies is enough.
    • No. By (5.6), \(\tau_1\)’s payoff is a weighted average of her pure strategies’ payoffs, and none of those beat her payoff at the equilibrium.
    • Correct. If every number you’re averaging is at most \(\pi_1(s^*)\text{,}\) the average is too.
    🔗
    So a Nash equilibrium in the Chapter 3 sense is also a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium. The converse is even more clear: if no player can improve by switching to any mixed strategy, then in particular no player can improve by switching to a pure one. Together, these give Theorem 5.4: for pure strategy profiles, the new definition of Nash equilibrium agrees with the old one.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.9.

    Recall that a Nash equilibrium is strict if every player does strictly worse by switching. Is the Tennis equilibrium strict?
    🔗
    • Yes, since it’s the only Nash equilibrium of the game.
    • Being the only equilibrium and being strict are different things. Strictness is about whether switching can ever tie.
    • No. The server can switch to always serving to the backhand and still get \(.52\text{.}\)
    • Correct. By Theorem 5.3, any mix of her active strategies earns the same, so she has plenty of switches that tie.
    • Yes. No player can do better by switching.
    • That’s just the definition of a Nash equilibrium. Strict means every switch makes you worse, and here some switches leave the server exactly where she was.
    🔗
    That’s Theorem 5.5. Only pure equilibria can be strict. Whenever a player mixes (uses more than one active strategy) at a Nash equilibrium, Theorem 5.3 says she can switch to another active strategy and tie, so the equilibrium can’t be strict.
