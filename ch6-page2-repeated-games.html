    Section 6.2 Repeated Games and the Wine Merchant

    Many games are not played in isolation. Back in Chapter 2, you played the Prisoner’s Dilemma over and over against a robot that used the “tit for tat” strategy. Repeated games allow many new kinds of strategies, and many new Nash equilibria! For example, there is more incentive to cooperate in a repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma: if you use a strategy that keeps cooperating as long as your opponent cooperates, then your opponent may be better off cooperating too.
    🔗

    Section 6.6, Discount Factor and Repeated Games.

    Read Section 6.6.
    🔗
    The first sentence mentions Rubinstein’s bargaining model from Section 6.5, which we skipped (you don’t need it). Then for the subsections:
    • 6.6.1: You can skim the salary argument. The important part is what the discount factor \(\delta\) means.
      🔗
      🔗
    • 6.6.2: This is the definition of a repeated game. We take a game in normal form \(G\text{,}\) called the stage game, and play it over and over at times \(k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\) with discount factor \(\delta\text{.}\) This forms the repeated game \(R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The discount factor \(\delta\) is a number between 0 and 1. A payoff received \(k\) rounds from now counts as \(\delta^k\) times as much as the same payoff today. For example, if \(\delta = \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) then in the first stage (\(k = 0\)) a player receives the full payoff of the outcome. In the next stage (\(k = 1\)), the payoff counts \(\frac{1}{2}\) as much; in stage \(k = 2\text{,}\) it counts \(\frac{1}{2^2} = \frac{1}{4}\) as much; and so on!
    🔗
    To follow the computations in this section, you’ll need the formula from calculus for the sum of a geometric series:
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a\delta^k = a + a\delta + a\delta^2 + \cdots = \frac{a}{1-\delta}. \end{equation*}
    This is the usual formula \(a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \frac{a}{1 - r}\) (for \(|r| \lt 1\)) with \(r = \delta\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.1.

    In the computations in this section, we’ll often consider the payoff when we start at round \(k\) instead of round 0. What is \(\delta^k + \delta^{k+1} + \delta^{k+2} + \cdots\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Hint: factor out \(\delta^k\text{,}\) so \(\delta^k + \delta^{k+1} + \delta^{k+2} + \cdots = \delta^k(\text{fill this part in!})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(\dfrac{\delta^k}{1-\delta}\)
    • Correct. Factor out \(\delta^k\) to get \(\delta^k(1 + \delta + \delta^2 + \cdots)\text{,}\) and use the geometric series formula on what’s left.
    • \(\dfrac{1}{1-\delta^k}\)
    • Not quite. Try factoring \(\delta^k\) out of every term first.
    • \(\dfrac{1}{1-\delta} - \delta^k\)
    • Not quite. The full sum from round 0 is \(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\text{,}\) but starting at round \(k\) drops \(k\) terms, not just one. Try factoring out \(\delta^k\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Section 6.7, The Wine Merchant and the Connoisseur (Part 1).

    Read Section 6.7 up to Theorem 6.1. Stop right before the statement of Theorem 6.1.
    🔗
    This part sets up the game, shows that it is a Prisoner’s Dilemma, and defines the trigger strategy \(\sigma\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is the stage game for reference, with \(c\) for cooperate and \(d\) for defect.
    🔗
    Before getting to the theorem, let’s make sure the trigger strategy is clear. A player using the trigger strategy starts out willing to cooperate, but holds a grudge forever! They cooperate as long as the other player cooperates, but after a single defection from the other player, they never cooperate again.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.3.

    • Suppose Bernard uses the trigger strategy.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The Merchant cooperates (sells good wine) in rounds 0, 1, and 2, defects (sells bad wine) in round 3, and then cooperates again in every round after that.
      🔗
      🔗
    What does Bernard do in each round?
    🔗
    • Bernard cooperates in rounds 0 through 3, defects in round 4, and then cooperates again from round 5 on, since the Merchant went back to cooperating.
    • Not quite. The trigger strategy never forgives. Once the Merchant defects, Bernard defects in every round after that, even though the Merchant goes back to selling good wine.
    • Bernard cooperates in rounds 0 through 3, and defects in every round from round 4 on.
    • Correct. Bernard cooperates in round 3, since he can’t know yet that the Merchant is about to defect. He reacts starting in round 4, and the grudge lasts forever, no matter what the Merchant does afterwards.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.4.

    Suppose the Merchant uses the trigger strategy, and Bernard always defects. We’ll call the strategy “always defect” \(\beta\text{,}\) so this is the strategy profile \((\sigma, \beta)\text{.}\) What are the outcomes, written (Merchant, Bernard)?
    🔗
    • \((d, d)\) in every round, starting in round 0.
    • Not quite. The trigger strategy starts by cooperating, and it only switches to \(d\) the round after it sees a defection.
    • \((c, d)\) in round 0, then \((d, d)\) in every round after that.
    • Correct. In round 0, the Merchant cooperates, since nobody has defected yet, and Bernard steals good wine, for a payoff of 4. From round 1 on, the Merchant sells bad wine and Bernard steals it, so Bernard gets 0. His total is \(4 + 0 \cdot \delta + 0 \cdot \delta^2 + \cdots = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now let’s think about when both players use the trigger strategy, which is the strategy profile \((\sigma, \sigma)\text{.}\) It turns out this strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium (for large enough \(\delta\)), which you’ll soon read. But first, let’s get a more intuitive grasp of the mathematics. Compare Bernard’s payoffs in the two strategy profiles:
    🔗
    • \((\sigma, \sigma)\text{,}\) both use trigger: neither player ever defects first, so they both cooperate in every round, and Bernard gets 1 every round. Factoring in the discount factor,
      \begin{equation*} \pi_2(\sigma, \sigma) = 1 + \delta + \delta^2 + \cdots = \frac{1}{1-\delta}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • \((\sigma, \beta)\text{,}\) Bernard always defects: as in the previous checkpoint, in round 0 the Merchant cooperates, and Bernard gets 4 for stealing good wine. From round 1 on, both defect, and Bernard gets 0 every round. So
      \begin{equation*} \pi_2(\sigma, \beta) = 4 + 0 \cdot \delta + 0 \cdot \delta^2 + \cdots = 4, \end{equation*}
      no matter what \(\delta\) is.
      🔗
      🔗
    So if the Merchant uses the trigger strategy, should Bernard use the trigger strategy too, or always defect? Move the \(\delta\) slider in the widget below to compare \(\pi_2(\sigma, \sigma)\) and \(\pi_2(\sigma, \beta)\text{.}\) The answer depends on the discount factor \(\delta \text{!}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.6.

    In the interactive, using the trigger strategy (and so cooperating every round) is better for Bernard when \(\delta\) is large, and always defecting is better when \(\delta\) is small. Why does this make sense?
    🔗
    • When \(\delta\) is small, later rounds count for very little, so the 4 that Bernard gets in round 0 by defecting matters more than all the 1s he would get later by cooperating.
    • Right. Defecting gives Bernard a big payoff now, and cooperating gives him a small payoff every round forever. The smaller \(\delta\) is, the less the future is worth to him, and the more the payoff now matters.
    • When \(\delta\) is large, the Merchant is more forgiving, so Bernard is more likely to get away with defecting.
    • Not quite. The trigger strategy never forgives, no matter what \(\delta\) is. The discount factor doesn’t change how the Merchant plays, only how much Bernard cares about future rounds.
    🔗
    It doesn’t take much more work to see why the cutoff is at \(\delta = \frac{3}{4}\text{.}\) Bernard does at least as well with the trigger strategy as with always defecting exactly when
    \begin{align*} \underbrace{\frac{1}{1-\delta}}_{\substack{\text{Bernard's payoff when} \\ \text{both use trigger}}} \amp \geq \underbrace{4}_{\substack{\text{Bernard's payoff when} \\ \text{he always defects}}}\\ \delta \amp \geq \frac{3}{4}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    But Bernard has infinitely many other strategies. For example, he could cooperate for 10 rounds and then always defect. Theorem 6.1 says that when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{,}\) no strategy \(\sigma'\) does better for Bernard against the Merchant’s trigger strategy: \(\pi_2(\sigma, \sigma) \geq \pi_2(\sigma, \sigma')\text{.}\) Since the game is symmetric, the same is true for the Merchant: \(\pi_1(\sigma, \sigma) \geq \pi_1(\sigma', \sigma)\text{.}\) That’s exactly what it means for \((\sigma, \sigma)\) to be a Nash equilibrium!!
    🔗

    Section 6.7, Theorem 6.1.

    Read Theorem 6.1 and its proof.
    🔗
    The proof splits into two cases, depending on whether Bernard ever defects. The math gets more interesting in Case 2!
    🔗
    🔗
    Before reading the rest of Section 6.7, recall the last paragraph of Section 6.6.2, which defines a subgame of a repeated game. A subgame starts at the beginning of some round, and it remembers everything that happened before. The book only skims over subgames of repeated games, so we’ll spend most of the rest of this page making them clearer.
    🔗

    Section 6.7, The Wine Merchant and the Connoisseur (Part 2).

    Read the rest of Section 6.7.
    🔗
    There are two ideas in this reading. First, \(\delta\) can also be read as the probability that the game continues for another round. Second, the book asks whether \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is subgame perfect, and to answer it, the book has to redefine the trigger strategy more carefully.
    🔗
    🔗
    From here on, we’ll call the Merchant Player 1 and Bernard Player 2, and talk about cooperating (\(c\)) and defecting (\(d\)). Nothing in the argument below depends on the story, so it works for any repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma with these payoffs.
    🔗
    The picture below shows the start of the repeated game. Each branch is one possible outcome of round 0, and each node after round 0 starts a different subgame. The dotted lines show that the game keeps branching in the same way, every round. We’ve labeled one node \(n_1\text{:}\) the subgame that starts in round 1, after Player 1 defected and Player 2 cooperated in round 0.
    🔗
    The redefined trigger strategy \(\sigma\) says to cooperate only if both players have cooperated in every earlier round. As soon as anyone defects, including the player themselves, the player will defect forever.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.8.

    Suppose both players use the redefined trigger strategy \(\sigma\text{.}\) Consider the subgame at \(n_1\text{.}\) What is the outcome in round 1, written (Player 1, Player 2)?
    🔗
    • \((c, d)\)
    • Not quite. That’s what the original trigger strategy would do, where each player only reacts to the other player’s defections. With the redefined \(\sigma\text{,}\) a player also reacts to their own defections.
    • \((d, d)\)
    • Right. The outcome of round 0 was \((d, c)\text{,}\) so someone has already defected.
      • Player 2 saw Player 1 defect, so \(\sigma\) tells Player 2 to defect.
      • Player 1 defected themselves, so \(\sigma\) tells Player 1 to defect too.
      Both players defect in round 1, and in every round after that.
    🔗
    In fact, because there was a defection on the way to \(n_1\text{,}\) both players will defect forever starting at \(n_1\text{,}\) as long as they are both using the redefined trigger strategy.
    🔗
    Now we’ll go through why this strategy profile is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{.}\) The book doesn’t spend much time on this, but it will be important for our class.
    🔗

    Example 6.2.9. The trigger strategy is subgame perfect.

    Suppose both players use the redefined trigger strategy \(\sigma\text{,}\) and \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{.}\) To show that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium, we need to show that its restriction to every subgame is a Nash equilibrium of that subgame. Take any subgame, starting at a node \(n\) at the beginning of round \(k\text{.}\) There are only two possibilities for what happened before \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a) Nobody has defected before \(n\).

    Then every round before \(n\) had the outcome \((c, c)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Since nobody has defected, the trigger strategy tells both players to start with \(c\) at \(n\text{,}\) so we are in exactly the same situation as at the start of the original game.
    🔗
    So the analysis showing that the restriction is a Nash equilibrium is identical to Theorem 6.1, except that all the payoffs get multiplied by \(\delta^k\text{!}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Someone has defected before \(n\).

    If either player defected at any point before \(n\text{,}\) the trigger strategy tells both players to defect at \(n\) and keep defecting forever.
    🔗
    If the other player defects forever no matter what, the best you can do is defect too each round (you’ll think about why in a checkpoint below!). Defecting each round is exactly what the (redefined) trigger strategy says to do at node \(n\text{.}\) So the restriction is a Nash equilibrium of the subgame starting at \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Every subgame falls into one of these two cases, so \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.11.

    In the second case of the Example, someone has defected before \(n\text{,}\) so Player 2 defects in every round of the subgame, no matter what Player 1 does. What is Player 1’s best response?
    🔗
    • Defect in every round.
    • Right. In every round, Player 2 defects, so Player 1 gets 0 by defecting and \(-4\) by cooperating. And nothing Player 1 does can change what Player 2 does later, since the grudge lasts forever. So Player 1 just picks the better payoff each round, which is defecting.
    • Cooperate for a while, so that Player 2 goes back to cooperating.
    • Not quite. Player 2 never goes back to cooperating, no matter what Player 1 does. In each round, cooperating just gets Player 1 \(-4\text{,}\) when defecting would get 0.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.12.

    Why does the Example need \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • Both cases need \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{:}\) when nobody has defected before \(n\text{,}\) and when someone has.
    • Not quite. When someone has defected, Player 1 compares 0 with \(-4\) in each round, and 0 is better no matter what \(\delta\) is. So that case works for every \(\delta\text{.}\)
    • The first case (nobody has defected) needs \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{,}\) and the second case (someone has defected) works for any \(\delta\text{.}\) So together, \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\) covers every subgame.
    • Right. The first case uses Theorem 6.1, which needs \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{.}\) The second case only compares payoffs within each round, so \(\delta\) doesn’t matter.
