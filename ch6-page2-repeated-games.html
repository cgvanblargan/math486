    Section 6.2 Repeated Games and the Wine Merchant

    Many games are not played in isolation. Back in Chapter 2, you played the Prisoner’s Dilemma over and over against a robot that used the “tit for tat” strategy. Repeated games allow many new kinds of strategies, and many new Nash equilibria! For example, there is more incentive to cooperate in a repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma: if you use a strategy that keeps cooperating as long as your opponent cooperates, then your opponent may be better off cooperating too.
    🔗

    Section 6.6, Discount Factor and Repeated Games.

    Read Section 6.6.
    🔗
    The first sentence mentions Rubinstein’s bargaining model from Section 6.5, which we skipped (you don’t need it). Then for the subsections:
    • 6.6.1: You can skim the salary argument. The important part is what the discount factor \(\delta\) means.
      🔗
      🔗
    • 6.6.2: This is the definition of a repeated game. We take a game in normal form \(G\text{,}\) called the stage game, and play it over and over at times \(k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\) with discount factor \(\delta\text{.}\) This forms the repeated game \(R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The discount factor \(\delta\) is a number between 0 and 1. A payoff received \(k\) rounds from now counts as \(\delta^k\) times as much as the same payoff today. For example, if \(\delta = \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) then in the first stage (\(k = 0\)) a player receives the full payoff of the outcome. In the next stage (\(k = 1\)), the payoff counts \(\frac{1}{2}\) as much; in stage \(k = 2\text{,}\) it counts \(\frac{1}{2^2} = \frac{1}{4}\) as much; and so on!
    🔗
    To follow the computations in this section, you’ll need the formula from calculus for the sum of a geometric series:
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} r\delta^k = r + r\delta + r\delta^2 + \cdots = \frac{r}{1-\delta}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.1.

    In the computations in this section, we’ll often need the total of a payoff of 1 per round, starting at round \(k\) instead of round 0. What is \(\delta^k + \delta^{k+1} + \delta^{k+2} + \cdots\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Hint: factor out \(\delta^k\text{,}\) so \(\delta^k + \delta^{k+1} + \delta^{k+2} + \cdots = \delta^k(\text{fill this part in!})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(\dfrac{\delta^k}{1-\delta}\)
    • Correct. Factor out \(\delta^k\) to get \(\delta^k(1 + \delta + \delta^2 + \cdots)\text{,}\) and use the geometric series formula on what’s left.
    • \(\dfrac{1}{1-\delta^k}\)
    • Not quite. Try factoring \(\delta^k\) out of every term first.
    • \(\dfrac{1}{1-\delta} - \delta^k\)
    • Not quite. The full sum from round 0 is \(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\text{,}\) but starting at round \(k\) drops \(k\) terms, not just one. Try factoring out \(\delta^k\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Section 6.7, The Wine Merchant and the Connoisseur (Part 1).

    Read Section 6.7 up to Theorem 6.1. Stop right before the statement of Theorem 6.1.
    🔗
    This part sets up the game, shows that it is a Prisoner’s Dilemma, and defines the trigger strategy \(\sigma\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is the stage game for reference, with \(c\) for cooperate and \(d\) for defect.
    🔗
    Before getting to the theorem, let’s make sure the trigger strategy is clear. A player using the trigger strategy starts out willing to cooperate, but holds a grudge forever! They cooperate as long as the other player cooperates, but after a single defection from the other player, they never cooperate again.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.3.

    Suppose Bernard uses the trigger strategy. The Merchant cooperates (sells good wine) in rounds 0, 1, and 2, defects (sells bad wine) in round 3, and then cooperates again in every round after that. What does Bernard do in each round?
    🔗
    • Bernard cooperates in rounds 0 through 3, defects in round 4, and then cooperates again from round 5 on, since the Merchant went back to cooperating.
    • Not quite. The trigger strategy never forgives. Once the Merchant defects, Bernard defects in every round after that, even though the Merchant goes back to selling good wine.
    • Bernard cooperates in rounds 0 through 3, and defects in every round from round 4 on.
    • Correct. Bernard cooperates in round 3, since he can’t know yet that the Merchant is about to defect. He reacts starting in round 4, and the grudge lasts forever, no matter what the Merchant does afterwards.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.4.

    Suppose the Merchant uses the trigger strategy, and Bernard always defects. What are the outcomes, written (Merchant, Bernard)?
    🔗
    • \((d, d)\) in every round, starting in round 0.
    • Not quite. The trigger strategy starts by cooperating, and it only switches to \(d\) the round after it sees a defection.
    • \((c, d)\) in round 0, then \((d, d)\) in every round after that.
    • Correct. In round 0, the Merchant cooperates, since nobody has defected yet, and Bernard steals good wine, for a payoff of 4. From round 1 on, the Merchant sells bad wine and Bernard steals it, so Bernard gets 0. His total is \(4 + 0 \cdot \delta + 0 \cdot \delta^2 + \cdots = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now let’s think about when both players use the trigger strategy. Since neither one ever defects first, they both cooperate in every round, so each gets a payoff of 1 every round. Factoring in the discount factor \(\delta\text{,}\) each player’s total payoff is
    \begin{equation*} 1 + \delta + \delta^2 + \cdots = \frac{1}{1-\delta}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Compare that with the previous checkpoint, where the Merchant uses the trigger strategy and Bernard always defects. In round 0, the Merchant hasn’t seen a defection yet, so he still cooperates, and Bernard gets 4 for stealing good wine. From round 1 on, both defect, and Bernard gets 0 every round. So Bernard’s total payoff is
    \begin{equation*} 4 + 0 \cdot \delta + 0 \cdot \delta^2 + \cdots = 4\text{,} \end{equation*}
    no matter what \(\delta\) is.
    🔗
    So if the Merchant uses the trigger strategy, should Bernard use the trigger strategy too, or always defect? Move the \(\delta\) slider in the widget below to compare the two payoffs.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.6.

    In the interactive, using the trigger strategy (and so cooperating every round) is better for Bernard when \(\delta\) is large, and always defecting is better when \(\delta\) is small. Why does this make sense?
    🔗
    • When \(\delta\) is small, later rounds count for very little, so the 4 that Bernard gets in round 0 by defecting matters more than all the 1s he would get later by cooperating.
    • Right. Defecting gives Bernard a big payoff now, and cooperating gives him a small payoff every round forever. The smaller \(\delta\) is, the less the future is worth to him, and the more the payoff now matters.
    • When \(\delta\) is large, the Merchant is more forgiving, so Bernard is more likely to get away with defecting.
    • Not quite. The trigger strategy never forgives, no matter what \(\delta\) is. The discount factor doesn’t change how the Merchant plays, only how much Bernard cares about future rounds.
    🔗
    It doesn’t take much more work to see why the cutoff is at \(\delta = \frac{3}{4}\text{.}\) Suppose the Merchant uses the trigger strategy. We showed above that if Bernard also uses the trigger strategy, his payoff is \(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\text{,}\) and if he always defects, his payoff is 4. So the trigger strategy gives Bernard at least as good a payoff as always defecting exactly when
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{1-\delta} \geq 4, \quad \text{that is,} \quad \delta \geq \frac{3}{4}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    However, Bernard has infinitely many other strategies besides the trigger strategy and always defecting. For example, he could cooperate for the first 10 rounds and then always defect. It turns out that when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{,}\) the trigger strategy isn’t just better than always defecting, it is actually a best response to the Merchant’s trigger strategy, so there is no strategy Bernard would rather use. Because the game is symmetric, the same argument shows that the trigger strategy is the Merchant’s best response to Bernard’s trigger strategy. That’s exactly what it means for \((\sigma, \sigma)\) to be a Nash equilibrium! The proof of Theorem 6.1 handles all of Bernard’s strategies at once.
    🔗

    Section 6.7, Theorem 6.1.

    Read Theorem 6.1 and its proof.
    🔗
    The proof splits into two cases, depending on whether Bernard ever defects. The math gets more interesting in Case 2!
    🔗
    🔗
    Before reading the rest of Section 6.7, recall the last paragraph of Section 6.6.2, which defines a subgame of a repeated game. A subgame starts at some round \(k\text{,}\) and it remembers everything that happened before. So “the game starting at round 5, where both players always cooperated” and “the game starting at round 5, where Player 1 defected in round 2” are different subgames. This is the same idea as on the previous page: a subgame is a “what if we got here?”, and “here” includes the history.
    🔗

    Section 6.7, The Wine Merchant and the Connoisseur (Part 2).

    Read the rest of Section 6.7.
    🔗
    There are two ideas here. First, \(\delta\) can also be read as the probability that the game continues for another round. Second, the book asks whether \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is subgame perfect, and to answer it, the book has to redefine the trigger strategy more carefully.
    🔗
    🔗
    Why bother redefining \(\sigma\text{?}\) Here’s what goes wrong with the original definition, “use \(d\) forever if your opponent ever uses \(d\text{.}\)” Suppose that in round 0 the Merchant sells bad wine and Bernard pays for it. Look at the subgame starting in round 1.
    🔗
    • Bernard has never stolen, so the original \(\sigma\) tells the Merchant to keep selling good wine.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The Merchant cheated, so the original \(\sigma\) tells Bernard to steal forever.
      🔗
      🔗
    The Merchant gets \(-4\) every round, when switching to bad wine would give him 0. So the original \(\sigma\) is not a best response in that subgame. The redefined \(\sigma\) defects if anyone has defected, which fixes the problem.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.7.

    Under the redefined trigger strategy, what does a player do in round \(j\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • Use \(c\) if both players used \(c\) in every earlier round. Otherwise use \(d\text{.}\)
    • Correct.
    • Use \(c\) if the opponent used \(c\) in every earlier round. Otherwise use \(d\text{.}\)
    • Not quite, that’s the original definition. The example above shows why it fails in the subgame after the Merchant cheats.
    • Use whatever the opponent used in round \(j-1\text{.}\)
    • Not quite, that’s tit for tat.
    🔗
    The book leaves the proof of subgame perfection to you (“you should be able to convince yourself...”). Let’s do it. The key observation: there are only two kinds of subgames, depending on whether anyone has defected yet.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2.8.

    Put the blocks in order to prove that \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{,}\) using the redefined trigger strategy. Not every block belongs! Start with the case where nobody has defected.
    🔗
    Let \(H\) be the subgame starting at round \(k\text{,}\) with some history of play in rounds \(0, \ldots, k-1\text{.}\) We must show that both players using \(\sigma\) is a Nash equilibrium of \(H\text{.}\)

    
---


    
Case 1: in the history, both players used \(c\) in every round.
    
---


    Then \(H\) looks exactly like the original repeated game, with every payoff multiplied by \(\delta^k\text{,}\) and \(\sigma\) tells both players to play as if starting from scratch.
    
---


    Multiplying every payoff by \(\delta^k\) doesn’t change which strategies are best, so by Theorem 6.1, neither player can improve when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{.}\)

    
---


    
Case 2: in the history, at least one player used \(d\) at least once.
    
---


    Then \(\sigma\) tells both players to use \(d\) in every round of \(H\text{,}\) no matter what happens in \(H\text{.}\)

    
---


    Suppose Player 2 uses \(\sigma\text{.}\) Then Player 2 uses \(d\) every round, whatever Player 1 does.
    
---


    Against \(d\text{,}\) Player 1 gets 0 from \(d\) and \(-4\) from \(c\text{,}\) and Player 1’s choices can’t change Player 2’s future moves.
    
---


    So Player 1 can do no better than \(d\) every round, which is what \(\sigma\) says. By symmetry, the same holds for Player 2.
    
---


    Every subgame falls into Case 1 or Case 2, so \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium when \(\delta \geq \frac{3}{4}\text{.}\)

    
---


    
Case 2: in the history, the opponent used \(d\) at least once.
     #distractor
---


    In Case 2, Player 1 should go back to \(c\text{,}\) since \((c, c)\) pays 1, which is more than 0.
     #distractor
---


    By Theorem 6.1, \((\sigma, \sigma)\) is a Nash equilibrium of every subgame, so we are done.
     #distractor
    Hint.
    In Case 2, who controls what Player 2 does from now on? Can anything Player 1 does make Player 2 cooperate again?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Compare this to tit for tat: start with \(c\text{,}\) and after that, do whatever the other player did last round. On this week’s homework, you’ll show that two players using tit for tat is a Nash equilibrium for large enough \(\delta\text{,}\) but not a subgame perfect one. The difference is what happens after a defection: the trigger strategy’s punishment is a best response, but with tit for tat, a single defection leads to an endless \(c, d, c, d, \ldots\) cycle of revenge.
    🔗
    The trigger strategy \(\sigma\) is just one strategy for the repeated game. Here are some others you’ll meet on the homework. Each one is a rule that looks at what happened in earlier rounds and decides what to do now.
    🔗
    • Trigger, \(\sigma\text{:}\) start with \(c\text{.}\) If the other player ever defects, use \(d\) forever.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Tit for tat, \(\tau\text{:}\) start with \(c\text{.}\) After that, do whatever the other player did last round.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Suspicious tit for tat, \(\nu\text{:}\) start with \(d\text{.}\) After that, do whatever the other player did last round.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Generous tit for tat, \(\zeta\text{:}\) play \(c\) in rounds 0 and 1. After that, do whatever the other player did last round.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Always defect, \(\beta\text{:}\) use \(d\) every round.
      🔗
      🔗
