    Section 5.3 One-Card Two-Round Poker

    We’re gonna start analyzing a version of poker with game theory, so maybe by the end of the course you’ll want to hit up the casinos! We will study a much simpler version than standard poker (“one-card two-round poker”).
    🔗

    Section 5.4, The Setup.

    Read Section 5.4 up through both sets of bullet points.
    🔗
    So you are stopping after the three options for Player 1 when he has the high card:
    • bb: bet. If Player 2 bets back, bet again.
      🔗
      🔗
    • bf: bet. If Player 2 bets back, fold.
      🔗
      🔗
    • f: fold.
      🔗
      🔗
    The line you stop at is “If Player 1 is dealt the low card...” At this point you will have a sense of the setup and strategies. We will clarify a few points before moving on.
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that the last three bullet points are Player 1’s options when he has the high card. He has those same three options when he has the low card.
    🔗
    One technicality: the option f is really two options lumped together. Strictly, a strategy says what Player 1 does at every decision point, so we’d have “fold, then bet the second time” and “fold, then fold the second time.” But once he folds the game is over, so the book just calls both f. (It does the same with Player 2’s f.)
    🔗
    Think about what this means for the total number of (pure) strategies, counting f as one option: three options with the high card and three with the low card. Remember the multiplication rule we saw earlier in the course!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.1.

    How many pure strategies does Player 1 have?
    🔗
    • \(3\)
    • bb, bf, and f are options for one card. A strategy has to say what Player 1 does with both cards.
    • \(9\)
    • Correct. A strategy picks one of \(3\) options for the high card and one of \(3\) for the low card, so \(3 \times 3 = 9\text{.}\) If we didn’t lump the two fold options together as f, each card would have \(4\) options and we’d get \(4 \times 4 = 16\text{.}\) The extra strategies only differ in choices that never come up, so the book doesn’t count them separately.
    • \(6\)
    • A strategy is a pair of choices, one for each card, so we multiply. Think of outfits: \(3\) shirts and \(3\) pants make \(9\) outfits, not \(6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    When you read on, you’re going to see only three strategies for Player 1 in the tables. This is because the book throws out every strategy that doesn’t bet at every opportunity with the high card. That leaves three strategies, and the book names each one by its low card option only:
    • bb: bb if high, bb if low. Always bet, whatever the card.
      🔗
      🔗
    • bf: bb if high, bf if low. Always bet at first. With the low card, fold if Player 2 bets back.
      🔗
      🔗
    • f: bb if high, f if low. Fold right away with the low card.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    This is why, when you get to the next table (for when the card dealt is high), all of the rows are the same! In all three of these strategies, Player 1 always bets if he has the high card. The names bb, bf, and f only say what he does when the card is low.
    🔗

    Section 5.4, Finding the Equilibrium.

    Keep reading through the derivation of the Nash equilibrium \((q_1, q_2, q_3) = \left(\frac{8}{15}, \frac{2}{15}, \frac{1}{3}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So at this point you will have seen the expected payoff tables, the system of equations for finding the Nash equilibrium, and the solutions \((p_1, p_2, p_3) = \left(\frac{1}{5}, \frac{2}{5}, \frac{2}{5}\right)\) for Player 1 and \((q_1, q_2, q_3) = \left(\frac{8}{15}, \frac{2}{15}, \frac{1}{3}\right)\) for Player 2. A few things to watch for:
    • In the high card table, all three rows are the same. That makes sense now: all three strategies play bb with the high card.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The expected payoff matrix shows up twice. The second copy is the same matrix with the probabilities (the \(p\)’s and \(q\)’s) in the margins.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The book skips the cases with two active strategies and says it will come back to them in Section 5.5.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Now let’s make sure you understand a few important ideas from this reading!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.2.

    Why does the book set \(\pi_2(\sigma_1, bb) = \pi_2(\sigma_1, bf) = \pi_2(\sigma_1, f)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • Condition (2) of Theorem 5.2.
    • Condition (2) compares active strategies to inactive ones, and it’s an inequality. Here every strategy is active, and we want equalities.
    • Player 1 is mixing, so Player 1 must be indifferent.
    • Wrong player. These are Player 2’s payoffs, so this is about Player 2 being indifferent among his strategies.
    • Condition (1) of Theorem 5.2. All of Player 2’s pure strategies are active, so they have to earn him the same payoff against \(\sigma_1\text{.}\)
    • Correct. Same idea as Tennis, just with three strategies instead of two.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.3.

    Where does the equation \(p_1 + p_2 + p_3 = 1\) come from?
    🔗
    • The \(p_i\) are the probabilities of Player 1’s three strategies, and he has to play one of them. This means they sum to \(1\) (a 100 percent chance of playing one of these strategies).
    • Correct. Probabilities always add up to \(1\text{.}\) The two indifference equations aren’t enough to pin down three unknowns, so we need this one too.
    • It’s a third equation from Player 2 being indifferent.
    • Setting three things equal only gives two independent equations. This third one has nothing to do with payoffs. It’s just a fact about probabilities.
    • It comes from the game being zero-sum.
    • Zero-sum is about the players’ payoffs adding to \(0\text{.}\) This equation is about Player 1’s probabilities.
    🔗
    So from the three equations (two from Theorem 5.2, and one from the probabilities summing to \(1\)), the book finds \((p_1, p_2, p_3) = \left(\frac{1}{5}, \frac{2}{5}, \frac{2}{5}\right)\text{.}\) Before reading on, let’s think about what this actually means!!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.4.

    What does \(p_2 = \frac{2}{5}\) mean?
    🔗
    • Player 2 bets back and then folds \(\frac{2}{5}\) of the time.
    • The \(p\)’s belong to Player 1. Player 2’s probabilities are the \(q\)’s.
    • \(\frac{2}{5}\) of the time, Player 1 uses bf: bb if high, bf if low.
    • Correct. Remember bf is a full strategy (the name refers to what Player 1 does when dealt the low card). With the high card he still bets at every opportunity.
    • With the high card, Player 1 bets and then folds \(\frac{2}{5}\) of the time.
    • He never folds with the high card! The name bf only describes what he does with the low card.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.5.

    How would you compute Player 1’s expected payoff \(\pi_1(\sigma_1, \sigma_2)\text{?}\) (Don’t actually compute it.)
    🔗
    • Add up the three diagonal cells, where both players use the strategy with the same name.
    • Every cell can happen, since each player picks his strategy independently. Plus the names mean different things for the two players, so the diagonal isn’t special.
    • Average the nine payoffs.
    • That treats every cell as equally likely. Each cell happens with probability \(p_i q_j\text{,}\) so weight by that instead.
    • Take a weighted sum over all nine cells: each cell’s Player 1 payoff times \(p_i q_j\text{,}\) the probability of landing in that cell.
    • Correct. That’s equation (5.2), same as Tennis but with nine cells instead of four. Reading across the rows of the payoff matrix:
      \begin{align*} \pi_1(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) = \amp\ p_1 q_1 \cdot 0 + p_1 q_2 \cdot 4 + p_1 q_3 \cdot 2\\ \amp + p_2 q_1 \cdot 1 + p_2 q_2 \cdot 0 + p_2 q_3 \cdot 2\\ \amp + p_3 q_1 \cdot 2 + p_3 q_2 \cdot 1 + p_3 q_3 \cdot 0 \end{align*}
      Since this is an equilibrium, Theorem 5.3 also gives a shortcut. You’ll see both in the reading.
    🔗
