    1. The Pay Increase.

    Three elected county commissioners, \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) and \(C\text{,}\) hold a public vote on whether to give themselves a pay increase. The rules of the vote are as follows.
    🔗
    • Each commissioner votes yes (\(y\)) or no (\(n\)), and the measure passes if at least two of the three vote yes.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Votes are announced in order: \(A\) votes first; \(B\) observes \(A\)’s vote and then announces; \(C\) observes both votes and then announces.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If the measure passes, each commissioner receives a benefit of \(v\text{,}\) whether or not that commissioner voted for it.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Any commissioner who votes yes pays a political cost \(c\text{,}\) and pays it whether or not the measure passes.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Both \(v\) and \(c\) are positive, with \(v > c\text{.}\) Benefits and costs are additive, so \(A\)’s payoff is \(A\)’s benefit minus \(A\)’s cost.
      🔗
      🔗
    The extensive form game is shown below, with payoffs listed in the order \((A, B, C)\text{.}\) Four of the terminal nodes are marked \((?,?,?)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Determine the payoffs at the four terminal nodes marked \((?,?,?)\text{.}\) Include a sketch or screenshot of the game tree with the correct payoffs written in. A verbal description of the actions is not a substitute for the sketch.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    How many strategies does each commissioner have? Report your answers as \(|S_A|\text{,}\) \(|S_B|\text{,}\) and \(|S_C|\) (these represent the size of each player’s strategy set). You do not need to write out the strategy sets themselves, and you do not need to write an explanation, but show enough work that it is clear how you counted.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Write out one of \(C\)’s strategies, and describe what it means in words. Your description should be in complete sentences, and it should make clear why the thing you wrote down is a strategy rather than a single vote.
    🔗
    Hint.
    There are four nodes where \(C\) is called on to vote, one for each way the first two votes could have gone. A strategy has to say something about all four, including the ones that won’t be reached.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Use backward induction to analyze the game and predict the outcome. Show the steps graphically, in the style of the book’s Figures 1.4 and 1.5. Do not write out explanations of each step. Clearly indicate the path through the tree that play will follow.
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    According to your backward induction analysis, what is \(C\)’s rational strategy? What you report here must be a strategy for \(C\text{.}\) You may want to revisit the definition of a strategy before answering.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Four Players and an Entry Fee.

    Four players, \(P_1\text{,}\) \(P_2\text{,}\) \(P_3\text{,}\) and \(P_4\text{,}\) play the following game.
    🔗
    • Entry fee. Before play begins, every player pays a $1 entry fee.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Order of play. Players move one at a time in the order \(P_1\text{,}\) \(P_2\text{,}\) \(P_3\text{,}\) \(P_4\text{.}\) A player moves only if every player before her has chosen to continue.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The two choices. On her turn, a player either (E)nds the game or (C)ontinues.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Ending. A player who plays E pays an additional $1. The game ends immediately, and each of the other three players receives $11 ($10 plus the $1 entry fee back). The player who ended the game receives nothing.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Continuing. A player who plays C pays nothing, and play passes to the next player.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Reaching the end. If \(P_4\) plays C, the game simply ends with no further payments, and all four players are out their $1 entry fee.
      🔗
      🔗

    (a)

    Sketch the extensive form of this game. Be careful to account for every payment at each outcome: all four players pay the $1 entry fee before play begins, a player who ends the game pays a further $1, and the other three each collect $11. Report each player’s payoff as the net amount she gains or loses over the whole game.
    🔗
    Hint.
    The tree is a single chain of four decision nodes. Each node has one branch ending the game and one continuing, so there are five terminal nodes in all.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Determine the backward induction solution.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. A Three Player Game.

    Consider the following extensive form game with three players. Payoffs are listed in the usual way, so that \((p_1, p_2, p_3)\) means \(p_1\) is the payoff to player 1, \(p_2\) the payoff to player 2, and \(p_3\) the payoff to player 3.
    🔗

    (a)

    For each player \(i\text{,}\) determine \(|S_i|\) (that is the size of the strategy set for player i). You do not need to write out the strategy sets. An explanation isn’t necessary, but include enough work that it’s clear how you determined each size.

    🔗

    (b)

    For player 3, describe in words what is meant by the strategy \(s_3 = x\,b\text{,}\) where player 3’s two decision nodes are taken in left-to-right order. Write your description in complete sentences.

    🔗

    (c)

    Use backward induction to analyze the game. Indicate graphically the path of play your analysis predicts.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    According to your backward induction analysis, what strategy will each player use? Make sure that what you report for each player really is a strategy.
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Identify at least one outcome that all three players would strictly prefer to the outcome reached by backward induction. That is, every player must be better off, not merely no worse off.

    4. When Backward Induction Fails.

    We saw that the backward induction procedure can break down when, at some step, two moves give the deciding player the same best payoff. In both games below, player 2 is indifferent between her two moves, so the procedure breaks down in both. The two games differ in whether that breakdown actually costs us a prediction. In both trees, payoffs are listed in the order \((p_1, p_2)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Carry out backward induction on Game A as far as you can. At which node does the procedure break down, and why? Then explain, in complete sentences, why the breakdown leaves player 1 unable to decide between his two moves.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Now turn to Game B. Show that backward induction breaks down at player 2’s node for the same reason it did in Game A.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Even though the procedure breaks down, player 1 has a clearly rational choice in Game B. Identify it, and explain in complete sentences why player 1 can make that choice without knowing what player 2 would do.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compare player 1’s payoff from \(a\) with every payoff player 1 could end up with after playing \(b\text{,}\) rather than with the single payoff backward induction would have assigned to player 2’s node.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Classifying Commitments.

    Consider the two player game below. Player 1 moves first and has three choices. Payoffs are listed in the order \((p_1, p_2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Where you are asked to verify that a commitment is a threat or a promise, check the definition from Section 1.6 explicitly: identify the node at which the commitment applies, give the rational move and its payoffs \((\pi_1, \pi_2)\text{,}\) give the committed move and its payoffs \((\pi_1', \pi_2')\text{,}\) and state both comparisons.
    🔗

    (a)

    Use backward induction to find the outcome of the game. Show the steps graphically, in the style of the book’s Figures 1.4 and 1.5.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    For each of the four announcements below, decide whether it is a threat, a promise, or neither, using the Section 1.6 definition. Do not work out the consequences yet; that is the next part.
    🔗
    Write the verification out in full for (i) only: the node, the rational move and its payoffs, the committed move and its payoffs, and both comparisons. For (ii) through (iv), just give the two payoff pairs and your classification. You do not need to show your work or explain how you got there.
    🔗

    (i)

    Player 2 commits to playing \(v\) at the left node.
    🔗
    🔗

    (ii)

    Player 2 commits to playing \(t\) at the right node.
    🔗
    🔗

    (iii)

    Player 2 commits to playing \(w\) at the right node.
    🔗
    🔗

    (iv)

    Player 2 commits to playing \(s\) at the right node.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Now take each announcement in turn. Assuming Player 1 knows about the commitment, and that Player 2 will carry it out if play reaches the node in question, determine the outcome at which the game ends. Then state whether that outcome is better or worse for Player 1, and better or worse for Player 2, than the backward induction outcome from the first part.
    🔗
    Just give the outcome and the two comparisons for each announcement. You do not need to show the steps that got you there.
    🔗
    🔗
    Two of these are worth a second look once you have finished. One is a valid threat Player 2 would genuinely carry out, and yet it leaves both players worse off than if she had said nothing. Another is also a valid threat that changes nothing at all. Making your opponent’s current choice unattractive only helps if you check where he goes instead, and a commitment matters only at a node play actually reaches.

    6. Big Monkey, Little Monkey, and the Broken Leg.

    Section 1.6 ends with a suggestion: perhaps Little Monkey should break his own leg and show up on crutches. This exercise takes that suggestion seriously.
    🔗
    A broken leg does not change Little Monkey’s mind, and does not make him braver or more stubborn. What it changes is his payoffs, by making the climb cost real energy. That is, any time Little Monkey chooses “climb”, his payoff decreases by \(\ell\text{.}\) Setting \(\ell = 0\) returns you to the book’s Figure 1.7 exactly.
    🔗

    (a)

    Take \(\ell = 0\) and run backward induction on the tree above. Confirm that you recover the outcome given in Section 1.5, and state what Little Monkey’s payoff is.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Now let \(\ell\) vary. Find the value of \(\ell\) at which the backward induction outcome changes, and describe what the outcome is on either side of that value. Your answer should identify which of Little Monkey’s two decision nodes is responsible for the change, and should say what goes wrong with backward induction exactly at the threshold.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Finally, return to a healthy Little Monkey with \(\ell = 0\text{,}\) but suppose Little Monkey moves first and Big Monkey second. Draw the new game tree and find the backward induction outcome. Then compare any similarities and differences between this version where Little Monkey goes first and the original game with a threat. Do they feel like essentially the same game or not, and why?
    🔗
    🔗
    Note. A healthy Little Monkey announcing “I will wait no matter what” is making a threat in the exact sense of Section 1.6: at his left node, waiting drops his own payoff from \(1\) to \(0\) and Big Monkey’s from \(9\) to \(0\text{.}\) Both fall, which is what a threat requires, and the difficulty is getting Big Monkey to believe it.
    🔗
    Once the leg is broken this changes. With \(\ell > 1\text{,}\) waiting at the left node is simply Little Monkey’s rational move. However, if Little Monkey does not have a broken leg, he can make a threat that he will actually break his own leg!
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Commitment in the Centipede Game.

    This exercise uses a shortened version of Rosenthal’s Centipede Game from Section 1.8. The rules are as in the book, except that the game ends sooner. Mutt and Jeff start with $2 each, and Mutt moves first. On a player’s turn he has two moves: cooperate (\(c\)), in which he does nothing and the game master rewards him with $1; and defect (\(d\)), in which he steals $2 from the other player. The game ends when either player defects, or both players have at least $5. Payoffs are dollars held at the end.
    🔗
    The extensive form is below. The six decision nodes are labelled \(n_1\) through \(n_6\text{,}\) so Mutt owns \(n_1\text{,}\) \(n_3\text{,}\) \(n_5\) and Jeff owns \(n_2\text{,}\) \(n_4\text{,}\) \(n_6\text{.}\) The amounts above each node are what the players are holding when play arrives there; they are not payoffs. The payoffs are the pairs at the terminal nodes.
    🔗
    In the commitment parts below, unless a part asks for more, just state the resulting outcome and give a sentence or two saying why. You do not need to show your work or write out the node by node analysis again.
    🔗

    (a)

    Use backward induction to find the outcome of the game. You do not need to draw anything. Starting with \(n_6\) and working backward, state the move each player chooses at each node. Give the payoff comparison explicitly at \(n_6\) and \(n_5\text{.}\) At the remaining nodes the comparison has the same shape, so just state the move; you do not need to show your work there.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose Jeff can credibly commit, before play begins, to cooperating at \(n_2\text{.}\) Verify against the Section 1.6 definition whether this is a threat or a promise, and find the new outcome. You should find that the game ends at \((5,1)\text{;}\) make sure you can get that answer before going on, since the remaining parts use the same method.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Now suppose Jeff commits to cooperating at both \(n_2\) and \(n_4\text{.}\) Find the new outcome. You do not need to show the steps.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Now suppose Jeff commits to cooperating at all three of \(n_2\text{,}\) \(n_4\text{,}\) and \(n_6\text{.}\) Find the new outcome, and explain what you find. Your explanation should say something about which nodes are actually reached. Apart from that explanation, you do not need to show the steps.
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Now suppose instead that Mutt commits to cooperating at \(n_3\) and \(n_5\text{,}\) and that Jeff makes no commitment at all. Find the outcome. You do not need to show the steps. Were you surprised?
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Finally, suppose Mutt and Jeff can both make commitments. Is there a combination of commitments that ends in a payoff better than \(4\) for Mutt and better than \(2\) for Jeff? If so, give one and state the outcome it produces. If not, explain why not. Either way, you do not need to show the steps.
    🔗
    🔗

One of the two kinds of commitment from Section 1.6 is available to her here and the other is not. Say which is which, and explain what it is about this game that rules the other one out.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    In your opinion, is there a point past which such a commitment would stop being believable? Say roughly where you think that point is and why. There is no single right answer here; give your reasoning.

    9. A Duopoly Where the Firms Have Different Costs.

    In Section 1.10 both firms had the same unit cost of production. Here we work through a Stackelberg duopoly in which they do not.
    🔗
    • Firm 1 chooses a quantity \(q_1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Firm 2 observes \(q_1\) and then chooses a quantity \(q_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    The market price is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p = 150 - q_1 - q_2, \end{equation*}
    and the cost functions are \(c_1(q_1) = 9q_1\) and \(c_2(q_2) = 4q_2\text{.}\) Notice that Firm 2 is the cheaper producer. Each firm’s payoff is its total profit, revenue minus cost.
    🔗
    As in Subsection 1.10.1, we place no restrictions on \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) in this exercise: they may be any real numbers, even ones that make the price negative. This is not realistic, and we will fix it in the next exercise. For now it keeps every function you work with a single upside-down parabola, with no boundary to check!
    🔗

    (a)

    Determine the payoff function for each firm. Build each one from the price rule and that firm’s own cost, and simplify. Reference the notes or book to confirm you are setting it up correctly! The only differences here are the price and cost functions.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find Firm 2’s best-response function \(b(q_1)\text{.}\) Before taking a derivative, say in one sentence why a critical point is guaranteed to be the maximum here.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Substitute to obtain \(\pi_1\bigl(q_1, b(q_1)\bigr)\text{,}\) and simplify it to a function of \(q_1\) alone.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Finish the backward induction to determine the rational outcome of the game. Report \(q_1^*\text{,}\) \(q_2^*\text{,}\) the resulting market price, and each firm’s profit.
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    In Section 1.11 the book shows that when both firms have the same unit cost, Firm 1 produces twice as much and earns twice as much as Firm 2, whatever \(\alpha\text{,}\) \(\beta\text{,}\) and \(c\) happen to be. Check whether that holds in this game. Then say, in a sentence or two, which assumption of Section 1.11 fails here.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute the two ratios \(q_1^*/q_2^*\) and \(\pi_1/\pi_2\) and compare them to \(2\text{.}\) They are not equal to each other either.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10. The Same Duopoly, Without Negative Quantities.

    We now redo the previous exercise the way Subsection 1.10.2 redoes Subsection 1.10.1. The two firms and their costs are unchanged: \(c_1(q_1) = 9q_1\) and \(c_2(q_2) = 4q_2\text{.}\) What changes is that production levels must be nonnegative, \(0 \leq q_1 \lt \infty\) and \(0 \leq q_2 \lt \infty\text{,}\) and that the price is not allowed to fall below zero:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p = \begin{cases} 150 - q_1 - q_2 \amp \text{if } q_1 + q_2 \lt 150, \\[1ex] 0 \amp \text{if } q_1 + q_2 \geq 150. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    Work this exercise the same way we worked through the example in Subsection 1.10.2. Part of the point is to see where the restrictions force extra work, and where they turn out not to matter.
    🔗
    One warning before you start. In Subsection 1.10.2 several different cutoffs all happened to land on the same number, \(s = 8\text{,}\) because the two firms there had the same unit cost. Here they come apart. You will find more than one threshold in the course of this exercise, and they are different numbers. Keep track of which one you are computing and why.
    🔗

    (a)

    Write the payoff function for each firm. Each one is now piecewise. Say in one sentence what the second piece represents for the firm in question.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find the smallest value of \(q_1\) with the property that Firm 2 cannot earn a positive profit, no matter which \(q_2\) it chooses. Justify your answer with an inequality, not with a graph.
    🔗
    Hint.
    On the first piece, factor \(q_2\) out of \(\pi_2\) rather than multiplying everything through. Written that way, \(\pi_2\) is Firm 2’s profit on each unit sold times the number of units sold, and you can ask when that first factor can be positive.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Using the previous part, write Firm 2’s best-response function \(b(q_1)\text{.}\) It should be piecewise, with the value on each piece justified. For the piece where Firm 2 produces nothing, use your conclusion from the previous part to explain why \(0\) is the best available choice, not merely an allowed one. This second piece is a corner solution, like the one in the notes.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Now find the corresponding threshold for Firm 1: the smallest \(q_1\) at which Firm 1 itself can no longer earn a positive profit. Take Firm 2 to be producing nothing at all here, which is the most favorable case for Firm 1. You should get a different number than in the earlier part. Say which of the two thresholds rules out part of the domain when we go looking for Firm 1’s optimal \(q_1\text{,}\) and why it is that one.
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Both \(\pi_1\) and \(b(q_1)\) are piecewise, so before writing a formula for \(\pi_1\bigl(q_1, b(q_1)\bigr)\) we have to know which piece of \(\pi_1\) is in force. Compute \(q_1 + b(q_1)\) on the range of \(q_1\) left over from the previous part, and use it to show that only the first piece of \(\pi_1\) can occur.
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Finish the backward induction. Report \(q_1^*\text{,}\) \(q_2^*\text{,}\) the market price, and each firm’s profit, and compare your answer to the one you found in the previous exercise.
    🔗
    Before you report your answer, confirm that the \(q_1^*\) you found actually lies in the range you carved out earlier, and say so explicitly.
    🔗
    🔗
    As in the book, the more realistic model gives exactly the same answer as the unrestricted one. The restrictions cost you two pieces of extra work, both of which turned out to be housekeeping: a case in \(b(q_1)\) that the optimal \(q_1\) never reaches, and a second piece of \(\pi_1\) that \(q_1 + b(q_1)\) never gets to.
    🔗
    🔗

    11. Manufacturer and Retailer.

    A large retailer buys a particular model of tablet from a manufacturer and sells it in its stores. We model this as a two player game. The structure is close to a Stackelberg duopoly, but the two players here are not competitors: one sells to the other.
    🔗
    • It costs the manufacturer $100 to make each tablet. The manufacturer chooses a wholesale price \(w\) per tablet, with \(w \in [100, \infty)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The retailer observes \(w\) and then chooses a quantity \(q \in [0,\infty)\) to buy from the manufacturer.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The retailer sells every tablet it buys, at the market price \(p = 500 - 0.1q\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    For each player the payoff is total profit, revenue minus cost. As in Subsection 1.10.1, we let the price formula apply for all \(q\text{,}\) even where it returns a negative price; you will see that no rational player goes anywhere near there.
    🔗
    The restriction \(w \geq 100\) will not cause you any trouble, since no manufacturer would set a wholesale price below what a tablet costs it to make. The restriction \(q \geq 0\) does give you one corner case to attend to, though. It is this: for a wholesale price high enough, the retailer’s best response is \(q = 0\text{,}\) since it can no longer resell at a profit. You should find where that happens and handle it, but you do not have to hunt for any other cases.
    🔗

    (a)

    Write the payoff function for each player. Be careful about what each player pays and what each player receives; the wholesale price is revenue to one of them and a cost to the other.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find the retailer’s best-response function \(b(w)\text{,}\) including the corner case described above.

    (c)

    Use backward induction to determine the rational outcome of the game. Report the wholesale price, the quantity, the price consumers pay, and each player’s profit.

    (d)

    Now suppose the manufacturer sells directly to consumers instead, with no retailer involved. It chooses a quantity \(q\) to make, faces the same market price \(p\) the retailer faced, and has total profit \(pq - 100q\text{.}\) Find the manufacturer’s optimal choice of \(q\text{,}\) together with the resulting price and profit.
    🔗
    Hint.
    There is no second player here, so there is no game and no backward induction. This part is a one-variable maximization.
    🔗

    (e)

    Make a table comparing the two scenarios. Include the quantity sold, the price consumers pay, and each firm’s profit, along with the combined profit of the manufacturer and retailer in the first scenario.
    🔗
    Then answer in a short paragraph: the two firms in the first scenario each behaved rationally, so why do they end up with less between them than the single firm does on its own? Your answer should say something about what the retailer treats as its cost per tablet, and how that compares to what a tablet actually costs to produce.
    🔗
    🔗
    This is an example of double marginalization. A markup is taken twice on the way to the consumer, and because the retailer decides how much to buy as though each tablet cost what it paid rather than what it cost to make, the chain sells fewer tablets than a single firm would. Notice that consumers do worse under the two-firm arrangement as well, which is not what we usually expect when a single firm takes over a whole market.
