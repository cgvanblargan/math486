    Section 7.1 Symmetric Games

    This chapter is short: we only need one idea from it, symmetric games, because it sets up everything we’ll do with evolutionary stability in Chapter 8.
    🔗

    Section 7.1, Interchangeable Players.

    Read Section 7.1.
    🔗
    Focus on the idea, not the notation. You don’t need to master the notation with permutations. The main point is this: a two-player game is symmetric if both players have the same strategies, and swapping the players just swaps their payoffs. In symbols,
    \begin{equation*} \pi_1(s, t) = \pi_2(t, s) \end{equation*}
    for all strategies \(s\) and \(t\text{.}\) In a payoff table, this means the entry in row \(s\text{,}\) column \(t\) is the entry in row \(t\text{,}\) column \(s\) with the two payoffs switched.
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is the main result of the section, restated.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.1.1. Theorem 7.1.

    Every symmetric game with finitely many pure strategies has a symmetric Nash equilibrium, one in which every player uses the same (possibly mixed) strategy. For a two-player game, this is a Nash equilibrium of the form \((\sigma, \sigma)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This is essential for Chapter 8. There, we’ll think of a game as being played over and over by members of a large population, and a population state \(\sigma\) describes how often each strategy is used. Evolutionary stability is about Nash equilibria of the form \((\sigma, \sigma)\text{,}\) and Theorem 7.1 guarantees that every symmetric game has at least one.
    🔗
    Some games keep coming back, in this course and in game theory in general. Here is a reminder of four canonical symmetric games we’ve already seen. In each table, the pure strategy Nash equilibria are boxed.
    🔗

    Prisoner’s Dilemma.

    Each player can cooperate (\(c\)) or defect (\(d\)).
    🔗
    Defecting is a strictly dominant strategy, so \((d, d)\) is the only Nash equilibrium, even though both players would be better off if they both cooperated. It is already symmetric.
    🔗
    🔗

    Stag Hunt.

    Each hunter can hunt a stag together with the other hunter, or hunt a hare alone.
    🔗
    Both Nash equilibria are symmetric. The hunters agree that (stag, stag) is better, but hunting stag is risky if you aren’t sure the other hunter will join you.
    🔗
    🔗

    Chicken.

    Each teenager can go straight or swerve.
    🔗
    Each teenager wants to be the one who goes straight while the other swerves, so the two pure Nash equilibria are not symmetric. The symmetric Nash equilibrium promised by Theorem 7.1 is mixed: each teenager goes straight with probability \(\frac{1}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Water Pollution.

    Three firms each either purify their waste (\(g\)) or pollute (\(b\)). Purifying costs 1, and if two or more firms pollute, the water is polluted and each firm pays 3. Since there are three players, we use two tables, one for each choice of Firm 3, with payoffs listed as (Firm 1, Firm 2, Firm 3).
    🔗
    In the pure Nash equilibria where two firms purify and one pollutes, the polluter gets clean water for free. These aren’t symmetric. The symmetric one is the worst outcome: all three firms pollute, and nobody wants to be the only one paying to purify water that stays polluted anyway.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 7.1.7.

    Chicken is a symmetric game, but neither of its pure strategy Nash equilibria is symmetric. Does this contradict Theorem 7.1?
    🔗
    • Yes, because Theorem 7.1 says there is a symmetric Nash equilibrium.
    • Not quite. Theorem 7.1 allows the symmetric Nash equilibrium to be mixed.
    • No, because the symmetric Nash equilibrium can be mixed.
    • Right. In Chicken, the symmetric Nash equilibrium is \((\sigma, \sigma)\) where each teenager goes straight with probability \(\frac{1}{2}\text{.}\)
