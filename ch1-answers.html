    Exercises 1.9 Answers to Ch. 1 Exercises

    Answers to the first eight exercises of this chapter. Click an exercise to open it, click a part to open that part, then click Solution to reveal the answer.
    🔗
    These are answers, not worked solutions. Enough is shown that you can tell whether your own work landed in the right place, but the intermediate steps are yours to supply. Where a question asks for a tree or for backward induction shown graphically, the picture is the answer, so the picture is given. In those pictures a thick edge is a move that gets chosen and a crossed, dashed edge is one that does not.
    🔗

    1. The Pay Increase.

    (a) The four unknown terminal payoffs.

    Determine the payoffs at the four terminal nodes marked \((?,?,?)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    From left to right: \((v-c,\,v-c,\,v-c)\text{,}\) \((v-c,\,v-c,\,v)\text{,}\) \((v-c,\,v,\,v-c)\text{,}\) and \((-c,\,0,\,0)\text{.}\) Only the last of these is a node where the measure fails: \(A\) voted yes alone, so \(A\) pays \(c\) and nobody collects \(v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Sizes of the strategy sets.

    How many strategies does each commissioner have?
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(|S_A| = 2\text{,}\) \(|S_B| = 2^2 = 4\text{,}\) \(|S_C| = 2^4 = 16\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) One of \(C\)’s strategies, in words.

    Write out one of \(C\)’s strategies and describe what it means.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Any of the sixteen is acceptable. Listing \(C\)’s move at each of his four nodes, from left to right, gives a strategy. For example, the strategy \(n\,n\,n\,y\) corresponds to the following in words:
    • If \(A\) chooses \(y\) and \(B\) chooses \(y\text{,}\) then \(C\) chooses \(n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(A\) chooses \(y\) and \(B\) chooses \(n\text{,}\) then \(C\) chooses \(n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(A\) chooses \(n\) and \(B\) chooses \(y\text{,}\) then \(C\) chooses \(n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(A\) chooses \(n\) and \(B\) chooses \(n\text{,}\) then \(C\) chooses \(y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) Backward induction.

    Analyze the game by backward induction and indicate the path of play.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The path is \(A: n\text{,}\) \(B: y\text{,}\) \(C: y\text{,}\) with payoffs \((v,\,v-c,\,v-c)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e) \(C\)’s rational strategy.

    According to your backward induction analysis, what is \(C\)’s rational strategy?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The rational strategy is the rational decision \(C\) would make at each node he is at. We start with the move at the leftmost node, which gives the strategy \(s_C = (n,\,y,\,y,\,n)\text{.}\) In words, \(C\) votes yes exactly when his vote is the deciding one.
    🔗

    3. A Three Player Game.

    (a) Sizes of the strategy sets.

    For each player \(i\text{,}\) determine \(|S_i|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(|S_1| = 3 \cdot 3 = 9\text{,}\) \(|S_2| = 2 \cdot 3 = 6\text{,}\) \(|S_3| = 3 \cdot 4 = 12\text{.}\) Player 1 moves at the root (three moves) and at the node reached by \(z, c\) (three moves); player 2 moves at two nodes, with two and three moves; player 3 moves at two nodes, with three and four moves. Multiply the number of moves across a player’s own nodes.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) What the strategy \(x\,b\) means.

    Describe in words what is meant by \(s_3 = x\,b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    If player 1 plays \(y\) and player 2 responds with \(a\text{,}\) then player 3 plays \(x\text{.}\) If player 1 plays \(z\text{,}\) then player 3 plays \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Backward induction.

    Analyze the game by backward induction and indicate the path of play.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The path of play is \(x\) then \(y\text{,}\) ending with a payoff \((0,\,6,\,2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) Each player’s strategy.

    What strategy will each player use?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Taking each player’s nodes left to right, \(s_1 = x\,a\text{,}\) \(s_2 = y\,c\text{,}\) and \(s_3 = y\,a\text{.}\) Each of these names a move at both of that player’s nodes, including the ones off the predicted path. Reporting only \(x\) for player 1, or only \(y\) for player 2, gives a move rather than a strategy.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e) An outcome all three prefer.

    Identify an outcome all three players strictly prefer to the backward induction outcome.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The predicted outcome leads to a payoff \((0,\,6,\,2)\text{,}\) so we need an outcome beating \(0\text{,}\) \(6\text{,}\) and \(2\) in all three coordinates at once. The following three all work:
    1. The terminal node \((10,\,12,\,8)\text{,}\) reached by \(1\) playing \(z\) and \(3\) playing \(b\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The terminal node with a payoff of \((5,\,10,\,5)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The terminal node with a payoff of \((6,\,12,\,8)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    4. When Backward Induction Fails.

    (a) Where Game A breaks down.

    At which node does the procedure break down in Game A, and why does that leave player 1 unable to decide?
    🔗
    Solution.
    It breaks down at player 2’s node. Both \(c\) and \(d\) pay player 2 the same amount, \(1\text{,}\) so there is no unique best move there and no single payoff pair to fold back up to the root.
    🔗
    Player 1 is then stuck. He does not know whether choosing \(a\) or \(b\) will lead to a better payoff, because he does not know what player 2 will choose at the right node.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) The same breakdown in Game B.

    Show that backward induction breaks down at player 2’s node in Game B for the same reason.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 2 gets \(2\) from \(c\) and \(2\) from \(d\text{,}\) so the payoffs are equal and backward induction cannot predict what happens at this node.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Player 1’s rational choice in Game B.

    Identify player 1’s rational choice and explain why he can make it without knowing what player 2 would do.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 1 should play \(a\text{.}\) It pays him \(5\text{,}\) while the only payoffs he can reach after \(b\) are \(4\) and \(1\text{,}\) and \(5\) beats both. He therefore does not need to know, or guess, how player 2 breaks her tie: whichever move she would make, \(a\) leaves him better off.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Classifying Commitments.

    (a) Backward induction.

    Find the outcome of the game, showing the steps graphically.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 1 plays \(a\text{,}\) Player 2 plays \(u\text{,}\) and the payoff is \((5,2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Classifying the four announcements.

    Classify each announcement as a threat, a promise, or neither.
    🔗
    Solution.
    (i) Player 2’s left node: rational move \(u\text{,}\) \((\pi_1,\pi_2) = (5,2)\text{;}\) committed move \(v\text{,}\) \((\pi_1',\pi_2') = (1,1)\text{.}\) Both fall, \(1 \lt 5\) and \(1 \lt 2\text{,}\) so it is a threat.
    🔗
    Announcement Rational move Committed move Classification
    (i) \(v\) at the left node \(u\text{:}\) \((5,2)\) \(v\text{:}\) \((1,1)\) Threat
    (ii) \(t\) at the right node \(w\text{:}\) \((3,6)\) \(t\text{:}\) \((7,4)\) Promise
    (iii) \(w\) at the right node \(w\text{:}\) \((3,6)\) \(w\text{:}\) \((3,6)\) Neither
    (iv) \(s\) at the right node \(w\text{:}\) \((3,6)\) \(s\text{:}\) \((0,5)\) Threat
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Consequences of each announcement.

    For each announcement, give the resulting outcome and compare it to the backward induction outcome \((5,2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Announcement Player 1 plays Outcome Player 1 Player 2
    (i) \(v\) \(c\) \((4,0)\) worse worse
    (ii) \(t\) \(b\) \((7,4)\) better better
    (iii) \(w\) \(a\) \((5,2)\) same same
    (iv) \(s\) \(a\) \((5,2)\) same same
    🔗

    6. Big Monkey, Little Monkey, and the Broken Leg.

    (a) A healthy Little Monkey.

    Run backward induction with \(\ell = 0\) and state Little Monkey’s payoff.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Big Monkey waits and Little Monkey climbs, with payoffs \((9,1)\text{.}\) Little Monkey’s payoff is \(1\text{,}\) which is the outcome of Section 1.5.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) The threshold value of \(\ell\).

    Find the value of \(\ell\) at which the outcome changes and describe the outcome on either side.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Threshold \(\ell = 1\text{,}\) at Little Monkey’s left node.
    🔗
    • \(\ell \lt 1\text{:}\) Big Monkey waits, Little Monkey climbs, \((9,\,1-\ell)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\ell \gt 1\text{:}\) Big Monkey climbs, Little Monkey waits, \((4,4)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\ell = 1\text{:}\) both moves pay Little Monkey \(0\) there, so backward induction breaks down.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Little Monkey moves first.

    With \(\ell = 0\) and Little Monkey moving first, draw the tree, find the outcome, and compare with the threat version.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Little Monkey waits and Big Monkey climbs, with payoffs \((4,4)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Different games, but the same scenario in effect. Whether Little Monkey waits first or announces in advance that he will wait, Big Monkey compares \(0\) against \(4\) and climbs for the same reason. Moving first only makes the waiting a fact instead of a claim.
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Commitment in the Centipede Game.

    (a) Backward induction.

    Find the outcome, giving the payoff comparison at \(n_6\) and \(n_5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(n_6\text{:}\) Jeff gets \(6\) from \(d\) against \(5\) from \(c\text{,}\) so \(d\text{.}\) \(n_5\text{:}\) Mutt gets \(6\) from \(d\) against \(3\) from \(c\text{,}\) so \(d\text{.}\) Then \(d\) at \(n_4\text{,}\) \(n_3\text{,}\) \(n_2\text{,}\) \(n_1\text{.}\) Payoff \((4,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Jeff commits at \(n_2\).

    Classify the commitment and find the new outcome.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Promise. At \(n_2\text{:}\) rational move \(d\text{,}\) \((1,4)\text{;}\) committed move \(c\text{,}\) \((5,1)\text{.}\) Better for Mutt, worse for Jeff. Mutt then cooperates at \(n_1\) and the game ends at \(n_3\) with \((5,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Jeff commits at \(n_2\) and \(n_4\).

    Find the new outcome.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Mutt defects at \(n_5\text{.}\) Payoff \((6,2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) Jeff commits at \(n_2\text{,}\) \(n_4\text{,}\) and \(n_6\).

    Find the new outcome and explain what you find.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Still \((6,2)\text{.}\) Mutt defects at \(n_5\text{,}\) so \(n_6\) is never reached and the commitment there does nothing.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e) Mutt commits at \(n_3\) and \(n_5\).

    Find the outcome when only Mutt commits.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((4,0)\text{,}\) the same as with no commitments at all.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f) Both players commit.

    Is there a combination giving Mutt more than \(4\) and Jeff more than \(2\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Yes. Jeff commits to \(c\) at \(n_2\text{,}\) \(n_4\text{,}\) \(n_6\) and Mutt commits to \(c\) at \(n_3\text{,}\) \(n_5\text{.}\) Outcome \((5,5)\text{,}\) the only terminal payoff that qualifies.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. The Ultimatum Game.

    (a) Which kind of commitment is available.

    Which of threat and promise is available to Player 2 here, and what rules the other one out?
    🔗
    Solution.
    A threat: “I will reject any offer below \(x\text{.}\)” Rejecting takes Player 1 from \(100-x\) to \(0\) and Player 2 from \(x\) to \(0\text{,}\) so both fall. A promise is impossible, since accepting already pays Player 1 the better of her two moves.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) When the commitment stops being believable.

    Is there a point past which the commitment stops being believable?
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is your opinion!
