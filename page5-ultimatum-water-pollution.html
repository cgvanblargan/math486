    Section 5.5 Finishing Zero-Sum Games, the Ultimatum Minigame, and Water Pollution

    This page covers three sections. First we’ll finish Section 5.5 by seeing what Theorem 5.6 tells us about One-Card Two-Round Poker. Then come two more examples of finding mixed strategy Nash equilibria with the Fundamental Theorem (Theorem 5.2).
    🔗
    A quick recap from the last page. In a two-player zero-sum game, the players’ payoffs add up to \(0\) in every cell. Theorem 5.6 says that in these games, Nash equilibria and maximin equilibria are exactly the same thing, and at every Nash equilibrium, each player gets their maximin payoff.
    🔗
    Before you read on, see if you can work out a consequence of Theorem 5.6 on your own.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.5.1. Interchangeability.

    Suppose \((\sigma_1, \sigma_2)\) and \((\tau_1, \tau_2)\) are both Nash equilibria of a two-player zero-sum game. Is \((\sigma_1, \tau_2)\) also a Nash equilibrium?
    🔗
    • Yes. By Theorem 5.6, \(\sigma_1\) and \(\tau_2\) are both maximin strategies, so \((\sigma_1, \tau_2)\) is a maximin equilibrium, and therefore a Nash equilibrium.
    • Correct! You can mix and match halves of Nash equilibria in a zero-sum game. By part (2), all of them give the same payoffs, too.
    • There’s no way to tell without seeing the payoffs.
    • Theorem 5.6 answers this for every two-player zero-sum game, no matter what the payoffs are. Which part of the theorem connects Nash equilibria and maximin strategies?
    🔗
    So Nash equilibria of zero-sum games have an interchangeability property: you can swap halves of two Nash equilibria and get another one.
    🔗

    Section 5.5, The Rest.

    Finish reading Section 5.5. Pick up right after the statement of Theorem 5.6. You can skip or skim the book’s proof, since we did the main part of it on the last page.
    🔗
    The first paragraph after the proof is the interchangeability property you just found in the checkpoint. The book then uses it to show that One-Card Two-Round Poker has only one Nash equilibrium. The argument can be hard to follow, so after you give it a first reading, we’ll put it together step by step.
    🔗
    🔗
    The book’s argument is a proof by contradiction. We’ll build a direct version instead, using the interchangeability property and one more fact that came out of the calculations in Section 5.4.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.5.2. Only One Equilibrium in Poker.

    Setup. In Section 5.4 we found the Nash equilibrium \((\sigma_1^*, \sigma_2^*)\text{,}\) where
    \begin{align*} \sigma_1^* \amp = \tfrac{1}{5}\,bb + \tfrac{2}{5}\,bf + \tfrac{2}{5}\,f\\ \sigma_2^* \amp = \tfrac{8}{15}\,bb + \tfrac{2}{15}\,bf + \tfrac{1}{3}\,f \end{align*}
    In both, all three strategies are active (every pure strategy gets positive probability). At the end of Section 5.4, the book also points out the following.
    🔗
    Fact from 5.4: In any Nash equilibrium of One-Card Two-Round Poker where Player 2 uses all three of his strategies, Player 1 must play \(\sigma_1^*\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Section 5.6, The Ultimatum Minigame.

    Read all of Section 5.6.
    🔗
    It’s a short section. Pay attention to how the book uses the Fundamental Theorem: first on the pure strategy equilibrium \((f, r)\text{,}\) and then on the mixed profiles \((f,\ qa + (1-q)r)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s make sure the way the book applied the theorem is clear. The book looks at strategy profiles of the form \((f,\ qa + (1-q)r)\) with \(0 \lt q \lt 1\text{.}\) Theorem 5.2 gives a list of conditions to check, and which conditions show up depends on which strategies are active.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.5.3.

    To show that \((f,\ qa + (1-q)r)\) with \(0 \lt q \lt 1\) is a Nash equilibrium, which conditions from Theorem 5.2 do we need to check? Here \(\sigma_2 = qa + (1-q)r\) is Bob’s mixed strategy. Notice that the strict inequalities in \(0 \lt q \lt 1\) mean both of Bob’s strategies, \(a\) and \(r\text{,}\) are active!
    🔗
    Drag every condition we need into the answer area. The order doesn’t matter, and not every condition belongs!
    🔗
    \(\pi_2(f, a) = \pi_2(f, r)\) #tag:bob-eq; depends:;
---
\(\pi_1(f, \sigma_2) \geq \pi_1(u, \sigma_2)\) #tag:alice-ineq; depends:;
---
\(\pi_1(f, \sigma_2) = \pi_1(u, \sigma_2)\) #distractor

    Hint.
    Alice has only one active strategy, \(f\text{,}\) and one inactive strategy, \(u\text{.}\) Bob has two active strategies and no inactive ones. Which kind of condition does each active or inactive strategy give?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    So the equality \(\pi_2(f, a) = \pi_2(f, r)\) holds automatically (both are \(2\)), and all the work is in Alice’s inequality, which comes out to \(q \leq \frac{2}{3}\text{.}\) Before moving on, let’s make sure that answer makes sense.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.5.4.

    The condition turned out to be \(q \leq \frac{2}{3}\text{.}\) Why does it make sense that this is an upper limit on \(q\text{,}\) rather than a lower limit?
    🔗
    • \(q\) is how often Bob accepts an unfair offer. The more he accepts, the more tempting the unfair offer is for Alice, so \(q\) can’t be too big or she’d switch to \(u\text{.}\)
    • Correct. The fair offer always pays Alice \(2\text{.}\) The unfair offer pays her \(3\) when Bob accepts and \(0\) when he rejects, so it’s worth \(3q\) on average. Alice sticks with the fair offer as long as \(2 \geq 3q\text{.}\) In other words, Bob doesn’t have to reject unfair offers for sure. Rejecting them at least \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the time is enough.
    • Bob wants to accept unfair offers, so \(q\) has to stay in a range that keeps Bob happy.
    • Bob is actually indifferent here: against \(f\text{,}\) accepting and rejecting both give him \(2\text{.}\) The condition on \(q\) comes from Alice’s inequality, not Bob’s. Think about what happens to Alice’s incentive to make an unfair offer as Bob becomes more willing to accept it.
    🔗
    We’re going to skip Section 5.7 (Colonel Blotto). Next up is Section 5.8.

    Checkpoint 5.5.7.

    🔗
    So the second indifference equation is just \(\pi_2(x, 1, z) = \pi_2(x, 0, z)\text{,}\) using the two expressions above. The third one, \(\pi_3(x, y, 1) = \pi_3(x, y, 0)\text{,}\) works the same way. Once you’re comfortable with that, the rest is solving the three equations, as the book does.
