    Section 3.4 Finding Nash Equilibria Using Best Response

    So far we have found Nash equilibria two ways: by inspecting profiles one at a time, and by eliminating dominated strategies and hoping the theorems from the last page let us keep what survives. This page gives us the method we will use for the rest of the course. It is faster than inspection, it never loses an equilibrium the way weak elimination can, and once you have the hang of it you can read the answer off a matrix in about a minute.
    🔗

    Section 3.8, Finding Nash Equilibria Using Best Response.

    Read Section 3.8. It is two pages and mostly notation.
    🔗
    🔗
    Two notational points before we go on, since the book writes things out longhand and we are going to compress them.
    🔗
    First, recall \(s_{-i}\) from the first page of this chapter. It means the strategies of everybody except player \(i\text{.}\) On page 1 we only ever used it with a star, as \(s^*_{-i}\text{,}\) because we were always talking about the other players sitting at their equilibrium strategies. Here we need the plain version too: \(s_{-i}\) is just some fixed choice by everyone else, not necessarily an equilibrium one.
    🔗
    Second, the book writes player 1’s best response set as \(B_1(s_2, \ldots, s_n)\text{,}\) and for a general player \(i\) it would have to write \(B_i(s_1, \ldots, s_{i-1}, s_{i+1}, \ldots, s_n)\text{,}\) with an awkward gap where \(s_i\) would go. We will write \(B_i(s_{-i})\) instead. It means exactly the same thing, and it is the same compression we used on page 1 when we shortened the definition of a Nash equilibrium. Do not go looking for \(B_i(s_{-i})\) in the textbook; that notation is ours, not theirs.
    🔗
    Now the definition itself. The key thing to notice is that a best response is not defined for a player on their own. It is only defined once you have fixed what everyone else is doing. Given that fixed choice \(s_{-i}\text{,}\) player \(i\)’s best responses are the strategies that give player \(i\) the highest payoff available against it. There may be more than one, since two strategies can tie for the highest payoff. That is why \(B_i(s_{-i})\) is a set, and why the book calls the mapping a best response correspondence rather than a function.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.1.

    In words, what is a best response for player \(i\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • Hold the other players’ strategies fixed. Player \(i\)’s best responses to that are the strategies giving player \(i\) the highest payoff available against it, and there may be more than one if some of them tie.
    • Correct. Both halves matter: a best response only makes sense relative to a fixed \(s_{-i}\text{,}\) and there can be several of them at once.
    • Hold the other players’ strategies fixed. Player \(i\)’s best response is the strategy giving player \(i\) the highest payoff against it.
    • Almost, but the word “the” is doing damage. Nothing stops two strategies from tying for the highest payoff, in which case both are best responses. You will see exactly this happen in the next section, and it is the reason \(B_i(s_{-i})\) is a set.
    • The strategy that gives player \(i\) the highest payoff no matter what the other players do.
    • That is a dominant strategy, which is a much stronger condition and usually does not exist. A best response is always a response to something; change what the other players are doing and player \(i\)’s best response can change with it.
    🔗
    The reason we care is the sentence at the end of Section 3.8, which is worth stating on its own because it is what every method on this page rests on. A strategy profile \(s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\) is a Nash equilibrium if and only if every player’s own piece of it is a best response to what everyone else is doing:
    🔗
    \(s_i^* \in B_i(s^*_{-i})\) for every player \(i = 1, \ldots, n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This is not a new fact so much as the definition of a Nash equilibrium read in a different order. Saying that player \(i\) cannot improve by deviating from \(s_i^*\) is the same as saying \(s_i^*\) is already as good as anything else available against \(s^*_{-i}\text{,}\) which is what being a best response means. What is new is that it turns finding equilibria into a checklist: work out each player’s best responses separately, then look for the profiles where every player’s condition holds at once.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.2.

    In words, how does best response connect to Nash equilibrium?
    🔗
    • \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium exactly when every player’s strategy \(s_i^*\) is a best response to the other players’ strategies \(s^*_{-i}\text{.}\)
    • Correct, and note that it has to hold for every player at the same time. One player being happy is not enough.
    • \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium exactly when every player’s strategy \(s_i^*\) is a best response to \(s_{-i}\) for every possible choice \(s_{-i}\) by the other players.
    • This is the dominant strategy condition again, and it is much stronger than what a Nash equilibrium requires. The Nash condition only asks about one particular \(s_{-i}\text{,}\) namely \(s^*_{-i}\text{.}\) This is the same distinction the checkpoint on page 1 was drawing, now in best response language.
    • \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium exactly when at least one player’s strategy \(s_i^*\) is a best response to \(s^*_{-i}\text{.}\)
    • Not enough. If only some players are best responding, the ones who are not can improve their payoff by switching, and that is exactly what a Nash equilibrium forbids. Every player’s condition has to hold simultaneously.
    🔗

    Section 3.9, Big Monkey and Little Monkey 5.

    Read Section 3.9.
    🔗
    This is the same matrix you have now seen in Chapter 2 and on the last page, so you can spend your attention on the boxes rather than the game. Pay attention to how the boxes are drawn, because your homework will ask you to produce them and will explicitly ask you not to write out your reasoning in words.
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is the matrix once more, unboxed, so you can work along.
    🔗
    Let’s do one of Little Monkey’s best responses carefully, because it is the interesting one. Suppose Big Monkey plays \(c\text{.}\) To find Little Monkey’s best responses to that, we hold \(c\) fixed and look only at the \(c\) row. Her payoffs are the second entries, so reading across we get \(4, 3, 4, 3\text{.}\) The highest is \(4\text{,}\) and it occurs twice, under \(ww\) and under \(cw\text{.}\) So both of those are best responses, and we box her \(4\) in each of those two cells.
    🔗
    That tie is not a quirk of this example, it is the whole reason for the word “correspondence.” Little Monkey’s best response to \(c\) is a set with two things in it, so there is no way to write it as a function that returns one strategy. The book points out that Big Monkey’s best response correspondence, by contrast, happens to be a function here, since he never ties.
    🔗
    Big Monkey’s direction works the same way with the roles of rows and columns swapped. His strategies are the rows, so to find his best response you fix one of Little Monkey’s columns and read down it, comparing his payoffs, which are the first entries. Against \(ww\) he compares \(0\) and \(4\text{,}\) so he climbs, and we box the \(4\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.4.

    To find Big Monkey’s best response to Little Monkey’s strategy \(cw\text{,}\) what do you do, and what do you box?
    🔗
    • Look down the \(cw\) column, compare Big Monkey’s payoffs \(9\) and \(4\text{,}\) and box the \(9\) in the \(w\) row.
    • Correct. Fixing Little Monkey’s strategy means fixing a column, and Big Monkey’s payoffs are the first entries. He waits, so the \(9\) gets boxed.
    • Look across the \(w\) row, compare Big Monkey’s payoffs \(0, 0, 9, 9\text{,}\) and box the two \(9\)s.
    • This compares Big Monkey’s payoff against four different things Little Monkey might do, which is not a best response question at all. A best response requires the other player’s strategy to be held fixed, and here that means staying inside a single column. Reading across a row is how you find Little Monkey’s best responses.
    • Look down the \(cw\) column, compare the payoffs \(1\) and \(4\text{,}\) and box the \(4\text{.}\)
    • Right column, wrong entries. Those are Little Monkey’s payoffs, the second numbers in each pair. Big Monkey’s are the first numbers, so the comparison is \(9\) against \(4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Once every box is drawn, the payoff is immediate. A cell is a Nash equilibrium exactly when every entry in it is boxed, because that is what our condition \(s_i^* \in B_i(s^*_{-i})\) for all \(i\) looks like on a matrix. A cell where only some entries are boxed means at least one player is not best responding, and that player would rather switch.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.5.

    Draw all the boxes for the matrix above, then use them. Which of these cells is a Nash equilibrium?
    🔗
    • \((c, ww)\text{,}\) with payoffs \((4, 4)\text{.}\)
    • Correct. In the \(ww\) column Big Monkey’s \(4\) beats his \(0\text{,}\) so it is boxed. In the \(c\) row Little Monkey’s \(4\) ties for her highest, so it is boxed too. Both entries boxed, so it is a Nash equilibrium, and it is the threat equilibrium from the last page.
    • \((c, wc)\text{,}\) with payoffs \((5, 3)\text{.}\)
    • Big Monkey’s \(5\) is indeed boxed, and it is the largest payoff he can get anywhere in this matrix. But look at Little Monkey. In the \(c\) row her payoffs are \(4, 3, 4, 3\text{,}\) so her \(3\) here is not boxed and she would rather switch to \(ww\) or \(cw\text{.}\) A big payoff for one player is not what makes a cell an equilibrium; every entry has to be boxed.
    • \((c, cw)\text{,}\) with payoffs \((4, 4)\text{.}\)
    • Little Monkey’s \(4\) is boxed here, since it ties for her highest in the \(c\) row. But in the \(cw\) column Big Monkey’s \(4\) loses to the \(9\) above it, so his entry is not boxed and he would rather wait. Compare this with \((c, ww)\text{,}\) which has the same payoffs \((4, 4)\) but is an equilibrium. The payoffs alone cannot tell you; the boxes can.
    🔗

    Section 3.10, Water Pollution 2.

    Read Section 3.10.
    🔗
    This is the same technique with three players instead of two, and there is nothing new to learn beyond the bookkeeping. It is worth reading closely anyway, because this is the exact format your homework will use.
    🔗
    🔗
    One thing about the three player case is worth saying out loud, because it is the only part of the method that is genuinely different and it is easy to get wrong. With three players the game is drawn as two matrices, one for each of player 3’s strategies. Players 1 and 2 work exactly as before, inside whichever matrix you are looking at: player 1 reads down columns, player 2 reads across rows, both staying in a single table.
    🔗
    Player 3 is the exception. Fixing the strategies of players 1 and 2 means fixing a cell position, and player 3’s two options are the two tables. So you compare the third entry of the same cell in the top table against the third entry of that cell in the bottom table, and box the larger. That comparison runs between the tables rather than inside one of them. As always, a profile is a Nash equilibrium when all three entries in its cell are boxed.
    🔗

    Section 3.11, Cournot Duopoly.

    Read Section 3.11. Read it for the idea rather than the algebra; you will not be asked to reproduce this computation.
    🔗
    🔗
    This section closes a loop we opened at the end of page 2. When we did the Tobacco Market we found ourselves taking partial derivatives and setting them to zero, and it was not entirely clear what we were computing. Now it is: we were finding best responses. In a game with finitely many strategies you find a best response by comparing entries in a column, and in a game where strategies are real numbers you find it with calculus, but it is the same question either way.
    🔗
    The picture is the thing to take away. Each firm’s best response is a function of the other firm’s quantity, so each one graphs as a curve, and a Nash equilibrium is a point where both players are simultaneously best responding, which is a point where the curves cross. That is the same condition \(s_i^* \in B_i(s^*_{-i})\) for all \(i\) that we have been checking with boxes all page, drawn in the plane instead of in a matrix. Boxes work for finite games; the definition itself works for everything.
