    Section 3.4 Finding Nash Equilibria Using Best Response

    We start with a reading to explore another way to find Nash equilibria.
    🔗

    Section 3.8, Finding Nash Equilibria Using Best Response.

    Read Section 3.8.
    🔗
    An equivalent way to think of a strategy profile that is a Nash equilibrium is that each player’s strategy is a best response to the rest of the strategy profile.
    🔗
    🔗
    Before moving on, let’s make sure we understand both the conceptual meaning of this and the notation!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.1.

    In words, what is a best response for player \(i\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • Hold the other players’ strategies fixed. Player \(i\)’s best response is the strategy giving player \(i\) the highest payoff against it.
    • Almost, but the word “the” is misleading. Nothing stops two strategies from tying for the highest payoff, in which case both are best responses. You will see exactly this happen in the next section, and it is the reason \(B_i(s_{-i})\) is a set.
    • Hold the other players’ strategies fixed. Player \(i\)’s best responses to that are the strategies giving player \(i\) the highest payoff available against it, and there may be more than one if some of them tie.
    • Correct. Both halves matter: a best response only makes sense relative to a fixed \(s_{-i}\text{,}\) and there can be several of them at once.
    • The strategy that gives player \(i\) the highest payoff no matter what the other players do.
    • That is a dominant strategy, which is a much stronger condition and usually does not exist. A best response is always a response to something; change what the other players are doing and player \(i\)’s best response can change with it.
    🔗
    It is also worth reviewing the notation \(s_{-i}\) from earlier in this chapter! It means the strategies of everybody except player \(i\text{.}\) So when we examine a Nash equilibrium \((s_1^*, s_2^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\text{,}\) we have \(s^*_{-i} = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_{i-1}^*, s_{i+1}^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Furthermore, we can use a more concise notation for best response! For example, \(B_1(s_{-1})\) denotes the set of player 1’s best responses when the other players’ strategies are fixed at some specific profile \(s_{-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    With this notation, we can write the book’s formulation of Nash equilibrium in terms of best response even more concisely! That is, \(s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\) is a Nash equilibrium if and only if
    \begin{equation*} s_i^* \in B_i(s^*_{-i}) \text{ for every player } i = 1, \ldots, n. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.2.

    In words, how does best response connect to Nash equilibrium?
    🔗
    • \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium exactly when every player’s strategy \(s_i^*\) is a best response to \(s_{-i}\) for every possible choice \(s_{-i}\) by the other players.
    • This is the dominant strategy condition again, and it is much stronger than what a Nash equilibrium requires. The Nash condition only asks about one particular \(s_{-i}\text{,}\) namely \(s^*_{-i}\text{.}\) This is the same distinction the checkpoint on page 1 was drawing, now in best response language.
    • \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium exactly when at least one player’s strategy \(s_i^*\) is a best response to \(s^*_{-i}\text{.}\)
    • Not enough. If only some players are best responding, the ones who are not can improve their payoff by switching, and that is exactly what a Nash equilibrium forbids. Every player’s condition has to hold simultaneously.
    • \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium exactly when every player’s strategy \(s_i^*\) is a best response to the other players’ strategies \(s^*_{-i}\text{.}\)
    • Correct, and note that it has to hold for every player at the same time. One player being happy is not enough.
    🔗
    Now for a quick example from the book demonstrating this.
    🔗

    Section 3.9, Big Monkey and Little Monkey 5.

    Read Section 3.9.
    🔗
    This is the same matrix you have now seen in Chapter 2 and on the last page, so you can spend your attention on the boxes rather than the game. Pay attention to how the boxes are drawn, because your homework will ask you to analyze games in this way.
    🔗
    🔗
    For reference, here is the matrix once more (unboxed).
    🔗
    The main thing to be careful about is the bookkeeping: whether you read across a row or down a column, and which entry of each payoff pair you compare. It helps to first ask yourself whose best response you are finding. You move through that player’s options while the other player’s strategy stays fixed, and you compare only that player’s payoffs.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.4.

    To find Big Monkey’s best response to Little Monkey’s strategy \(cw\text{,}\) what do you do, and what do you box?
    🔗
    • Look down the \(cw\) column, compare Big Monkey’s payoffs \(9\) and \(4\text{,}\) and box the \(9\) in the \(w\) row.
    • Correct. Fixing Little Monkey’s strategy means fixing a column, and Big Monkey’s payoffs are the first entries. He waits, so the \(9\) gets boxed.
    • Look across the \(w\) row, compare Big Monkey’s payoffs \(0, 0, 9, 9\text{,}\) and box the two \(9\)s.
    • This compares Big Monkey’s payoff against four different things Little Monkey might do, which is not a best response question at all. A best response requires the other player’s strategy to be held fixed, and here that means staying inside a single column. Reading across a row is how you find Little Monkey’s best responses.
    • Look down the \(cw\) column, compare the payoffs \(1\) and \(4\text{,}\) and box the \(4\text{.}\)
    • Right column, wrong entries. Those are Little Monkey’s payoffs, the second numbers in each pair. Big Monkey’s are the first numbers, so the comparison is \(9\) against \(4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now let’s check that you understand how the “best response boxes” relate to Nash equilibria.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.5.

    Once all the best response boxes are drawn in a matrix, how can you tell which cells are Nash equilibria?
    🔗
    • A cell is a Nash equilibrium when at least one payoff in it is boxed.
    • Not enough. An unboxed payoff means that player is not best responding, so they would rather switch. For example, in \((c, wc)\) Big Monkey’s \(5\) is boxed, but Little Monkey’s \(3\) is not, and she would rather switch to \(ww\) or \(cw\text{.}\)
    • A cell is a Nash equilibrium exactly when every payoff in it is boxed.
    • Correct. A boxed payoff means that player is best responding to what the others are doing in that cell, so a fully boxed cell is one where every player is best responding at once.
    • A cell is a Nash equilibrium when its boxed payoffs are the largest ones in the matrix.
    • The size of the payoffs is not what matters. For example, \((c, ww)\) and \((c, cw)\) both have payoffs \((4, 4)\text{,}\) but only \((c, ww)\) is a Nash equilibrium: in the \(cw\) column, Big Monkey’s \(4\) loses to the \(9\) above it, so it is not boxed. The payoffs alone cannot tell you; the boxes can.
    🔗
    This works because a boxed payoff means that player is best responding to the other player’s strategy. So a cell with every payoff boxed is one where every player is best responding at the same time, which is exactly our condition \(s_i^* \in B_i(s^*_{-i})\) for every player \(i\text{,}\) drawn on a matrix.
