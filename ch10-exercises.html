    1. Deriving the Replicator Equation.

    Consider the following symmetric game:
    🔗

    (a)

    Let \(\sigma = p_1 a + p_2 b\) denote the population state. Derive the replicator system for this game (a system of two differential equations \(\dot{p}_1 = g(p_1, p_2)\) and \(\dot{p}_2 = h(p_1, p_2)\)).
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Let \(p_1 = p\text{,}\) so that \(p_2 = 1 - p\text{.}\) Reduce the system from part (a) to a single differential equation \(\dot{p} = f(p)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    You should expect \(f(p)\) to have both \(p\) and \(1 - p\) as factors.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\dot{p} = p(1 - p)(4 - 5p)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find the equilibria of \(\dot{p} = f(p)\) in \([0, 1]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Without using your formula for \(f(p)\text{,}\) explain why we already know that \(p = 0\) and \(p = 1\) must be equilibria. Which theorem tells us this? Then explain which theorem tells us that the interior equilibrium (the one between 0 and 1) is where both strategies earn the same payoff against the population state at that equilibrium, and why.
    🔗

    2. Phase Line and Stability.

    This is a continuation of Exercise 10.4.1, with the same game.
    🔗

    (a)

    Using your results from Exercise 10.4.1, sketch a graph of \(f(p)\) and use it to sketch the phase line on \([0, 1]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\underset{0}{\bullet} \longrightarrow\!\!\longrightarrow \underset{4/5}{\bullet} \longleftarrow\!\!\longleftarrow \underset{1}{\bullet}\text{,}\) so \(p = \frac{4}{5}\) is stable and \(p = 0\) and \(p = 1\) are unstable.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Summarize in words the behavior of a typical solution that does not begin at an equilibrium. What happens to a solution that begins with \(p\) near 0? What about one that begins with \(p\) near 1?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Analyze the game using Theorem 8.5: which case is this game in, and what does that case tell you? Then explain the connection between the results of that analysis and part (b).
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Suppose the population starts exactly at the interior equilibrium. What happens? What if it starts just below it?
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark: In Microsoft vs. Apple, the mixed equilibrium was unstable, but here it is stable. You can see why from the game itself. When almost everyone plays \(a\text{,}\) a typical opponent plays \(a\text{,}\) so \(b\) earns 1 while \(a\) earns 0, and \(b\) spreads. When almost everyone plays \(b\text{,}\) \(a\) earns 4 while \(b\) earns 0, and \(a\) spreads. Either way, the population is pushed toward a mix of the two strategies.
    🔗

    3. A Game Outside the Four Cases.

    This is the Stag Hunt with Easily Spooked Hares from Section 8.6.3 of the book, which you analyzed in Exercise 8.3.3 (there, with \(a\) = stag and \(b\) = hare). Two hunters together can kill a stag, worth 4 to each of them. A hunter alone can’t kill a stag, but can catch four hares, each worth 1. If both hunters go after the hares, the hares run away and each hunter catches only one. The strategies are hunt the stag (\(s\)) or hunt the hares (\(h\)):
    🔗

    (a)

    Derive the replicator system for this game and reduce it to a single differential equation \(\dot{p} = f(p)\text{,}\) where \(p\) is the share using \(s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find the equilibria of \(\dot{p} = f(p)\) in \([0, 1]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Sketch a graph of \(f(p)\) and use it to draw the phase line.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\underset{0}{\bullet} \longleftarrow\!\!\longleftarrow\!\!\longleftarrow\!\!\longleftarrow \underset{1}{\bullet}\text{,}\) so \(p = 0\) is stable and \(p = 1\) is unstable.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Give a brief interpretation of your results from part (c).
    🔗
    Solution.
    Unless everyone starts with \(s\text{,}\) \(s\) dies out and the population approaches all-\(h\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    In Exercise 8.3.3, you found that \((s, s)\) and \((h, h)\) are Nash equilibria. Which theorem from this chapter, combined with your answer to part (b), tells us that there are no other symmetric Nash equilibria? Explain.
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    In Exercise 8.3.3, you found the evolutionarily stable states of this game. Compare them with the stability of the equilibria from part (c). Then explain why this game does not fit any of the four cases in Table 10.2.2.
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

      🔗
      🔗
    • Theorem 10.3 needs \(\pi_1(h, \sigma) \gt \pi_1(\sigma, \sigma)\text{.}\) Does that hold here? Does Theorem 10.3 apply?
      🔗
      🔗
    • From our analysis earlier, the all-\(s\) state is unstable. Why doesn’t this contradict Theorem 10.3?

    4. Using the Theorems with Three Strategies.

    In an earlier homework, we considered a Prisoner’s Dilemma repeated for two stages, with three strategies:
    • Always cooperate (\(F\))
      🔗
      🔗
    • Always defect (\(S\))
      🔗
      🔗
    • Tit-for-tat (\(P\)): choose \(C\) in stage 1. In stage 2, copy the other player’s move from stage 1.
      🔗
      🔗
    This led to the following game:
    🔗
    With three strategies, the replicator system has two variables, so we won’t analyze it directly. Instead, answer each part using Theorems 10.1 through 10.4.
    🔗

    (a)

    Use Theorem 10.1 to name three equilibria of the replicator system without any computation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The pure states: all-\(F\text{,}\) all-\(S\text{,}\) and all-\(P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Consider the all-\(F\) state, where everyone always cooperates.
    • Use best response (box each player’s best responses in the payoff matrix) to show that \((F, F)\) is not a Nash equilibrium.
      🔗
      🔗
    • By Theorem 10.1, the all-\(F\) state is an equilibrium of the replicator system. Why doesn’t this contradict Theorem 10.2?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Again consider the all-\(F\) state, where \(\sigma = F\text{.}\)
    • A quick computation shows that \(\pi_1(S, \sigma) = 10\) and \(\pi_1(\sigma, \sigma) = 6\text{.}\) What does Theorem 10.3 tell you about the behavior over time of solutions that contain some \(S\)-players?
      🔗
      🔗
    • What happens if the population starts at all-\(F\) with no \(S\)-players at all? Which part of Theorem 10.1 tells you this?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Notice that \((S, S)\) is a strict Nash equilibrium. Use one theorem from Chapter 8 and another from Chapter 10 to conclude that the all-\(S\) state is an asymptotically stable equilibrium of the replicator system.
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Notice that the all-\(P\) state is not evolutionarily stable. A quick check shows that \((P, P)\) is a Nash equilibrium. However, it fails condition (2) of Theorem 8.1: taking \(\tau = F\text{,}\) we have \(\pi_1(F, P) = \pi_1(P, P) = 6\text{,}\) but \(\pi_1(P, F) = \pi_1(F, F) = 6\text{,}\) so \(\pi_1(P, F)\) is not greater than \(\pi_1(F, F)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A classmate says, “Since the all-\(P\) state isn’t evolutionarily stable, Theorem 10.4 shows that it is not asymptotically stable.” Explain the error.
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark: The classmate’s conclusion happens to be true, even though the reasoning is not. Section 10.7 of the book analyzes this game with slightly different payoffs, and shows that populations near the all-\(P\) state stay near it, but don’t return exactly to all-\(P\text{.}\)
