    1. Two Types of Interaction.

    (a) Player 1’s strategy \(DU\).

    Describe Player 1’s strategy \(DU\) in words.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 1 moves down if the type is \(A\) and up if the type is \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Player 2’s strategy \(LR\).

    Describe Player 2’s strategy \(LR\) in words.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 2 moves left if Player 1 moved up and right if Player 1 moved down.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Payoffs for \((DU, LR)\).

    What are the payoffs for \((DU, LR)\) when the interaction is of type \(A\text{?}\) Of type \(B\text{?}\) What is the expected payoff to each player?
    🔗
    Solution.
    • Type \(A\text{:}\) \((1, 1)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Type \(B\text{:}\) \((6, 4)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Expected payoffs: \(\frac{3}{4}(1, 1) + \frac{1}{4}(6, 4) = \left(\frac{9}{4}, \frac{7}{4}\right)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) The Bayesian normal form.

    Construct the Bayesian normal form of this game.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 2: \(LL\) Player 2: \(LR\) Player 2: \(RL\) Player 2: \(RR\)
    Player 1: \(UU\) \(\left(\frac{9}{2}, 4\right)\) \(\left(\frac{9}{2}, 4\right)\) \(\left(0, \frac{15}{4}\right)\) \(\left(0, \frac{15}{4}\right)\)
    Player 1: \(UD\) \((3, 3)\) \(\left(4, \frac{9}{2}\right)\) \(\left(0, \frac{15}{4}\right)\) \(\left(1, \frac{21}{4}\right)\)
    Player 1: \(DU\) \(\left(\frac{21}{4}, 1\right)\) \(\left(\frac{9}{4}, \frac{7}{4}\right)\) \(\left(\frac{15}{4}, 0\right)\) \(\left(\frac{3}{4}, \frac{3}{4}\right)\)
    Player 1: \(DD\) \(\left(\frac{15}{4}, 0\right)\) \(\left(\frac{7}{4}, \frac{9}{4}\right)\) \(\left(\frac{15}{4}, 0\right)\) \(\left(\frac{7}{4}, \frac{9}{4}\right)\)
    🔗

    (e) Bayesian Nash equilibria.

    Find all Bayesian Nash equilibria of the game.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((UU, LR)\) and \((DD, RR)\text{.}\)

    (b) The Bayesian normal form.

    Construct the Bayesian normal form of this game.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Player 2: \(B\) Player 2: \(F\)
    Player 1: \(BB\) \((0, 0)\) \((2, -2)\)
    Player 1: \(BF\) \((1, -1)\) \((0, 0)\)
    Player 1: \(FB\) \((-3, 3)\) \((0, 0)\)
    Player 1: \(FF\) \((-2, 2)\) \((-2, 2)\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Strictly dominated strategies.

    Show that \(FB\) and \(FF\) are strictly dominated, say what dominates each, and explain what the two strategies have in common.
    🔗
    Solution.
    • \(FB\) is strictly dominated by \(BB\text{:}\) \(0 \gt -3\) and \(2 \gt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(FF\) is strictly dominated by \(BF\) (\(1 \gt -2\) and \(0 \gt -2\)), and also by \(BB\) (\(0 \gt -2\) and \(2 \gt -2\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    • Both strategies fold with the high card. Folding always gives Player 1 \(-2\text{,}\) while bidding with the high card gives him \(4\) or \(2\text{,}\) so folding the high card is never a good idea.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) No Bayesian Nash equilibrium.

    Use the reduced \(2 \times 2\) game to explain why there are no Bayesian Nash equilibria, and why it is enough to look at the smaller game.
    🔗
    Solution.
    In the reduced game, the best responses cycle:
    🔗
    • \((BB, B)\text{:}\) Player 1 switches to \(BF\) (1 beats 0).
      🔗
      🔗
    • \((BF, B)\text{:}\) Player 2 switches to \(F\) (0 beats -1).
      🔗
      🔗
    • \((BF, F)\text{:}\) Player 1 switches to \(BB\) (2 beats 0).
      🔗
      🔗
    • \((BB, F)\text{:}\) Player 2 switches to \(B\) (0 beats -2).
      🔗
      🔗
    So no profile of the reduced game is a Nash equilibrium. It is enough to look at the reduced game because the eliminated strategies were strictly dominated, and by Theorem 3.2 the original game and the reduced game have exactly the same Nash equilibria.
    🔗

    4. The Travails of Boss Gorilla 2.

    (a) The Bayesian normal form.

    Find the Bayesian normal form of the game in terms of \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Visiting Gorilla: \(c\) Visiting Gorilla: \(l\)
    Boss Gorilla: \(f\) \((5 - 6p,\ 6p - 1)\) \((6, 0)\)
    Boss Gorilla: \(a\) \((3, 3)\) \((6, 0)\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Equilibria in three cases.

    Find the Bayesian Nash equilibria when \(0 \lt p \lt \frac{1}{6}\text{,}\) \(\frac{1}{6} \lt p \lt \frac{1}{3}\text{,}\) and \(\frac{1}{3} \lt p \lt 1\text{,}\) and describe each in a sentence.
    🔗
    Solution.
    • \(0 \lt p \lt \frac{1}{6}\text{:}\) the only equilibrium is \((f, l)\text{.}\) Boss Gorilla would fight if challenged, so the visitor leaves.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\frac{1}{6} \lt p \lt \frac{1}{3}\text{:}\) the only equilibrium is \((f, c)\text{.}\) The visitor challenges and Boss Gorilla fights.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\frac{1}{3} \lt p \lt 1\text{:}\) the only equilibrium is \((a, c)\text{.}\) The visitor challenges and Boss Gorilla acquiesces.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) What the visitor’s knowledge changes.

    Speculate: why does the visitor’s knowledge make this difference?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Answers will vary. Here is one possible explanation.
    🔗
    In Section 4.5, the visitor’s decision to challenge can depend on whether he would win, so being challenged tells Boss Gorilla something about who he is facing. If Boss Gorilla fights, Weak visitors stay away, so only Tough ones challenge and he should acquiesce instead; but if he acquiesces, Weak visitors challenge too, and when \(p \lt \frac{1}{3}\) he should fight. Neither choice is stable. In this version, the visitor can’t base his decision on whether he is Tough or Weak, so a challenge tells Boss Gorilla nothing new. His best response depends only on \(p\text{,}\) and an equilibrium exists for every \(p\text{.}\)
